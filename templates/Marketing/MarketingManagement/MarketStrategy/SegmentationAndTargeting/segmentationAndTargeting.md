# Identifying Market Segments and Target Customers

Targeting is a deliberate choice of which customers you will optimize your offering for and which you will ignore. Choose segments strategically, by whether you can create superior value for them (compatibility) and whether they can create value for you (attractiveness, both monetary and strategic), then find tactical, observable profile traits that let you reach exactly those customers effectively and efficiently. Segment on differences that matter for the offering, ignore differences that do not, and tailor a distinct offering to each segment you choose.

## Segmentation, targeting, positioning

1. **Segmentation**: identify distinct groups of buyers with different needs and wants.
2. **Targeting**: select one or more segments to enter.
3. **Value proposition and positioning**: establish, communicate and deliver the right benefits for each target (see the positioning skill).

Marketers increasingly combine several variables to define smaller, better-defined groups (wealthy retirees split further by income, assets, savings and risk appetite), which favors needs-based targeting.

## Levels of targeting

| Level | What it is | Fits when | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass (undifferentiated) marketing | One offer, mass distribution and communication | Preferences are similar and no natural segments exist | Fragmented media and channels make mass reach costly; vulnerable to focused rivals |
| Targeted (differentiated) marketing | Different offerings for different segments | Groups differ in needs | Complexity and cost |
| One-to-one marketing | Each segment is a single customer | You hold rich individual data and sell high-value products that can be cross-sold, replaced or upgraded | Data and systems investment can exceed payout; costs rise above willingness to pay |
| Mass customization | Individually designed products, services, programs and messages produced on a mass basis (configurators, custom-mix dispensers, personalized services) | Flexible operations and digital interfaces | Choice overload; operational complexity |

## Strategic versus tactical targeting

| | Strategic targeting | Tactical targeting |
|---|---|---|
| Question | Who are the customers with whom we can build a mutually beneficial relationship? | How do we reach them effectively and cost-efficiently? |
| Logic | Trade market size for fit: deliberately forgo some customers to serve others better | Exclude no strategically important customer; reach all of them, and only them |
| Focus | Value created for and captured from customers | Means of communication and delivery |

The two are complementary: value is unobservable, so strategy needs tactics to make it actionable, and tactics without strategy reach the wrong people.

## Strategic targeting

Start from the customer need the offering will fulfil, then answer two questions for each candidate segment.

### 1. Target compatibility: can we create superior value for them?

Compatibility is your ability to outdo competitors in meeting the segment's needs, based on resources:

- **Business infrastructure**: manufacturing, service (call centers, CRM), supply chain, management systems and culture.
- **Access to scarce resources**: unique materials, prime locations, memorable domains; they limit competitors' options.
- **Skilled employees**: technological, operational and business expertise.
- **Technological expertise**: proprietary processes, patents, trade secrets.
- **Strong brands**: meaning beyond attributes, crucial in commoditized categories.
- **Collaborator networks**: vertical (suppliers, distributors) and horizontal (R&D, manufacturing, promotion partners).
- Also an established ecosystem and capital.

A **core competency** (1) is a source of competitive advantage that contributes significantly to perceived customer benefits, (2) applies across many markets, and (3) is hard to imitate. Own and nurture core competencies; outsource the rest when others do it better or cheaper.

### 2. Target attractiveness: can they create superior value for us?

**Monetary value**

- **Revenues**: market size and growth, buying power, brand loyalty, price sensitivity, your pricing power, competitive intensity, and context (economy, regulation, environment).
- **Costs to serve**: tailoring benefits, communication and delivery, acquisition and retention, post-purchase support, incentives and loyalty programs.

**Strategic value** (nonmonetary, harder to measure, often decisive)

- **Social value**: influence on other buyers (opinion leaders, trendsetters, mavens). Some influential customers may never pay much but sway profitable segments.
- **Scale value**: volume that pays off through the business model (high-fixed-cost businesses filling capacity; early-stage firms building a user base or network effects, even at low margin).
- **Information value**: data on needs and profiles that help serve others; **lead users** and early adopters whose feedback improves the offering.

Firms fail when they will not give up market breadth. A viable strategy requires an explicit statement of whom you will **not** serve.

## Tactical targeting

### The customer profile

Link the need you serve to observable characteristics:

- **Demographic**: age, gender, income, occupation, education, religion, ethnicity, nationality, employment, urban or rural, social class, household size, life-cycle stage. For organizations, **firmographics**: size, structure, industry, growth, revenue, profitability.
- **Geographic / geolocation**: enduring (home address) and dynamic (current location from mobile devices).
- **Behavioral**: experience with the offering (current customer, competitor's customer, new to category), frequency, quantity, price and promotion sensitivity, loyalty, online versus offline buying, outlets used, role in the decision (initiator, influencer, decider, buyer, user), stage in the decision journey, how they learn about products, how they socialize and spend free time.
- **Psychographic**: values, attitudes, interests, lifestyle. These link observable and unobservable traits and are usually inferred from behavior (sports interest shown by subscriptions, viewing, club membership, equipment purchases). Digital platforms make them more visible.

### Aligning customer value with the profile

Kotler's travel-rewards credit card example: the strategic target is people who value travel rewards, will use the card often and will not default. None of that is observable, so map it to observable proxies: credit scores, demographics, location and card-spend patterns for usage and risk; travel-media consumption, luggage purchases and travel-site visits for the travel need. Then use travel-related channels to reach them.

Two principles:

- **Effectiveness**: reach all strategically viable customers, make them aware and give them access.
- **Cost efficiency**: reach only the targeted customers; do not waste resources on people you cannot serve well or who cannot create value.

### Personas

Detailed profiles of one or a few hypothetical target customers (name, photo, bio, attitudes, behaviors, everyday frustrations) that keep a concrete customer point of view in every decision. Build them from quantitative, qualitative and observational research. A single persona does not represent everyone, so use several when the target varies (one persona per segment, and possibly one for a key influencer or employee audience).

## Single-segment and multi-segment targeting

### Single-segment concentration

- Deep knowledge of one segment, strong presence, and economies from specializing production, distribution and promotion.
- Often a **niche**: a small, well-defined group seeking a distinctive mix of benefits. Kotler cites a rental-car firm that focused on insurance-replacement customers and a leisure airline flying nonstop from small cities to vacation spots, both avoiding head-on competition.
- **Attractive niche criteria**: distinct needs; willingness to pay a premium to the firm that serves them best; enough size, profit and growth potential; few likely competitors; economies through specialization. Niches that once seemed too small can become profitable as marketing efficiency rises.
- Risk: the segment can shrink or be invaded (a brand that moved into a very different segment with the same name failed).

### Multi-segment targeting

As customer bases diversify, firms move from one offering to a product line; each segment needs its own tailored offering.

- **Selective specialization**: several objectively attractive segments with little synergy; diversifies risk.
- **Product specialization**: one product type sold to several segments (instruments for university, government and commercial labs); risk of being displaced by a new technology.
- **Market specialization**: many products for one customer group (everything a university lab needs); strong reputation and a channel for additional products.
- A card-and-gift company with sub-lines for specific ages, life stages, cultures and causes illustrates disciplined multi-segment design.

Common mistake: building offerings around development capability or production capacity rather than explicit segment needs. The result is offerings that cannibalize each other in one segment, neglect others, and confuse customers who cannot tell them apart.

## Segmenting consumer markets

A **segment** is a group sharing similar needs and/or profile traits. Choose variables that relate to needs.

### Demographic

- Popular because often linked to needs and easy to measure; even non-demographic segments need demographic links for sizing and media.
- **Age and generation**: cohorts (Silent Generation, Baby Boomers, Gen X, Millennials or Gen Y, Gen Z) share formative experiences, icons and values; some products are age-specific.
- **Life stage**: a person's major concern (divorce, remarriage, caring for a parent, moving in together, buying a home) creates opportunities; not everyone passes through the same stages (single-person households are a large market).
- **Gender**: differences in attitudes and information processing have shaped categories, and products designed from real research on one gender's needs can dominate. Roles are converging in many categories (men as primary grocery shoppers, women as main decision makers on homes and cars), and gender-neutral lines are growing. Do not rely on stereotypes.
- **Income**: does not always predict the best customers. Markets are often **hourglass-shaped** as the middle trades down to value and up to premium; firms stuck in the middle lose share unless they add premium and value lines.
- **Race and culture (multicultural marketing)**: build cultural norms, language nuance, buying habits and business practices into the strategy from the start, not as an afterthought; account for generation and immigrant status; sample carefully; campaigns aimed at one group can cross over to the general market.

### Geographic

- Nations, regions, cities, neighborhoods, down to postal codes; operate in a few areas or adapt locally everywhere. Regional consumption differs markedly.
- Mapping software shows where customers cluster around a store.
- **Geoclustering** combines geography and demographics into lifestyle clusters to find where the most valuable customers live, how deeply each cluster is penetrated, which channels and media reach it, and where to find look-alike prospects ("customer cloning").

### Behavioral

- **User status**: nonusers, potential users (including those entering via a life event), first-time, regular and ex-users each need a different strategy. To win nonusers, learn whether the barrier is a deep attitude or just lack of knowledge.
- **Usage rate**: heavy users are a small share of people but a large share of volume. They are either very loyal or always price shopping, and may have little room to grow; light users may respond more to new appeals.
- **Buyer-readiness stage**: unaware, aware, informed, interested, desiring, intending. The mix of stages shapes the program (first awareness with a simple message, then benefits and risks of inaction, then an incentive to act).
- **Loyalty status**: hard-core loyal, split loyal (two or three brands), shifting loyal, switchers. Retain the loyal and grow their usage; grow share of purchases among the less loyal.
- **Occasions**: when the need arises, when people buy and when they use (business versus leisure travel; flowers as gift or decoration; wine for drinking or cooking). Different occasions mean different needs and value.

### Psychographic

- Traits, lifestyle and values reveal needs that demographics miss: some older buyers are psychologically young, so a product aimed at 21-year-olds can end up bought mostly by people twice that age.
- **VALS** classifies adults by primary motivation (ideals, achievement, self-expression) and resources (energy, confidence, intellectualism, novelty seeking, innovativeness, impulsiveness, leadership, vanity plus demographics). Rich, but somewhat removed from actual behavior.
- Identity-based segments such as LGBTQ+ consumers respond to brands that are genuinely supportive; authenticity and consistency matter, and some brands accept the risk of backlash.

## Segmenting business markets

| Variable group | Key questions |
|---|---|
| Demographic | Which industries? What company size? Which locations? |
| Operating variables | Which customer technologies? Heavy, medium, light users or nonusers? Customers needing many or few services? |
| Purchasing approaches | Centralized or decentralized purchasing? Engineering- or finance-dominated power structure? Existing strong relationships or the most desirable companies? Preference for leasing, service contracts, systems purchases, sealed bids? Purchasing criteria: quality, service or price? |
| Situational factors | Urgent delivery or service? A specific application or all? Large or small orders? |
| Personal characteristics | Buyer-seller similarity in people and values? Risk-taking or risk-avoiding? Loyal to suppliers? |

Segment sequentially (industry, then size, then purchase criteria). Kotler cites a bearings maker that found some big customers had little profit potential, shifted toward more attractive industries, and kept selling its premium product only for the applications where customers valued it, letting price-sensitive applications go elsewhere.

## The long tail

Online commerce lowers distribution and search costs, so many niche products together can rival hits (closer to 50-50 than 80-20 in some categories). Premises: cheaper distribution reduces the need for precise demand prediction; more variety taps latent niche demand; aggregated niches form a large market. Large firms can carry more low-volume variety; small firms can enter with niche offers. Limits: categories with complex production or high inventory costs remain hit-driven; poor recommendation systems bury tail items; physical stocking costs can outweigh tail revenue; hits often win on quality.

## Template: segment evaluation scorecard

| Segment (need-based name) | Core need and occasion | Profile (observable traits) | Size and growth | Monetary value (revenue minus cost to serve) | Strategic value (social, scale, information) | Compatibility (our relevant resources vs best competitor) | Competitive intensity | Reachability (channels, media) | Decision: target / later / ignore |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | | | | | | |

Score attractiveness and compatibility separately (for example 1 to 5 each), plot segments on a two-by-two, and target only those high on both. Record the reasons for every 'ignore' decision.

## Worked example: an HR-software startup choosing its first target

A startup has built onboarding software. Founders want to sell to "any company that hires".

- **Candidate segments (need-based)**: (A) multi-site hospitality and retail chains onboarding many hourly staff quickly; (B) tech scale-ups onboarding salaried engineers with complex equipment and access needs; (C) staffing agencies onboarding contractors for clients; (D) enterprises with existing HR suites.
- **Compatibility**: the product's mobile-first, multilingual flows and e-signature for shift workers are a real advantage for A and C; it lacks the IT provisioning integrations B needs; D requires deep suite integrations and a long security process the team cannot support.
- **Attractiveness**: A has high volume and high turnover (recurring need), moderate price sensitivity and a manageable buying center (operations director plus HR); C has thin margins and high churn; B is attractive but crowded; D has big contracts but huge cost to serve.
- **Strategic value**: two well-known restaurant groups in A would act as social proof for the rest of the segment (social value); their franchisees provide feedback on edge cases (information value).
- **Decision**: target A (multi-site hourly-workforce employers, 20 to 300 locations); revisit C in a year; explicitly ignore D and do not build features for B.
- **Tactical targeting**: firmographics (industry codes, location counts, job-posting volume for hourly roles); behavioral signals (franchise systems, seasonal hiring spikes); reach through hospitality trade associations, franchise expos and operations-director communities (effective), avoiding broad HR-tech ads that mostly reach B and D (cost efficient).
- **Personas**: "Marta, operations director at a 60-location fast-casual chain" and "Dev, general manager who onboards five people a week on a tablet".

## Workflow

1. State the need the offering fulfils and list candidate segments defined by need or occasion, not just demographics.
2. For each segment, assess compatibility (resources, core competencies versus the best competitor) and attractiveness (monetary and strategic value) with the scorecard.
3. Choose the targeting level (mass, differentiated, one-to-one, mass customization) and the pattern (single segment, selective, product or market specialization).
4. Write down which segments you will ignore and why.
5. Build the customer profile that maps each target's unobservable value to observable demographic or firmographic, geographic, behavioral and psychographic traits.
6. Choose channels and media that satisfy effectiveness (reach all targets) and cost efficiency (reach only targets).
7. Create one persona per target segment from research, and check every offering in the line maps to a distinct segment need.
8. Hand the chosen segments to positioning to design the value proposition for each.

## Red flags

- "Our target market is everyone" or a target defined only by age and gender.
- No list of customers the company has chosen not to serve.
- Segments chosen because they are big, not because the company can win there.
- Ignoring cost to serve, so large accounts look attractive but destroy margin.
- Overlooking strategic value: dropping influential or lead-user customers because they spend little.
- Personas invented in a meeting rather than drawn from research, or a single persona standing in for a varied market.
- Several products in the line aimed at the same segment while other segments go unserved; customers cannot tell the variants apart.
- Ad targeting that reaches far beyond the strategic target (waste) or misses part of it (lost demand).
- Mass customization or one-to-one programs whose data and system costs exceed what customers will pay.
- Multicultural or identity-based marketing added as a translation or a seasonal campaign rather than built into strategy, or used without genuine commitment.
- Micro-targeting on sensitive traits (health, religion, sexual orientation, financial distress) or exploiting vulnerable groups; keep targeting lawful, consented and fair.
