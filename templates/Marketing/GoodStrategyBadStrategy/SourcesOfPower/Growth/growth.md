# Growth

Growth is not a strategy, and growth itself does not create value. Healthy growth is the **outcome** of superior products, capabilities, efficiency and innovation; engineered growth (by acquisition or by chasing a booming market without an advantage) usually destroys value. Before endorsing a growth plan, ask what advantage the growth rests on and whether it survives once the market slows.

## The growth trap

Rumelt's account of a can maker after its founder's era:

- The new leadership 'had to light a fire' because revenue growth was slow, though shareholder returns had been far above the market.
- It made a long series of acquisitions to become the world's largest container maker, much of it in plastic bottles because that segment was booming, and hired deal specialists to do it.
- It assumed the strength being leveraged was cost control. In fact its historic competence was **flexibility and short runs**, a focus that lowered customers' bargaining power. Plastics had lower changeover costs, eroding that basis, and the new strategy left the advantage behind without replacing it.
- Results: the plastic boom ended, prices fell instead of firming, industry overcapacity persisted, return on capital collapsed and the share price fell by about 90%. Rapid growth produced shareholder returns far below the index for nearly two decades.
- All of this (slowing growth, overcapacity, price spillover from substitutes) was predictable with **basic industry analysis** such as Porter's Five Forces.

## Why growth disappoints

- **Focus misread**: treating focus as 'we only make metal and plastic packaging' (a product-line restriction) instead of the coordination of action and resources that creates advantage.
- **Growth by substitution has a ceiling**: when growth comes from a new material or technology replacing an old one rather than from rising underlying demand, it stops abruptly once conversion is complete, and can even reverse.
- **Commodity growth is an industry phenomenon**: rising demand lifts profits, everyone invests in capacity, profits are ploughed back into plant, and when growth slows, firms without an advantage see profits vanish. The growing commodity industry is a 'black hole' that absorbs more cash than it returns to ordinary competitors.
- **Growth rhetoric is magical invocation**: 'our growth slowed, so we will grow bigger to have a platform for more growth' just repeats the word for the object of desire.

## Acquisitions

- Buying a company, especially a public one, usually means **paying too much**: a premium (often about a quarter above market value) plus fees.
- An acquisition creates value only if you **buy for less than the company is worth**, or you are **specially positioned to add more value** to it than anyone else.
- Common poor reasons for deals:
  - belief that administrative costs fall with size;
  - parking executives on the periphery instead of letting them go;
  - bigger firms pay their leaders more;
  - deals are more fun than reviewing divisional performance;
  - advisers (bankers, consultants, lawyers, finders) earn large fees.
- Test of a rationale: if the claimed benefit could be obtained with a **contract** (moving traffic over a partner's network, a distribution deal, a licence), a merger is not needed for it.
- 'Economies of mass' (combined cash flow) is arithmetic, not a reason. Rumelt recounts a banker whose ultimate argument for a giant merger was that more cash flow enables an even bigger deal.

## Healthy growth

- Comes from **growing demand for your special capabilities**, or from **expanded or extended capabilities**.
- Is the reward for superior products and skills, successful innovation, cleverness, efficiency and creativity.
- Is not merely an industry phenomenon; it normally shows up as **a gain in market share together with a superior rate of profit**.

## Marketing and GTM translations

- Entering a hot adjacent category (AI features, a new channel, a trending format) because it is growing is the commodity-growth trap unless you have an advantage there that will persist when growth slows.
- Revenue growth bought with discounts, paid acquisition at rising CAC, or channel stuffing is engineered growth; check unit economics and payback, not just top line.
- 'Expand TAM' slides are often growth for its own sake. Ask what capability makes you win in the new segment.
- Before abandoning a niche because it grows slowly, check its returns; a slow-growing, high-return focus can beat a fast-growing, low-return expansion.

## Worked example: a meal-kit brand's expansion plan

Plan: 'Grow revenue 3x in 2 years by entering frozen ready-meals (a fast-growing segment) and acquiring a regional frozen producer.'

- Advantage today: weekly recipe innovation and a loyal cooking-enthusiast base; margins come from premium pricing to that base.
- Frozen segment growth is partly substitution from chilled; many entrants are adding capacity; frozen competes on price and grocery shelf access, where the brand has no advantage.
- The acquisition premium plus integration cost exceeds any plausible synergy; co-packing contracts could test frozen demand without owning plants.
- Verdict: engineered growth that abandons the existing advantage. Alternative: grow within the enthusiast base (premium add-ons, cooking classes, a subscription tier), and run a co-packed frozen test sold only to existing subscribers.

## Workflow

1. Restate the growth plan and its stated rationale in one paragraph.
2. Identify the user's current source of advantage and profit, with evidence.
3. Run basic industry analysis on the target market: demand drivers, substitution, capacity additions, price trends, buyer power.
4. Ask whether the plan keeps, extends or abandons the advantage.
5. For any acquisition, test price versus worth and unique value added; look for contract alternatives; note incentive conflicts.
6. Project margin and return on capital, not only revenue.
7. Recommend healthy growth paths: extend capabilities into adjacent demand where they confer an advantage.

## Red flags

- 'Grow' is the strategy, or growth is justified as a platform for more growth.
- Revenue rises while margins, return on capital or payback periods worsen.
- Entering a market chiefly because it is growing fast.
- Booming demand driven by substitution with no discussion of the ceiling.
- Deal rationales of scale, mass, global brand or cash flow with no specific mechanism.
- Benefits that a contract could provide justified as reasons to merge.
- Advisers who profit from the deal framing the strategy.
