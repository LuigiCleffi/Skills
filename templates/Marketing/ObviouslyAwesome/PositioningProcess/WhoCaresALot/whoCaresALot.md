# Step 7. Determine Who Cares a Lot

Not every customer cares equally about your unique value. For each value theme, ask who cares a lot and why, and describe them by easily identifiable characteristics that explain that intensity, not by demographics alone. Then target as narrowly as your near-term sales goals allow: best-fit customers buy faster, pay more, refer others and stay. You can broaden later.

## Actionable segmentation

- Segmentation is widely misunderstood. Textbook examples are consumer demographics ("single men 18 to 30"), which bleed into weak business segments ("small businesses", "100 to 2,000 employees").
- An actionable segment is a list of easily identifiable characteristics of a person or company that make them really care about what you do.
  - Consumers: other brands they own or like, stores they shop, their job, entertainment or music tastes.
  - Businesses: how they sell, other products they have invested in, skills they have or lack in-house.
- Intensity example: an invoicing tool's accounting integration only matters to customers on that accounting package, and matters most to those sending many invoices.

## Best-fit versus OK customers

| | OK customers | Best-fit customers |
|---|---|---|
| Understanding | Need convincing | Get it immediately |
| Sales cycle | Slow | Fast; they are "buying" as much as you are selling |
| Price | Ask for discounts | May say you are underpriced |
| Loyalty | Leave for cheaper or better | Fight anyone who tries to remove you |
| Advocacy | Little | Tell their friends |

- Broad targeting feels safer but makes it harder to convince anyone you are their best choice. Limited resources (every company has them) belong on prospects who look like best-fit customers, provided there are enough of them.

## Size it against near-term goals

- Do not get positioning far ahead of the business. Look at this year's sales targets and the number of deals needed.
- Can you hit them by selling only to best-fit customers? If no, broaden the definition. If yes, stay focused; it is the fastest, cheapest path to target.
- Positioning and targets evolve; you do not need a segment that fits where you will be in two, five or ten years.

## Finding who cares

- Logical shortcuts often work: heavily regulated companies care more about security; managers of very large teams feel collaboration pain; small companies without IT need easy setup.
- For each value theme, repeat "who cares, and why?" and then narrow. Dunford's scheduling example: multi-location, multi-service booking matters to businesses with many locations *and* that run on appointments; drugstores have locations but not appointments; tax and financial-service firms have both.
- Segments often narrow a category: "accounting software for freelancers", "sporting goods for dogs".

## Two tests for a segment

1. **Big enough** to meet the business's goals.
2. **Important, specific, unmet needs** common to the segment.

(A racquet for seniors needs enough tennis-playing seniors and a real senior-specific need.)

Also check it is identifiable: could you build a list of these buyers from data you can obtain (job titles, installed tools, licensing databases, communities)?

## Worked example

HVAC dispatch SaaS. Theme "win every emergency call":

- Who cares a lot: residential HVAC and plumbing contractors with 15 to 60 technicians that advertise 24/7 emergency service and run paid search on "emergency plumber" terms.
- Why: emergency calls are their highest-margin jobs, and callers dial the next contractor within minutes.
- Identifiable: "24/7" on their website and Google Business profile, active emergency-keyword ads, technician count from licensing records.
- Excluded: two-truck shops doing scheduled maintenance (low call volume), commercial facilities contractors (contracted response times, different buying process).
- Sizing: about 9,000 such contractors in target regions; 120 new customers needed this year; segment is large enough.

## Workflow

1. Take the value themes from [Step 6](../ValueThemes/valueThemes.md).
2. For each theme ask "who cares a lot, and why?", using best-customer traits from [Step 1](../BestCustomers/bestCustomers.md).
3. Express the answer as observable, list-buildable characteristics, not just industry and size.
4. Apply the two tests: big enough for this year's goals, specific important unmet need.
5. Check list-buildability: name the data source.
6. Narrow as far as the sales targets allow; broaden only if the numbers require it.
7. Pass the segment to [Step 8](../MarketFrameOfReference/marketFrameOfReference.md) to pick the frame that makes the value obvious to them.

## Red flags

- ICP written as firmographics only ("mid-market, North America, SaaS").
- A target defined by traits you cannot find or list ("companies that value innovation", "design-minded").
- "Everyone who could use it" as the target.
- No link between the segment and a specific value theme.
- A niche rejected as "too small" without comparing it to the number of deals needed this year.
- A niche chosen without checking for a real, segment-specific unmet need.
- Marketing reaching large audiences while win rate and deal velocity stay low.
