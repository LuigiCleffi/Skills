# Defining Marketing for the New Realities

Treat marketing as the whole organization's job of identifying and meeting customer needs profitably, not as a department that sells what the company happens to make. Define the business by the customer need it serves, choose target markets deliberately, and coordinate every function so the customer sees one consistent value. Use the holistic marketing lens (relationships, integration, internal alignment, measured performance) to check any plan, and diagnose weak marketing with the ten deadly sins.

## Definitions to work from

- **Marketing (short form)**: identifying and meeting human and social needs in a way that serves the organization's goals; turning a private or social need into a profitable opportunity.
- **Formal (AMA) definition**: the activities, institutions and processes for creating, communicating, delivering and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners and society.
- **Marketing management**: the art and science of choosing target markets and getting, keeping and growing customers by creating, delivering and communicating superior customer value. Note the three verbs (get, keep, grow) and the three value tasks (create, deliver, communicate).
- **Social definition**: a societal process through which people obtain what they need and want by creating, offering and freely exchanging valued products and services. Co-creation of value with customers and partners is part of modern marketing.
- **Selling is the tip of the iceberg.** Drucker's point: the aim of marketing is to make selling superfluous by understanding the customer so well that the offering fits and sells itself. When a launch is swamped with orders, it is usually homework on customers, competitors and costs, not sales skill.

## What can be marketed

Ten domains, each with its own marketing problems:

| Domain | Typical marketing task |
|---|---|
| Goods | Differentiate, distribute, build brand preference |
| Services | Make intangibles tangible, manage quality and capacity |
| Events | Drive attendance and sponsorship on a fixed date |
| Experiences | Orchestrate goods and services into a staged experience |
| Persons | Build a personal brand (professionals, artists, executives) |
| Places | Attract tourists, residents, employers, headquarters |
| Properties | Market rights of ownership (real estate, securities) |
| Organizations | Build image and compete for audiences, talent and funds |
| Information | Package and sell knowledge (research, media, data) |
| Ideas | Social marketing and causes that change behavior |

## Markets, industries and exchange

- A **marketer** seeks a response from another party: attention, a purchase, a vote, a donation. Marketers manage the level, timing and composition of demand, not just its volume.
- Economists use "market" for buyers and sellers of a product class. Marketers call the sellers the **industry** and the buyers the **market**.
- Markets can be cut by need (diet seekers), product (shoes), demographics (youth), geography (a country), or role (voters, donors, workers).
- Five basic markets linked by flows of goods, services, money and taxes: resource, manufacturer, intermediary, consumer and government markets.
- A simple marketing system has two loops: goods and services flow to the market for money, and communication flows to the market for information (attitudes, sales data). Listening is half of the system.
- There is no healthy bottom line without an effective top line: finance and operations only matter if there is demand.

## The new marketing realities

Four **market forces** produce three **market outcomes**, which call for **holistic marketing**.

### Four market forces

- **Technology**: e-commerce, mobile, data analytics, machine learning and AI enable one-to-one understanding and interaction, and are now affordable for small firms. New business models disrupt incumbents.
- **Globalization**: competitors and customers anywhere; multicultural home markets; innovations developed for one country can be sold back into others (reverse innovation).
- **Physical environment**: climate change and global health conditions (up to pandemics) can reshape demand, operations and whole business models. Plan for them.
- **Social responsibility**: marketing affects society, so the task is to satisfy target needs better than competitors while preserving or enhancing consumers' and society's long-term well-being. Responsibility can differentiate, but it involves real trade-offs (convenience versus waste).

### Three market outcomes

**New consumer capabilities**

- Research and compare prices, features and reviews anywhere, at any time; showrooming. Transparency is no longer optional.
- Search, communicate and buy on mobile, on the go.
- Share opinions and loyalty through social media and communities.
- Interact actively with brands (opt in to lists, scan codes, use apps).
- Reject marketing they find intrusive (ad blocking, skipping, unsubscribing).
- Get more value from what they own through sharing and access models.

**New company capabilities**

- Use the internet as an information and sales channel, including for individually customized goods.
- Collect richer data on markets, customers, prospects and competitors; power recommendations.
- Reach people quickly through social, mobile and location-based messages; harness word of mouth.
- Improve purchasing, recruiting, training and internal communication.
- Improve cost efficiency (online sourcing, reverse auctions, better logistics).

**New competitive environment**

- Deregulation and privatization open markets to new rivals.
- Retail transformation: stores compete with every direct channel and respond by selling experiences.
- Disintermediation and reintermediation: digital players bypass channels; incumbents become "brick-and-click".
- Private labels from powerful retailers.
- Mega-brands stretching into adjacent and converging categories.

## Holistic marketing

Everything matters, and an integrated view is usually necessary. Marketing must be less departmental, build brands more through performance than promotion, and run on good information systems. Four components:

### Relationship marketing

- Build mutually satisfying long-term relationships with four constituents: **customers, employees, marketing partners** (channels, suppliers, distributors, dealers, agencies) and the **financial community** (shareholders, investors, analysts).
- The outcome is a **marketing network**: the company plus the stakeholders with whom it has mutually profitable relationships. Build the network and profits follow; many firms own brands and outsource non-core activities.
- Tailor offers, services and messages to individual customers from transaction, demographic, psychographic and media data. Focus on the most profitable customers, products and channels, estimate **customer lifetime value**, and price and design to profit over that lifetime.
- Treat key suppliers and distributors as allies in delivering end-customer value; co-create with customers.

### Integrated marketing

- Coordinate all activities so they create, communicate and deliver consistent value and a consistent message: the whole should exceed the sum of the parts. Design each activity with all the others in mind.
- Assess each channel for its direct effect on sales and brand equity and its indirect effect on other channels.
- Integrate communications so each medium works on its own and boosts the others, with one brand message at every contact.
- Manage every touch point (store layout, packaging, product function, staff training, shipping). Marketers must think like other executives, and other executives must think like marketers.

### Internal marketing

- Hire, train and motivate able employees who want to serve customers well. Do not promise service the staff cannot deliver.
- Needs **vertical alignment** (senior management sponsors the customer agenda) and **horizontal alignment** (engineering, finance, purchasing, operations, HR and accounting all support it).
- Typical failure: marketing promises better service while catering, cleaning, hiring and pricing are run on cost alone by departments marketing does not control. Fix it by showing the bottom-line case with competitor and customer data, then engaging every employee through training, internal communication and recognition.

### Performance marketing

- Understand the financial and non-financial returns of marketing to the business and to society.
- Look beyond revenue to a **marketing scorecard**: market share, customer loss rate, satisfaction, product quality, plus legal, ethical, social and environmental effects (double or triple bottom line).
- Value intangibles (brand, customer base, employees, partner relations, intellectual capital) with metrics such as brand equity, customer lifetime value and return on marketing investment.

## Five business philosophies

| Concept | Core belief | When it fits / risk |
|---|---|---|
| Production | Buyers want widely available, cheap products; maximize efficiency and mass distribution | Fits low-cost expansion of a market; ignores what buyers value beyond price |
| Product | Buyers want the best quality, performance or features | Risk of the "better mousetrap" fallacy: a superior product still fails if badly priced, distributed, promoted and sold |
| Selling | Buyers will not buy enough unless pushed | Used for unsought goods and overcapacity; hard selling breeds returns, bad word of mouth and complaints |
| Marketing | Customer-centered sense-and-respond: make the right products for your customers, not find customers for your products | Selling serves the seller's need to convert product to cash; marketing serves the buyer's need through the whole offering |
| Market-value | Every function creates value for customers, the company and its collaborators; programs are designed with their breadth and interdependencies in mind | The most sophisticated stance; the default this agent should apply |

### Define the business by the need, not the product

Products are transient; basic needs and customer groups endure. Rewrite product definitions as market-value definitions:

| Product definition | Market-value definition |
|---|---|
| We make accounting software | We help small firms stay on top of their cash |
| We run gyms | We help busy people stay fit and energetic |
| We sell project-management licenses | We help teams ship work on time |

A need-based definition widens the competitive set (a soft-drink maker competes with water, juice, tea and coffee) and reveals growth opportunities. Avoid going so broad that the definition no longer guides choices.

## Organizing the marketing department

| Structure | Use when | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Functional (specialists report to the CMO) | Few products and markets | Turf fights and budget competition as products and markets multiply |
| Geographic (regional, zone, district managers; area market specialists) | Markets differ by region or local culture | Duplication; local plans drifting from the brand |
| Product or brand (hub and spoke around a brand manager) | Many different products or more than functional teams can handle | Responsibility without authority, short tenures, short-term thinking, focus on share over relationships |
| Category (and aisle) management | Retail trade thinks in categories; internal brand rivalry | Needs strong coordination across brands |
| Market or customer (market managers; key-account or customer managers) | Distinct user groups with different buying practices; complex buyers of integrated bundles | Same pros and cons as product management |
| Matrix (product and market managers) | Many products for many markets | Costly, conflict-prone, unclear focus and accountability |

Process-based, cross-functional teams (solid line to the team, dotted line to marketing) are an increasingly common overlay.

### Managing the department

- Marketing is too important to leave to the marketing department; ideally you cannot tell who works in marketing because everyone decides by impact on the customer. A dedicated unit still runs day-to-day marketing.
- **CEO role**: convince senior management that customer focus matters, hire strong marketing talent and a respected CMO, fund in-house marketing training, align rewards with building a satisfied, loyal customer base, and model customer commitment personally.
- **CMO role**: C-suite member reporting to the CEO, leading product development, brand, communication, research and analytics, sales, promotion, distribution, pricing and service. Needs quantitative and qualitative skills, digital fluency, entrepreneurial independence with cross-functional collaboration, the customer's voice plus bottom-line rigor. Priorities (Day and Malcolm): be the company's visionary, build adaptive marketing capabilities, win the war for talent, tighten alignment with sales, own the return on marketing spend. The most important job: inject the customer perspective into every decision affecting a touch point, increasingly with a global view.
- **Relationships with other departments**: conflicts of viewpoint are unavoidable, so the CMO works through persuasion rather than authority. Work closely with customer insight and analytics teams, communication agencies, and online and offline channel partners.

## Building a customer-oriented organization

- Invert the pyramid: customers on top, then frontline staff who serve them, then managers who support the front line, then top management who hire and support good managers. Managers at every level must personally engage with customers.
- Marketing no longer owns every customer interaction; it integrates all customer-facing processes so customers see a single face and hear a single voice.
- Customer-centric does not mean reactive: improve value, lower prices and invent before competitors force you to.

| Low customer-centricity | High customer-centricity |
|---|---|
| Product driven | Market driven |
| Mass-market focused | Customer focused |
| Process oriented | Outcome oriented |
| Reacting to competitors | Making competitors irrelevant |
| Price driven | Value driven |
| Hierarchical | Teamwork |

- Becoming market-driven requires company-wide passion for customers, organizing around customer segments rather than products, and qualitative plus quantitative customer research.
- Customer orientation is necessary but not sufficient: build capability for strategic innovation and imagination (tools, processes, skills, measures, inspiring work spaces), because rivals copy advantages quickly.
- Watch for trends; leaders miss them when risk-averse, protective of existing assets, or more interested in efficiency than innovation. Seek fresh ideas from three under-heard groups: young or diverse employees, people far from headquarters, and people new to the industry.

## The ten deadly marketing sins (diagnostic)

| # | Sin | Signs | Remedies |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Not market focused or customer driven | Poorly identified and prioritized segments; no segment owners; no customer culture or incentives | Better segmentation and prioritization; segment-specialized sales; clear values; easy contact and fast response |
| 2 | Does not fully understand target customers | Latest study is years old; sales slipping; rivals selling better; high returns and complaints | Better research and analytics, customer and dealer panels, CRM, data mining |
| 3 | Poor definition and monitoring of competitors | Watches only near rivals; misses distant competitors and disruptive technologies; no intelligence system | Competitive-intelligence function; watch technology; prepare counter-offerings |
| 4 | Poor stakeholder relationships | Unhappy employees, dealers and investors; good suppliers reluctant to partner | Move from zero-sum to positive-sum thinking with all stakeholders |
| 5 | Not good at finding new opportunities | No exciting opportunities in years; recent launches failed | A system that stimulates the flow of new ideas |
| 6 | Deficient planning process | Plan lacks the right components, no financial implications, no contingencies | Standard plan format (situation analysis, SWOT, issues, objectives, strategy, tactics, budgets, controls); ask what changes with 20 percent more or less budget |
| 7 | Loose product and service policies | Too many money-losing products; services given away; weak cross-selling | Track and fix or drop weak products; tier and price services; improve cross- and up-selling |
| 8 | Weak brand building and communication | Target market knows little; brand not distinctive; same budget mix every year; little ROI evaluation | Better brand strategy and measurement; shift spend to effective tools; require ROI estimates before funding |
| 9 | Not organized for effective marketing | Skills gap; friction between marketing, sales and other departments | Strong leader to build skills; repair cross-department relationships |
| 10 | Underuses technology | Minimal digital, outdated sales automation, no marketing automation, models or dashboards | Upgrade digital and sales automation; automate routine decisions; build decision models and dashboards |

## Worked example: a B2B SaaS company that thinks it is a feature factory

A 40-person company sells a scheduling tool to physiotherapy clinics. Leadership says "marketing should generate more leads".

- **Philosophy check**: roadmap is driven by engineering ideas (product concept) and quarter-end discounting (selling concept). Move to the market-value concept.
- **Market-value definition**: from "we sell clinic scheduling software" to "we keep independent clinics' treatment rooms full". The competitive set now includes the receptionist with a paper diary, generic calendar apps and marketplace booking platforms.
- **Holistic check**:
  - Relationship: no customer lifetime value analysis; the 20 percent of clinics with three or more locations produce most revenue and churn least.
  - Integrated: ads promise "set up in a day" while onboarding takes two weeks. Fix the delivery before the message.
  - Internal: support is measured on ticket closure time, not on clinic retention; align its incentives.
  - Performance: the scorecard tracks only leads. Add churn, net revenue retention, activation rate and CAC payback.
- **Deadly sins found**: 1 (no segment priorities), 2 (last customer interviews 18 months ago), 8 (same budget split for three years).
- **Organization**: functional team today; as the company adds a dental vertical, add market managers per vertical rather than product managers per module.

## Workflow

1. Restate the user's business in a product definition and a market-value definition; agree on the need, the customer groups and the realistic competitive set.
2. Identify which business philosophy the current plan actually reflects (production, product, selling, marketing, market-value) and name the consequences.
3. Scan the four market forces and three outcomes for opportunities and threats specific to the user's market.
4. Run the holistic check: relationship (which constituents, lifetime value), integrated (consistency across channels and touch points), internal (can staff deliver the promise, are other departments aligned), performance (scorecard beyond revenue, societal effects).
5. Run the ten deadly sins as a diagnostic and rank the findings by impact.
6. If the question is organizational, recommend a structure from the table based on the number and diversity of products and markets, and define the CMO's mandate and cross-functional links.
7. Hand off to planning: turn findings into goals and actions with the marketing planning skill.

## Red flags

- "Marketing" means only advertising, lead generation or a sales push.
- The business is defined by its current product or technology, and the competitor list contains only look-alike vendors.
- A better product is expected to sell itself without pricing, distribution and communication work.
- Revenue relies on hard selling, heavy discounting or tactics that generate returns and complaints.
- Promises in campaigns that operations, support or staff cannot keep.
- Channels and messages contradict each other; each team optimizes its own channel.
- The only metrics are short-term sales; no churn, satisfaction, lifetime value or brand measures.
- Customer research is years old; decisions are made from internal opinion.
- Brand managers with responsibility but no authority, rotating every year.
- Social or environmental claims the company cannot substantiate.
- The organization chart puts customers at the bottom and nobody senior talks to customers.
