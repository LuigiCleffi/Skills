# Commitment and Consistency

Once people take a stand, they feel personal and interpersonal pressure to behave consistently with it, and they generate new reasons to justify it. Commitments change self-image most when they are active, public, effortful and freely chosen. Use this to help customers take small, genuine steps toward something they actually want and to reinforce the identity behind it; never to trap them with extracted statements, survey pretexts or advantages you intend to withdraw.

## Why consistency is so powerful

- Bettors are more confident in their horse right after placing the bet than right before. The decision itself raises confidence.
- **Consistency is valued**: consistent people seem rational, stable, honest and strong; inconsistent people seem fickle or two-faced.
- **Automatic consistency is a shortcut**: once decided, we do not have to think about the issue again.
- **It is also a shield**: it lets people avoid unwelcome conclusions. Cialdini describes attendees at a recruiting talk who paid deposits immediately after the talk's logic was demolished, so that they would not go home and think about it.
- People will act against their interests to stay consistent. Beachgoers who had agreed to 'watch my things' chased a staged thief far more often than those who had not been asked.

## Commitment is the key

If you get someone to take a stand or go on record, you set the stage for consistency with it.

- **Asking for a prediction** ('Would you volunteer if asked?') increased later volunteering sharply; asking people whether they intended to vote raised turnout.
- **The 'How are you feeling?' opener**: after people say they are doing fine, appeals to help others less fortunate do better. A small public statement is an exploitable commitment.
- **Start small and build**: indoctrinators in a wartime prison camp moved captives from trivial statements ('my country is not perfect') to lists, signed essays and broadcasts. Each step was small; together they changed self-image and behavior.

## Foot-in-the-door

- A tiny first request makes a much larger related request far more likely to be accepted. Homeowners who displayed a three-inch safe-driving sign later agreed in large numbers to a huge, ugly lawn billboard most neighbors refused.
- Even a first request on a different topic (signing a petition for state beauty) roughly halved refusals of the billboard.
- **Mechanism: self-image change.** People who comply become, in their own eyes, the kind of person who does this sort of thing. Prospects become customers; signers become activists.
- For sales: the purpose of the first small order is not profit, it is commitment. Once someone has bought, they are a customer.
- The general effect is what makes it dangerous: a small commitment can increase compliance with a range of larger, only loosely related requests.

## What makes a commitment effective

| Property | What it means | Evidence the chapter uses | Marketing form |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Active** | Doing or writing, not just listening | Captives were asked to write answers, even copy them | Have the customer state their goal, pick their plan, write a sentence |
| **Public** | Visible to others | People who recorded a judgment publicly were the least willing to change it; public votes produce more hung juries than secret ballots | Shared goals, public pledges, team plans |
| **Effortful** | Costs effort, time or discomfort | Severe initiations make newcomers value the group more | Meaningful onboarding work, applications, challenges |
| **Freely chosen (inner responsibility)** | No large reward or strong threat to explain it away | Small prizes in essay contests; a mild instruction kept children away from a toy weeks later, a threat did not | Light incentives, real choice, no coercion |

Notes on each:

- **Written commitments** create physical evidence the act happened, can be shown to others, and observers assume the writer meant it even when they know the writer had little choice. Having customers fill out their own sales agreement sharply reduced cancellations. Testimonial contests ('why I like this product in 50 words') get large numbers of people to go on record praising a product.
- **Being labeled** also binds: people told they were charitable gave more afterward. Others' view of us pressures self-image from the outside.
- **Public goals** work for self-improvement: writing a goal and sharing it with friends, or handing signed promise cards to people whose respect matters.
- **Effort** increases valuation of what was gained. Groups with harsh initiations resist replacing them with good works, because hardship that could be credited to charity would not produce the same inner commitment.
- **Inner responsibility** is the most important property. Large rewards or strong threats produce compliance but not ownership; the behavior stops when the pressure stops. Use the smallest sufficient reason.

## Commitments grow their own legs

- After a choice, people generate new reasons to support it. Those reasons can hold the decision up even if the original reason is removed.
- **Lowballing**: offer an advantage that induces a decision; after the decision but before the deal closes, remove the advantage. Car dealers 'discover an error', a manager refuses the price, or a generous trade-in estimate is revised down. Many buyers still close, because the decision now stands on new legs.
- **The same mechanism can serve good ends**: homeowners promised newspaper recognition for saving energy kept saving, and saved more, after being told the publicity was cancelled, because the remaining reasons were their own.
- Lowballing as a sales tactic is deceptive: the advantage was never real. In most markets it is also a consumer-protection violation (bait and switch).

## Honest use in marketing

- **Ladder of genuine steps**: free account, first project, first success, team invite, paid plan. Each step must deliver value on its own.
- **Ask for intent, then make it easy**: 'Which goal are you working on?' during onboarding, then tailor the product to it.
- **Let customers articulate why they chose you**: post-purchase surveys, reviews and case studies reinforce real commitment. Ask for honest reviews, not only positive ones.
- **Identity, not trap**: 'You are someone who ships on time' is legitimate when the product actually helps them do so.
- **Use small incentives**: big rewards for a review or referral buy the action but not the advocacy, and can be deceptive if undisclosed.
- **Keep the deal you offered**: the price and terms at decision time must be the price and terms at close.

## Worked example: fitness app onboarding

| Step | Commitment property | Honest design |
|---|---|---|
| 'What is your goal?' (choose one) | Active, freely chosen | Goals map to real plans the app delivers |
| 'Commit to 3 sessions this week' | Active, small | User picks the number; default is modest |
| Optional 'share your goal with a friend' | Public | Off by default; one tap to skip |
| First workout, with a short reflection ('How did it feel?') | Effortful, written | Reflection feeds the plan, not a sales pitch |
| Day 7: 'You did 3 of 3. Keep going with Premium for coached plans' | Consistency with demonstrated identity | Price shown is the price charged; free tier keeps working |

Not allowed: a quiz that 'proves' the user needs the premium plan, a trial price that rises after the card is entered, or a cancellation flow that says 'Are you giving up on your goals?'.

## Defending against exploitative consistency

- Consistency is usually right; the target is **foolish consistency**, the mechanical kind (Emerson's original line was about a 'foolish consistency', not consistency in general).
- **Stomach signs**: the tight feeling when you realize you are trapped into complying with something you do not want. When it appears, say plainly what the requester is doing; you are not bound by a commitment extracted by a trick.
- **Heart-of-hearts signs**: when no stomach signal appears because the commitment has grown its own supports, ask: 'Knowing what I now know, if I could go back in time, would I make the same choice?' Trust the first flash of feeling before rationalizations arrive.
- In a lowball, the test is whether you would have chosen this offer at this price if it had been the first one shown.

## Workflow

1. Define the long-term behavior you want (activation, upgrade, advocacy, renewal) and the identity that naturally supports it.
2. Design the smallest genuine first step that delivers value on its own.
3. Make each step active (the customer chooses or writes), optionally public, appropriately effortful, and freely chosen.
4. Keep incentives small enough that customers attribute the action to themselves.
5. Ask customers to articulate their reasons (goals, reviews, case studies) and reflect those reasons back.
6. Lock the terms: verify that every price, bonus and condition shown before the decision holds at close.
7. Audit for traps: survey pretexts, extracted statements used against the user, guilt-based cancellation copy.
8. Measure persistence (retention, repeat use), not just first conversion.

## Red flags

- A 'survey' or quiz that is a pretext to extract statements the sales pitch then holds against the prospect.
- Advertised price, bonus or trade-in value that changes after the customer commits (lowball, bait and switch).
- Cancellation or downgrade copy that shames the user for being inconsistent.
- Large rewards for reviews or referrals, especially undisclosed ones.
- First steps that deliver no value and exist only to start a 'yes ladder'.
- Pre-checked pledges, public sharing on by default, or commitments made on the user's behalf.
- A plan that relies on sunk cost ('you have already invested so much') rather than ongoing value.
- Asking for a commitment the product cannot help the customer keep.
