# Using Dynamics

Advantaged positions are valuable, but capturing an existing one usually costs more than it is worth. Besides pure innovation, the main way to gain fresh, undefended high ground is to **exploit a wave of exogenous change**: understand how the landscape is likely to evolve and channel resources toward positions that will become valuable and defensible as the change plays out. Strategy skill matters most in transitions, when the old pecking order can be overturned.

## Waves of change

- Waves are the net result of many shifts in technology, cost, competition, politics and buyer perception; no single organization creates them. Like an earthquake, they raise new high ground and level old high ground, erase advantages and enable new strategies.
- A wave is like wind in a sail: raw power the leader harnesses with insight and skill.
- **Keep perspective.** Buzz-speak claims change is always accelerating. Most industries, most of the time, are fairly stable; history shows earlier periods of even larger change. Perspective helps judge which changes truly matter.
- After a wave passes its effects are obvious, but it is too late. Act in the early stages. **The challenge is not forecasting but understanding the past and present**: assemble clues already in plain sight into a pattern pointing to fundamental forces.
- **Look past the main effect to second-order effects.** When television arrived, the main effect (people stay home) was obvious; the subtler effect was that routine studio films no longer drew audiences, so production shifted to independent producers assembling handpicked teams.

## Discerning the fundamentals

- Get into the gritty details; acquire enough expertise to **question the experts**. Leaders who stay 'above the details' do well in stable times but cannot ride a wave.
- Rumelt's account: a small router startup beat giants despite scale barriers. The visible explanation (stock options attract talent) was shallow. The deeper force: performance now lay in clever software written by small teams, a shift from economies of size to individual skill, spreading well beyond one industry.
- **Find the common cause** behind several trends. The microprocessor explained both the rise of software as advantage and the deconstruction of the integrated computer industry:
  - A general-purpose chip specialized by software could be made in high volume, and software wins because its **trial-and-error and fix cycle is far faster and cheaper** than hardware's.
  - When every component got its own processor and spoke a standard interface, **systems integration became trivial**, so the integrated firm's core skill lost value and specialist firms appeared for each module. The modern web of supplier relationships is the 'ghost' of the old integrated giant.
- Many crucial decisions do not look like decisions at the time; standard procedures (for example, giving up or acquiring rights to a component) can shape a company's future for decades.
- Success in a wave is richer than skill versus luck: the startup rode several simultaneous waves (software centrality, corporate networking, open vendor-neutral protocols, public internet adoption), avoided fatal errors, worked around barriers by supporting rivals' protocols, and faced incumbents held back by inertia and loyalty to proprietary standards.

## Five guideposts

1. **Rising fixed costs**: a jump in development or fixed costs forces consolidation; only the largest can cover them (as when a mature category needed expensive new R&D and smaller players exited).
2. **Deregulation**: regulated prices cross-subsidize some buyers at others' expense. After deregulation subsidies vanish fast, but incumbents keep chasing formerly profitable segments because of inertia, old mental maps and **poor cost data** (regulated firms build systems that hide true costs even from themselves). Expect them to cut profitable lines and keep unprofitable ones for years.
3. **Predictable forecasting biases**:
   - Forecasts rarely show a **peak**. For durable goods, rapid uptake means faster saturation and a sharp drop to replacement demand. The faster the adoption, the sooner the peak.
   - The 'battle of the titans' forecast: leaders are expected to slug it out, but the ground beneath both may crumble.
   - 'Copy today's winners': advice to imitate the current largest, most profitable or highest-valued firm, whose success may rest on conditions that are disappearing.
4. **Incumbent response**: incumbents resist transitions that undermine their accumulated skills and positions (see Inertia and Entropy).
5. **Attractor states**: how the industry **should** work given technology and demand, evolving toward efficiency in meeting buyer needs. It differs from a corporate 'vision' because it is based on overall efficiency, not one firm's wish to capture the pie. Complement it with:
   - **Accelerants**, such as **demonstration effects**: in-your-face evidence that suddenly changes buyer perception (when people saw that a song was just a file they could copy).
   - **Impediments**, such as slow, uncertain licensing that blocks the efficient path.

Rumelt's attractor-state sketch for general news media: dimensions of territory, frequency and depth; the efficient end state has specialists on each dimension rather than generalists bundling everything, so the strategic question for big papers is **unbundling**, not just 'moving online'.

## Applying to marketing and GTM

- Ask what the attractor state of the category is: where would an efficient market put value, given new technology and buyer behaviour? Position the brand at a node that will become high ground.
- Expect incumbents to defend bundles, proprietary standards and old segments; target the segments and open standards they are reluctant to embrace.
- Look for demonstration effects you can create: a public benchmark, a free tool, a visible customer result that makes the new way obvious.
- Do not copy the current category winner during a transition; test whether its success depends on fading conditions.
- In demand forecasts for durable or one-time purchases, model saturation and replacement explicitly.

Current note: Rumelt's examples are from the shift to microprocessors, software and the internet, and his media predictions date from around 2010. The same method applies to later waves (mobile, cloud, generative AI, privacy regulation, retail media); treat his specific forecasts as illustrations, not current guidance.

## Worked example: an agency-management software vendor facing AI

- Observed shifts: generative AI cuts the time to produce first drafts; clients demand faster, cheaper output; agencies move from hourly billing toward fixed-fee and retainer models.
- Common force: production labour is becoming cheap; scarce value moves to strategy, client relationships and verified quality.
- Second-order effect: timesheet-based tools lose relevance as hours stop being the billing unit; agencies need scope, margin-per-deliverable and approval workflows.
- Bias check: forecasts assume steady growth in seats; seat counts per agency may fall.
- Attractor state: agencies price by outcome or deliverable, with AI production inside the workflow and human review as the premium.
- Incumbents tie revenue to per-seat timesheets, so they will be slow to reprice.
- Moves: reposition from 'time tracking' to 'deliverable profitability'; price per active client rather than per seat; publish a benchmark of margin per deliverable as a demonstration effect.

## Workflow

1. List the shifts observed in the user's market from data, customer research and news, not opinion pieces.
2. Get into details enough to question expert commentary; look for one common cause behind several trends.
3. Separate main effects from second-order effects.
4. Check each guidepost; flag forecasting biases in the user's own plans.
5. Describe the attractor state, its accelerants and impediments.
6. Identify positions that will become high ground, and how incumbents are likely to respond given their inertia.
7. Recommend early, concentrated moves toward those positions; state what the user should not copy from current winners.
8. Set signals to watch that would confirm or refute the view.

## Red flags

- 'Everything is changing faster than ever' with no specific force identified.
- Forecasts of uninterrupted growth for a durable or one-time purchase.
- A strategy of copying the current market darling during a transition.
- Planning for a head-to-head battle among leaders while ignoring shifting ground.
- A corporate 'vision' of capturing the whole pie mistaken for where the industry is heading.
- Leaders who rely on summaries and never examine the underlying technology or economics.
- Defending a bundle that the efficient end state will unbundle.
