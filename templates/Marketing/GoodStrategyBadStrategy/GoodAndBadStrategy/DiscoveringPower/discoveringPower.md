# Discovering Power

Look for strength and weakness that others have not seen. A shift in perspective can turn an apparent weakness into a decisive advantage, or reveal that a rival's apparent strength is a liability. Not every good strategy rests on such an insight, but the ones that do separate ordinary excellence from the extraordinary.

## Preconceived strengths can be unsound

- A standard list of strengths and weaknesses (size, budget, experience, brand) often points the wrong way. Rumelt uses David and Goliath: on paper the giant wins; once you see the sling as precision force at a distance, his armour and size become irrelevant and the uncovered forehead becomes the critical weakness.
- The goal is the **discovery of a decisive asymmetry**: something you can do that the opponent cannot easily answer, aimed at a weakness the opponent has not covered.
- Treat SWOT as raw material, not as the strategy. The power comes from the reframe, not the list.

## Always ask about the competition

- Studying only the winner shows half the picture. Whenever someone succeeds greatly, there is **blocked or failed competition** at the same time.
- Competition is blocked by legal protection (patents, licences) or by natural barriers to imitation; it fails through inertia, incompetence or attention elsewhere.
- Key question for any success story: **why didn't rivals copy it?** If the answer is 'they could and will', the advantage is temporary.
- Half of what you learn in strategy work is to consider competitors even when the brief does not mention them.

## Coherent design resists piecemeal copying

- Policies that **complement** one another (each specialized to the others) form an integrated design. Rumelt shows a discount retailer whose checkout scanner data fed integrated inbound logistics, cross-docking, frequent deliveries and supplier deals; a rival who installed the same scanners gained little because it lacked the rest of the system.
- Competitors who build each function around a generic 'best practice' have little design. Others may be coherent but aimed at a different purpose. Both struggle against a tight design.
- Copying one element yields little benefit; a rival must adopt the whole design, which is slow, costly and often culturally impossible.

## Redefine the unit

- Conventional wisdom (a full-line discount store needs a population of 100,000) rested on sound cost logic. The winner did not break the logic; it **redefined the store**: the real unit was a regional network of many stores, a distribution hub and shared data and management flows, which together served millions.
- Once you see the new unit, other choices change meaning: locations express network economics, not just local demand; power shifts from the individual store to the network.
- Marketing version: the unit might be the **customer community** rather than the individual account, the **platform ecosystem** rather than the product, the **portfolio of content** rather than the campaign.

## The hidden cost of doctrine

- Decentralization (each unit 'on its own bottom') has a forgotten cost: **lost coordination**. Units that choose their own vendors, terms or messages cannot share a network or learn from each other.
- That cost stays hidden while competitors are also decentralized. Once a rival's insight makes the structure a disadvantage, the incumbent faces a problem.
- Changing a technique is manageable; breaking with **doctrine** (a basic philosophy of how the company runs) is rare without a near-death experience. A rival's doctrine is therefore one of the strongest barriers to it copying you.

## Compete by imposing asymmetric costs

- Reacting to an opponent's strengths (matching their spend, features or headcount) produces a shopping-list strategy. Rumelt describes a defence analysis that instead asked how to use one's own distinctive competences to exploit the other side's weaknesses and constraints.
- The principle: invest where **your move is cheap for you and expensive for the rival to counter**, and where their counter does not add to their offensive capability.
- Marketing translations:
  - A challenger offers free migration and month-to-month contracts; the incumbent's answer would cannibalize its multi-year contract base.
  - A direct-to-consumer brand competes on fast product iteration; the retail-locked incumbent's counter requires reworking its whole channel calendar.
  - An open-source vendor competes on transparency and community; the closed incumbent cannot respond without undermining its licensing model.

## Worked example: a challenger accounting SaaS

The incumbent dominates with a large channel of accounting-firm resellers, annual licences and deep features. The challenger is small and has no channel.

- Conventional wisdom: you need the accountant channel to reach small businesses.
- Reframe: the accountant is not the gatekeeper for businesses under 10 employees who start online; the unit is the **founder's first-year stack** (bank, payments, invoicing).
- Asymmetry: the challenger embeds in bank and payment onboarding, prices monthly and syncs automatically. The incumbent cannot match monthly pricing or bypass accountants without antagonizing its channel (its doctrine).
- Coherent design: embedded onboarding, automatic bank feeds, monthly pricing and an accountant read-only portal that turns accountants into allies later.

## Workflow

1. List the apparent strengths and weaknesses of the user and its main rival, then question each one: in what situation does it reverse?
2. Write down the market's conventional wisdom and the logic behind it; ask what definition it depends on (store, customer, sale, product).
3. For the current leader, ask why rivals have not copied it; classify as blocked (legal, natural barrier) or failed (inertia, doctrine, distraction).
4. Map the user's policies and check whether they complement one another; identify the missing pieces of a coherent design.
5. Search for moves that are cheap for the user and expensive for the rival to counter, especially where countering conflicts with the rival's doctrine or existing customers.
6. Express the insight in one or two sentences and feed it into the diagnosis of the kernel.

## Red flags

- A SWOT grid presented as the strategy, with no reframe or insight.
- 'Do what the leader does, but better' or copying one visible tactic of a competitor.
- Success analyses that never ask why competitors did not imitate.
- Matching a larger rival's budget line by line (features, ad spend, headcount).
- Every team runs its own channels, data and messages with no shared system.
- Accepting the category's conventional wisdom without examining its underlying definition.
