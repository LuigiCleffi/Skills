# Driving Growth in Competitive Markets

Growth comes from choosing the right product-market moves, then playing them according to your competitive role (leader, challenger, follower or nicher) and the life-cycle stage of the product and market. Size the gap between desired and projected sales, pick growth vectors that fill it, defend what you already hold, and change positioning and mix as the market evolves. Treat the product life cycle as a thinking tool for each stage's challenges, not as a forecast.

## Assessing growth opportunities

If projected sales fall short of desired sales, close the gap by improving existing businesses, developing or acquiring new ones, and pruning old ones to free resources.

### Product-market growth framework (Ansoff matrix)

| | Current customers | New customers |
|---|---|---|
| **Current products** | **Market penetration**: sell more of what you have to whom you have (show benefits that drive more buying, teach new uses). Usually the easiest. | **Market development**: new user groups in current areas (consumer to office/factory), new channels (mass, online), new locations at home or abroad. |
| **New products** | **Product development**: new features, different benefit sets at different price tiers, alternative technology substitutes. | **Diversification**: only when the outside industry is highly attractive and you have the right strengths. |

Evaluate in that order. Diversification types:

- **Concentric**: new products with technology or marketing synergies, even for different customers.
- **Horizontal**: complementary products for current customers, even with different production processes.
- **Conglomerate**: no relation to current technology, products or markets.

### Organic growth versus mergers and acquisitions

- **Organic growth** expands output and revenue internally; penetration and market development are usually organic. Product development and diversification may be either.
- Integration routes: **backward** (acquire suppliers for control or profit), **forward** (acquire wholesalers or retailers), **horizontal** (acquire competitors where law allows). Joint ventures are a lighter version.
- Mergers often fail on operational incompatibility (networks, processes, cultures) even when the strategic and financial logic is sound; plan integration in detail.
- **Prune, harvest or divest** tired businesses to release resources and cut costs; the more diversified the portfolio, the more likely you will need to.

### Innovation versus imitation

Levitt's **innovative imitation**: the innovator pays to develop the product, build distribution and educate the market; a follower can copy or improve it and earn high profits without that cost. In homogeneous, capital-intensive industries, **conscious parallelism** (similar offers, stable shares, avoiding share grabs that provoke retaliation) is common.

Followers still need a strategy: keep current customers, win a fair share of new ones, bring a distinctive advantage (location, service, financing), keep costs low and quality high, and enter new markets as they open, without inviting retaliation. Three follower types:

- **Cloner**: emulates the leader's product, name and packaging with slight variations.
- **Imitator**: copies some things but differentiates on packaging, advertising, price or location.
- **Adapter**: adapts or improves the leader's product, often for other markets; frequently becomes a future challenger.

Counterfeiting (duplicating product and packaging for illicit sale) is illegal and unethical, not a follower strategy; never recommend look-alike trade dress that confuses buyers. Borrowing ideas from other industries is a legitimate and rich source.

## Gaining market position

Market position has three dimensions:

- **Share of market**: sales revenue or units relative to the total market.
- **Share of mind**: percentage of customers naming the company first in the industry.
- **Share of heart**: percentage naming it as the company they would prefer to buy from.

Steady gains in mind share and heart share lead to gains in market share and profit. A **market leader** has the largest share and usually leads in price changes, launches, distribution coverage and promotional intensity, but must stay vigilant against innovations, fresh marketing angles, big competitor investments and its own rising costs.

Three routes to gain and hold position: grow sales to current customers, create new markets, and protect share.

### Grow sales to current customers

- **Amount per occasion**: larger packages or redesigned products raise usage per occasion; more availability raises impulse consumption. Smaller packs can also raise volume through more frequent use.
- **New usage occasions**: link the brand to occasions where it is appropriate (holidays, after meals).
- **Correct perceived usage**: people underestimate how often to replace short-lived products. Tie replacement to a calendar event (daylight saving time for smoke-alarm batteries), tell users when they first used it, or build in a wear indicator.
- **New uses**: completely new applications (recipes, a baking soda as a fridge deodorizer), which can then support brand extensions.

### Create new markets

**Pioneer advantage**. Sources: early users remember a satisfying pioneer; the pioneer defines which attributes the category should have; it usually aims at the middle of the market; customer inertia; producer advantages (scale, technology leadership, patents, scarce assets, entry barriers). Long-term leadership rests on vision of a mass market, persistence, relentless innovation, financial commitment and strategic assets. Speed matters, but rushing a launch with incomplete execution stalls momentum.

It is not inevitable. Pioneers fail when the product is too crude, poorly positioned or early to demand; when development costs exhaust them; when bigger entrants outspend them; or through complacency. Imitators win with lower prices, continuous improvement or brute market power. Distinguish **inventor** (first patents), **product pioneer** (first working model) and **market pioneer** (first to sell): counting failed pioneers, more market pioneers fail and more early (non-pioneer) leaders succeed than often reported. Radical pioneers survive less often than incremental innovators. Brand leadership tends to persist better in downturns and high inflation, and less well in expansions; about half of leading brands eventually lose the lead, and lost leadership is rarely regained; persistence is higher in food and household goods than in durables and clothing.

**Niche markets**: lead a small market instead of following in a large one. Nichers know their customers so well they deliver superior value, charge a premium, keep costs low and build a strong culture: high margin versus the mass marketer's high volume. The principle is **specialization**:

- **Customer specialist**: one type of end user, or one locality or region.
- **Product or service specialist**: one product line or product, or a service nobody else offers.

Nichers also create, expand and protect their markets. Risk: the niche dries up or is attacked, leaving specialized resources with no alternative use. "Stick to your niching, not necessarily to your niche": prefer **multiple niching** for survival (Kotler cites a lighter maker that broadened from smoking to 'flame' and outdoor products as smoking declined).

### Expand the total market

When the total market grows, the dominant firm gains most. Look for new users (**new-market segment strategy**: people who never used the category; **geographical expansion**: people elsewhere) and more usage from current users. Design genuinely for the new segment rather than shrinking and recoloring existing products. Do not neglect existing customers while chasing new ones.

## Defending market position

The best defense is **continuous innovation**: lead in new products, services, distribution effectiveness and cost cutting, so customers feel privileged, not trapped.

Three ways of meeting needs:

- **Responsive marketing**: find a stated need and fill it.
- **Anticipative marketing**: look ahead to needs customers will soon have.
- **Creative marketing**: discover solutions customers did not ask for but embrace. Creative marketers are **market-driving**, not just market-driven, and escape the "tyranny of the served market" by changing the rules of the game.

A defensive strategy aims to reduce the probability of attack, divert attacks to less-threatened areas and reduce their intensity, by legally and ethically making it harder for rivals to launch, get distribution, and win awareness, trial and repeat. Speed of response matters. Six defenses:

| Defense | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Position** | Own the most desirable position in buyers' minds (the key functional benefit of the category) |
| **Flank** | Build outposts (often flanker brands) to protect a weak front or support a counterattack |
| **Preemptive** | Attack first: guerrilla hits across the market, broad envelopment, or a stream of announced new products. Do not announce products you will not ship ('vaporware') |
| **Counteroffensive** | Meet the attacker frontally, hit its flank, or use a pincer; or use economic clout (cross-subsidized prices), product-upgrade announcements, lobbying or legal action. Stay within competition law; predatory pricing and sham litigation are off-limits |
| **Repositioning** | **Market broadening** (refocus from the product to the underlying generic need, e.g. 'petroleum' to 'energy') or **market diversification** into unrelated industries |
| **Contraction** | **Strategic withdrawal**: give up weak markets and reassign resources to strong ones; split portfolios into focused businesses |

Choice depends on resources, goals and expected competitor reactions.

## Challenger strategies

First set the strategic objective (usually more share) and choose whom to attack:

- **The leader**: high risk, high payoff; sensible when the leader is serving the market poorly.
- **Firms of similar size** that are underperforming or underfinanced (obsolete products, excessive prices, unhappy customers).
- **Small local and regional firms** (roll-ups).
- **A whole industry** that underserves customers (disruption).

Five attacks:

- **Frontal**: match the opponent's product, price, promotion and distribution; the side with more resources wins. A **modified frontal** attack (e.g. lower price with credible equal quality) can work if the leader does not retaliate.
- **Flank**: find gaps created by market shifts or uncovered needs, or attack where the opponent underperforms geographically. Best for challengers with fewer resources; more likely to succeed than frontal.
- **Encirclement**: a grand offensive on several fronts at once; needs superior resources.
- **Bypass**: diversify into unrelated products, into new geographies, or leapfrog to new technology.
- **Guerrilla**: small intermittent attacks (selective price cuts, promotional blitzes, occasional legal action) to win footholds; usually must be followed by a stronger attack.

Any element of the mix can be the basis of attack; success comes from combining several over time. Once successful, keep a **challenger mentality** and keep showing how you do things differently.

## Product life cycle (PLC) strategies

Assumptions: products have limited lives; sales pass through distinct stages with different challenges; profits rise and fall; each stage needs different marketing, finance, manufacturing, purchasing and HR strategies. Apply it to a category, a product form or a brand. Not every product goes through every stage.

| | Introduction | Growth | Maturity | Decline |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | Low | Rapidly rising | Peak | Declining |
| Cost per customer | High | Average | Low | Low |
| Profits | Negative | Rising | High | Declining |
| Customers | Innovators | Early adopters | Mainstream | Laggards |
| Competitors | Few | Growing | Many | Declining |
| **Objective** | Awareness and trial | Maximize share | Maximize profit while defending share | Cut spending, harvest |
| Product | Basic | Improve, add line extensions | Diversify offerings | Phase out weak items |
| Price | Cost-plus | Penetration | Match or beat competitors | Cut |
| Communications | Awareness and trial among early adopters and dealers | Awareness and interest in the mass market | Stress brand differences, encourage switching | Minimum to retain customers |
| Distribution | Selective | Intensive | More intensive | Drop unprofitable outlets |

### Introduction

Slow growth while technical problems are fixed, pipelines filled and acceptance gained. Promotion-to-sales ratio is highest: inform, induce trial, secure distribution. Prices higher; focus on buyers most ready to buy. Expect well-resourced incumbents to enter once you prove the market.

### Growth

Rapid sales climb; competitors enter with new features and distribution; prices stabilize or fall; marketing-to-sales ratio falls; learning effects lower unit costs. Moves:

- Improve quality, add features and styling.
- Add models and **flanker** products (sizes, flavors) to protect the core.
- Enter new segments and channels; widen coverage.
- Shift communication from awareness and trial to preference and loyalty.
- Lower prices to reach the next layer of price-sensitive buyers.

You trade current profit for share and future profit; decide how much to reinvest based on goals and resources. Watch for the slowdown in growth rate and prepare.

### Maturity

Most products are here and it lasts longest. Three phases: **growth maturity** (growth slows, no new channels), **stable maturity** (per-capita sales flat; replacement and population growth drive sales), **decaying maturity** (sales fall, customers switch). Overcapacity intensifies competition; a few giants (often a quality leader, a service leader, a cost leader) plus many nichers remain. Decide: become one of the volume giants, or niche for margin. Markets often polarize into low and high ends and erode the middle; escape by re-segmenting around latent needs (Kotler's appliance-maker example segments around about 20 lifestyle types).

"Mature" categories can be revived by new business models. Two levers:

- **Market growth** (sales = number of users x usage rate):
  - More users: **convert nonusers** (primary demand stimulation) or **attract competitors' customers** (steal share).
  - More usage: more usage occasions, more consumption per occasion, new usage occasions.
- **Product modification**: **quality** improvement ('new and improved' performance), **feature** improvement (size, weight, materials, accessories that add versatility, safety or convenience), **style** improvement (aesthetics). Sustainability upgrades can be essential for survival in some categories.

### Decline

Causes: technology, taste shifts, domestic and foreign competition, leading to overcapacity, price cutting and profit erosion. Distinguish structural decline from a short-term marketing crisis. Options:

- **Harvest**: gradually cut costs (R&D, plant investment, then quality, sales force, marginal services, advertising) while maintaining sales as long as possible; increases cash flow; hard to execute.
- **Divest**: sell a product with strong distribution and residual goodwill (specialist firms revive 'orphan' brands for their remaining awareness). If no buyer, liquidate quickly or slowly, and decide what inventory and service to keep for existing customers.
- **Eliminate weak products**: they consume management time, need frequent price and inventory changes, short costly runs, divert ad and sales effort, hurt company image, and delay the search for replacements. Set up a **product-review committee** (marketing, R&D, manufacturing, finance) that recommends leave, modify or drop for each product.
- Exit timing depends on **exit barriers** (low barriers let rivals leave and survivors pick up their customers) and on industry attractiveness versus your strength: unattractive industry plus strength, shrink selectively; attractive industry plus strength, invest more. Rejuvenation usually works by adding value.

### Alternative patterns, fads and trends

Critics: shapes and durations vary too much to generalize, stages are hard to identify (a plateau can precede another surge), and the PLC can be self-fulfilling. Common patterns:

- **Growth-slump-maturity**: quick growth, then a petrified level from late adopters and replacements (small kitchen appliances).
- **Cycle-recycle**: a second, smaller cycle from a later promotional push (new drugs).
- **Scalloped**: successive cycles from new characteristics, uses or users (a fiber finding ever more applications).

- A **fad** is unpredictable, short-lived and without social, economic or political significance; quick zeal, early peak, fast decline, limited following. Profiting requires luck and timing, and it is sometimes hard to tell a fad from a lasting product.
- A **trend** is a direction or sequence of events with momentum and durability; more predictable, it reveals the shape of the future and gives strategic direction.

**Market evolution**: markets move through emergence, growth, maturity and decline; innovation and competition fragment and re-consolidate them as competitors copy each new attribute (Kotler traces paper towels from a single product to absorbent, strong, lint-free and surface-specific wipes). Visualize the path (needs, competitors, technology, channels) and reposition ahead of it.

Current note: digital platforms and network effects (two-sided marketplaces) can compress life cycles and make leadership more winner-take-most; the frameworks still apply, but check whether network effects change who can win.

## Worked example: growth plan for a mid-market B2B accounting SaaS

Situation: 8 percent growth in a maturing category; a low-cost entrant is attacking small firms; enterprise suites are moving down-market.

| Vector | Move | Role and rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Penetration | In-app usage nudges for under-used modules; annual billing incentive | Increase usage rate of current users |
| Market development | Package for accounting firms serving many small clients (new user group, new channel) | Convert nonusers through a partner channel |
| Product development | Payroll add-on via partnership | New benefit set at a higher tier |
| Defense | Flank brand 'Lite' tier against the low-cost entrant; position defense on 'audit-ready books' | Protect the core without cutting the main price |
| Contraction | Sunset the desktop edition (decline stage) with a migration offer and 24 months of support | Free engineering for growth bets |
| Avoid | Diversifying into unrelated HR software | Industry attractive but no relevant strengths |

PLC read: core product in stable maturity, so emphasize differentiation and switching incentives rather than awareness; the payroll add-on is in introduction, so sell selectively to early adopters.

## Workflow

1. Quantify the growth gap and identify the company's competitive role and position (market, mind and heart share).
2. Diagnose the life-cycle stage of category, product form and brand, using sales growth, competitor count, price trends and buyer type; check for alternative patterns.
3. Generate options with the Ansoff matrix, cheapest first; mark organic versus acquisition paths.
4. Choose role-appropriate moves: leaders expand the market and defend; challengers pick a target and attack type; followers choose cloner, imitator or adapter without provoking retaliation; nichers specialize and build multiple niches.
5. Set the mix (product, price, communications, distribution) to match the stage.
6. For mature products, test market-growth (users x usage) and product-modification levers before harvesting.
7. Run a product review: leave, modify, harvest, divest or drop; plan support for existing customers on exit.
8. Screen new opportunities as fad versus trend; map the market's evolution path and reposition ahead of it.
9. Pick metrics: share of market, mind and heart, usage rate, new users, profit by product.

## Red flags

- Jumping to diversification before exhausting penetration and market development.
- An acquisition plan with no operational integration plan.
- Assuming first-mover status guarantees leadership; launching a radical product before demand exists.
- A leader defending only by price cuts, or announcing products it cannot ship.
- A challenger attacking the leader head-on with fewer resources and no differentiation.
- Look-alike packaging close enough to confuse buyers.
- A single-niche business with no plan for the niche drying up.
- Using introduction-stage tactics (awareness, cost-plus pricing) in a mature market, or maturity tactics (steal-share discounts) in a nascent one.
- Keeping weak products alive by default with no review process.
- Mistaking a fad spike for a trend and building capacity for it.
- Chasing new segments while neglecting the existing base.
