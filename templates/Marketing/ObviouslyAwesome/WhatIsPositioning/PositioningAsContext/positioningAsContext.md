# Positioning as Context

Positioning is the deliberate choice of how a product is the best at something that a clearly defined market cares a lot about. It works as context setting: buyers meeting something new grab every available clue to decide what it is, who it is for and why they should care, and that context can make a world-class product look worthless or an ordinary one look essential. Never accept the "default" positioning a product was born with; check whether it still puts the product's strengths at the center, and change it deliberately when it does not.

## Why positioning comes first

- Every tactic (account-based marketing, content, campaigns, sales pitches, growth experiments) takes positioning as an input. Better targeting needs a clear best-fit customer; better content needs clear value and differentiators.
- Weak positioning is a constant headwind: a bigger budget behind unclear positioning just repeats a confusing message louder. Strong positioning feels like a tailwind; buyers "get it" without effort.
- Positioning has its own positioning problem: some people equate it with messaging, others with all of marketing. Settle the definition before doing the work (see [Positioning baggage](../../PositioningProcess/PositioningBaggage/positioningBaggage.md)).
- The idea dates from Ries and Trout (1981), who explained *what* positioning is but gave little *how*; they taught it through agency ad campaigns. Dunford's contribution is a repeatable process usable by a product team, not an ad agency.
- Choice overload makes it more critical now than then: millions of apps and products compete for shrinking attention.

## Positioning is context

- Buyers form context from the whole presentation: messaging, pricing, features, branding, partners and visible customers. Each is a clue about what the product is.
- Context determines perceived value. Dunford uses a famous experiment, a celebrated violinist playing unrecognized for tips in a subway station, to show that identical "product" quality is ignored when nothing in the setting signals that it is special.
- People make sense of the new by anchoring on something they already know ("like a skateboard, but self-propelled"). The frame of reference a buyer picks can make or break the business.
- Most products are exceptional only inside their best frame of reference. Outside it they look redundant, unremarkable or simply wrong.
- Teams fail to choose context deliberately because they believe their context is obvious.

## The two traps of default positioning

Most products can be positioned in several markets, but creators unconsciously pick one ("dessert", "email") at the moment of conception and never revisit it.

**Trap 1: the product became something else, and you did not notice.**

- The moment you name the market, you have made four business decisions before building anything:
  1. **Target buyers and where you sell.**
  2. **Competitive alternatives.**
  3. **Pricing and margin.**
  4. **Key features and roadmap.**
- Dunford's baker shows this: the same batter sold as a fancy cake versus a single-serve muffin implies different channels, rivals, price points and feature priorities. Same product, two different businesses.
- Iteration (prototype, feedback, add and remove features) slowly turns the product into something else: email drifts into group chat, a database into an analytics platform. Creators still see the original idea; customers see a mismatch and either get confused or dismiss the product as poorly conceived.

**Trap 2: you designed for a market, and the market moved.**

- Competitors, technology, preferences, economics and regulation keep reshaping how buyers see a market.
- A product can become poorly positioned without changing at all. Dunford's example: a healthy "diet muffin" loses to an almost identical product framed as a "gluten-free paleo snack" because buyers stopped thinking of themselves as dieting.
- Tech teams are especially exposed: their customers (nurses, insurance agents, plant managers) live in a context very different from the startup bubble, and shifts there are easy to miss.

## Default versus deliberate positioning

- Default positioning describes the product the way its creator thinks about it. Deliberate positioning finds the context in which the product's distinctive features make obvious sense.
- Test: in the default frame, do your best features sound like improvements, or like the product "doing things it was not meant to do"? Dunford's "cake on a stick" sounds wrong as cake but obvious as a grown-up lollipop (a cake pop): in the right frame, the stick and the ball belong.
- Dunford's own startup sold a fast-analysis "database" that prospects dismissed as an unnecessary second database; reframed as a data warehouse, it was compared to the right alternatives, its analysis speed became the point, and the roadmap re-aligned around the new frame.
- Repositioning changes how the company sees itself, not only how customers see the product: roadmap, pricing and target accounts follow.

## Symptoms of weak positioning

| Symptom | What is really happening | Quick diagnostic |
|---|---|---|
| Happy customers love you, but prospects cannot figure out what you sell | Prospects invent a position for you, often one that hides your strengths | Show the product to good-fit prospects (not friends) and ask them to describe it back; compare with how happy customers describe it |
| Long sales cycles, low close rates, late-stage losses | Poor-fit prospects consume sales time and then pick a rival that fit them all along | Look at won vs lost deals: were the losses ever a fit? |
| High early churn; customers asking for features you will never build | Customers bought for the wrong reasons and are trying to turn you into what they thought they bought | Read churn reasons and feature requests from customers who left in the first renewal cycle |
| Price pressure, "you are too expensive" | You look like everything else, so buyers default to price | Can prospects name what you do that alternatives cannot? |

When positioning is broken it usually looks like a marketing or sales execution problem. Dig below the surface before adding budget, headcount or new channels.

## What great positioning accounts for

1. The customer's view of the problem and of the **alternative** ways to solve it.
2. How you are **uniquely different** from those alternatives, and why that matters to customers.
3. The **characteristics of customers** who value what only you deliver.
4. The **market context** that makes that value obvious to those customers.

These become the components and the 10-step process: see [Five (plus one) components](../FiveComponents/fiveComponents.md).

## Worked example

A B2B SaaS team built "a project-management tool for creative agencies". Prospects compare it to Asana and Monday and ask why it lacks Gantt charts. Happy customers, though, use it mostly to log billable hours against client retainers and to stop over-servicing clients.

- Default frame: project management. Implied rivals: big PM suites. Implied features: Gantt, dependencies, portfolio views. Implied price: per seat, low.
- Candidate deliberate frame: retainer profitability / agency operations software. Implied rivals: spreadsheets plus a time tracker. Implied features: retainer burn, client profitability, over-servicing alerts, which it already has. Implied price: tied to revenue managed, higher.
- Next step: confirm with best-fit customers (Step 1) and their real alternatives (Step 4) before changing any copy.

## Workflow

1. Restate the user's problem in positioning terms: is the issue understanding (what is it, who is it for, why care), not reach or volume?
2. Check the four symptoms against data the user has: prospect descriptions, win/loss notes, early churn reasons, discount requests.
3. Write down the current (probably default) frame and the four decisions it implies: buyers and channel, alternatives, pricing, roadmap.
4. Ask whether the product drifted (Trap 1) or the market moved (Trap 2) since that frame was chosen.
5. Test whether the best features sound natural or strange in the current frame; brainstorm two or three alternative frames where they would sound obvious.
6. Do not rewrite messaging yet. Route the work into the 10-step process, starting with [best customers](../../PositioningProcess/BestCustomers/bestCustomers.md).

## Red flags

- "Our category is obvious" or "we have always been an X", with no evidence that customers see it that way.
- Demos and first calls spent explaining what the product is rather than why it is better.
- Increasing ad spend or sales headcount to fix confused prospects.
- Website copy describing the product exactly as the founders imagined it on day one.
- The product's most loved capability sounds odd or irrelevant in the stated category.
- Customers and prospects describe the product in incompatible ways.
- Features requested by churned customers driving the roadmap.
- Competing on price because nobody can say what is different.
