# Step 1. Understand the Customers Who Love Your Product

Every positioning component depends on the others, so begin with evidence that already exists: the customers who love you. Make a short list of best-fit customers (they understood the product quickly, bought quickly, became fans, referred others and serve as references) and use it as the reference point for every later step. If you do not yet have enough happy customers to see a pattern, keep positioning loose and test widely instead.

## Why start with customers

- Category depends on targets and value; value depends on differentiators; differentiators depend on alternatives; but the category also tells customers who the alternatives are. The loop is broken by starting with real customers.
- Look for what works and decode why: which campaigns brought the most leads, which content was consumed most, which events drew the most. Following those threads leads to the happiest customers.
- Then ask two questions: what about the offering makes them so happy, and what about *them* makes them such a good fit?
- Surveying all customers tends to give muddy results. Surveying only the ecstatic fans reveals a clear pattern, and suggests that more customers could look like them if marketing and sales targeted companies with similar characteristics.
- Heterogeneous customer bases hide patterns; isolate the best-fit subset first.

## The best-customer list

A best customer:

- Understood the product quickly.
- Bought quickly.
- Became a raving fan.
- Referred other customers and acts as a reference.
- Is the type of customer you want more of, at least in the short term.

Template:

| Customer | Time to "get it" | Sales cycle | Discount asked? | Referrals / reference? | Why they say they chose us | Notable traits |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | | | |

Aim for a short list (often 5 to 15 accounts or customer profiles). Record their own words; they feed Steps 4 to 7.

## No super-happy customers yet?

- The process assumes enough happy customers to see who loves the product and why. Without that pattern, hold off on tightening positioning.
- Put the product in front of a fairly wide range of prospects and watch for patterns: bigger vs smaller businesses, specific industries, shared traits among the happiest users.
- Guesses about who loves the product are often wrong; keep minds open and positioning broad until data says otherwise.
- Dunford's fishing-net analogy: call it a "fish net" rather than a "grouper net" until you see what you actually catch; if you keep landing tuna, move to where the tuna are.

## Do not start with the product

- Starting with features feels natural but is a trap: listing features unconsciously compares you to a set of competitors, usually the ones *you* watch (often rival startups competing for investment), not the ones customers consider.
- Most targets have never heard of you or your rivals; they want to know how you compare to what they use today. Customer-facing positioning must use the customer's frame of reference.

## Customers are not investors

| | Investors | Customers |
|---|---|---|
| Buying | What the company will become in 5 to 10 years | A fix for a problem they have now |
| Want to hear | Vision, big fast-growing market, leadership | Immediate value, relief of immediate pain, that you will still be around |
| "Disruption" sounds like | Opportunity | Ripping out things they invested in |

- Website, sales and marketing materials, pricing and the near-term roadmap serve customers and should reflect customer positioning.
- Investors understand that the pitch deck and the sales deck tell different stories. This process is designed for customers.

## Product, company or both?

- Single-product companies: position the product and the company as the same thing, with one name, brand and position. Fewer things for customers to remember. Many large companies (Slack, Twilio, WD-40 among Dunford's examples) grew big on one product; if you have a separate company brand and product brand, put your energy into the product.
- Multi-product companies: product positioning and company positioning are separate but linked. Each product sold individually needs its own position; company positioning is the umbrella explaining why to buy several offerings.
- Which first:
  - Most customers first meet you through one product, or you land with one product and expand: position that product first.
  - You mainly sell a bundle: position the company first.
- For company positioning, read "product" as "the entire portfolio of offerings" throughout the process.
- Dunford's Wave story shows a free accounting tool repositioning the company as small-business financial software as payroll and payments became core: the name, messaging and business model all followed.

## Worked example

A B2B SaaS for HVAC and plumbing contractors ("dispatch and scheduling") has 300 customers with mixed satisfaction.

- Mining what works: the webinar on "no-show and callback costs" drove the most demos; the most-read article is about emergency-call routing.
- Ecstatic fans (NPS 9 to 10, referred others, signed in under two weeks): 14 accounts. Pattern: 15 to 60 technicians, more than 30 percent of revenue from 24/7 emergency calls, previously dispatching from a whiteboard plus phone.
- Moderately happy customers: small two-truck shops that mostly book planned maintenance; they ask for discounts and churn.
- Output: the 14-account best-customer list, with their quotes, used for Steps 4 to 7.

## Workflow

1. Clarify what is being positioned: one product, a company, or a lead product of a portfolio.
2. Pull signals of what works: top lead sources, content, events, fastest-closing deals, highest NPS, referrals.
3. Build the best-customer list using the five criteria; exclude merely satisfied customers.
4. Interview or survey only those best customers: why they chose you, what they would do without you, what they love.
5. Note shared, observable traits (not just firmographics) for later steps.
6. If no clear pattern appears, recommend loose positioning and broad testing rather than forcing a narrow position.
7. Keep investor narrative separate from customer positioning in every artifact.

## Red flags

- Positioning built on averaged feedback from all customers.
- The persona is aspirational ("enterprise CIOs") while all the happy customers are something else.
- A pre-traction startup locking into a narrow niche based on a guess.
- The kickoff meeting starts with a feature list or a competitor matrix of startups customers have never heard of.
- The website reads like the investor deck: vision, TAM, "disrupting" the customer's industry.
- A single-product company spending effort building a separate corporate brand.
- A multi-product company positioning the umbrella while customers buy one entry product.
