# Why So Much Bad Strategy?

Bad strategy is rarely a calculation error. It is the active avoidance of the hard work of strategy: analysis, logic and above all choice. Watch for the three most common escape routes (refusing to choose, filling in a template, and positive-thinking magic) and steer teams away from them.

## Pathway 1: the unwillingness or inability to choose

- Strategy means focus, and focus means setting aside some goals, interests and people's hopes in favour of others. Strategy is 'scarcity's child'.
- **Why not do all the options?**
  - If conflict is always resolved by adopting everything, nobody has an incentive to sharpen their argument; only the prospect of choice draws out the best evidence and reasoning.
  - Deep transformations of the core (for example, shifting from hardware to services, or to components) each need wholly new skills; two at once is not feasible.
- **Condorcet cycling**: with three executives preferring three directions in different orders, any majority coalition is unstable; a better voting scheme cannot fix it (Arrow's theorem). Asking such a group for consensus produces a political compromise such as 'committed to high-quality products and services and to leadership', which hurts no ego and cripples the whole. Rumelt's example is a computer maker whose leadership deadlocked this way and chose a direction years too late.
- Serious strategy work in a successful organization often waits until 'the wolf is at the door'.
- When a strategy works, people remember what was achieved, not the painful options set aside. Rumelt cites a leader who dropped his own campaign promise after a rigorous policy study, and a chipmaker that exited the product line its identity was built on (taking over a year against resistance from sales and research) to focus on the business that made it a leader.
- Any change in strategy makes someone worse off unless collective ruin is imminent, so expect powerful opposition.
- **Universal buy-in usually signals the absence of choice.** Mom-and-apple-pie goals everyone agrees on are evidence of insufficient will or power to make hard choices.
- The longer a pattern of resource allocation persists, the more it is treated as an entitlement; new structures are malleable, old ones need crisis-level power to reshape.

Marketing translations: budgets split evenly across brand, demand gen, partner and events because every leader must get something; three ICPs kept 'for now'; two repositionings pursued in parallel; a product line kept alive because it was the founding product.

## Pathway 2: template-style strategy

- The template: **Vision** (be 'the best', 'the leading', 'the best known'), **Mission** (a high-sounding statement of purpose), **Values** (noncontroversial), **Strategies** (aspirations relabelled), then initiatives.
- It produces pious statements of the obvious presented as insight ('our university educates leaders and extends knowledge' = 'we are a university'). Being 'known' is a celebrity-magazine standard of success.
- It is attractive because it skips analysis of the real challenges, and because couching everything in positives hurts no feelings.
- It descends from the idea that charismatic, 'transformational' leadership (have a vision, inspire sacrifice, empower people) can be bottled as a formula: Rumelt calls it **charisma-in-a-can**.
- **Leadership is not strategy.** Leadership inspires and motivates sacrifice and helps people accept painful change. Strategy is the craft of figuring out which purposes are both worth pursuing and capable of being accomplished. They can live in the same person, but vision without attention to obstacles and action leads followers to sacrifice for nothing; vision joined to a careful strategy can achieve great ends.
- Cost of the template: people who want to build a real strategy are surrounded by empty rhetoric and bad examples, and audiences learn to tune all such statements out.

Marketing translations: brand 'pyramids' and purpose statements filled in from a workshop template, with nothing about which customers, against which alternative, by what mechanism the brand wins.

## Pathway 3: New Thought

- The belief, about 150 years old and constantly re-presented as new, that thinking about success produces success and thinking about failure produces failure.
- It has seeped into management through 'shared vision' and commitment doctrines that ask executives to suspend disbelief and banish negative thoughts.
- Its historical claims are distorted: famous successes came from pockets of outstanding competence and luck (a clever engineering trick, a killer application, materials and process genius), not from a vision shared at every level.
- Copy what successful leaders **did**, not what their quotations say. Rumelt notes that a famous advocate of 'stretch' also exited whole businesses where he saw no advantage, without asking those divisions to buy in.
- All analysis starts by considering what may happen, including unwelcome events. You would not fly in a plane designed by people who pictured only flying and never considered failure modes. Belief that intention alone shapes the world displaces critical thinking.

Marketing translations: 'if we believe in the launch it will go viral', forecasts justified by conviction, pre-mortems refused as negativity, manifesto-led brand plans with no competitive analysis.

## Template: choice forcing session

| Step | Output |
|---|---|
| State the competing directions as distinct options (A, B, C) | One-line description each |
| For each, list what it requires that the company lacks | Capability gaps |
| For each, list who loses resources or status | Affected teams and budgets |
| Can two be done at once? Do they share skills, channels, customers? | Feasibility verdict |
| Decision owner (not a consensus vote) | Named person |
| Chosen option and explicit 'set aside' list | Decision record |
| Pre-mortem: why might the chosen option fail? | Top risks and mitigations |

## Worked example: a mid-size fintech leadership offsite

Three VPs argue for three futures: deepen SMB lending (CRO), pivot to embedded-finance APIs for platforms (CPO), expand to consumer cards (CMO). A draft output reads: 'We will be the leading financial partner for businesses of every size, delivering innovative solutions.' That is the consensus failure.

Apply the chapter:

- Recognize the cycling: each pair can form a majority, none stable. Stop seeking consensus; the CEO decides after a time-boxed analysis.
- Feasibility: embedded APIs and consumer cards each require a new sales motion, compliance stack and brand; not both.
- Choose embedded finance; set aside consumer cards and freeze new SMB lending products; the brand team shifts from consumer awareness to developer marketing.
- Pre-mortem: platforms may build in-house; mitigate with revenue-share pricing and two anchor launches.
- Communicate what is being dropped and why, and expect resistance from teams losing budget.

## Workflow

1. Read the strategy material and classify any vision, mission, values or slogan content as non-strategy context.
2. Look for evidence of avoided choice: many priorities, even budget splits, several transformations at once, a compromise statement.
3. Map the competing options and their sponsors; check for cycling preferences or entitlements protecting old allocations.
4. Run the choice-forcing template; name the decision owner and the explicit set-aside list.
5. Separate leadership messaging (inspiration for the change) from strategy content (diagnosis, guiding policy, actions).
6. Run a pre-mortem or failure-mode review; treat refusal to discuss risks as a warning sign.
7. Rewrite into a kernel and flag who will be worse off so the leader can plan for opposition.

## Red flags

- 'Everyone bought in' is offered as proof of a good strategy.
- A strategy statement written to be acceptable to every executive in the room.
- Simultaneous repositioning, new segment entry and new business model.
- Vision, mission and values slides as the whole of the brand or marketing strategy.
- Analysis of risks dismissed as negativity; forecasts justified by belief.
- Copying a famous CEO's quotes rather than examining what that company actually did.
- Budgets allocated by history or seniority rather than by the strategy.
