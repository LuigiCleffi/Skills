# The Kernel of Good Strategy

Build every strategy around its kernel: a **diagnosis** of the challenge, a **guiding policy** for dealing with it, and a set of **coherent actions** that carry out the policy. Vision, goal hierarchies, time horizons and frameworks are supporting players; if the kernel is missing or misshapen, the strategy has a serious problem. Good strategy is coherent action backed by an argument.

## The three elements

1. **Diagnosis**: defines or explains the nature of the challenge. It simplifies overwhelming complexity by identifying certain aspects as critical.
2. **Guiding policy**: the overall approach chosen to cope with or overcome the obstacles in the diagnosis.
3. **Coherent actions**: steps coordinated with one another to accomplish the guiding policy, including resource commitments.

Analogies: a doctor reads symptoms and history (diagnosis), chooses a therapeutic approach (guiding policy), prescribes diet, therapy and medication (actions). In business the challenge is usually change and competition; in many large organizations it is internal (outdated routines, entrenched interests, poor cross-unit cooperation), so the guiding policy is reorganization and the actions are changes in people, power and procedures.

The kernel needs no particular theory of advantage, no debate about the difference between missions and objectives, and no corporate/business/product levels.

## The diagnosis

- The single most useful question: **'What's going on here?'** Much of strategy is comprehending the situation, not only deciding what to do.
- A diagnosis names or classifies the situation, links facts into patterns and says which issues deserve more attention and which less.
- Classifying the situation as a type gives access to knowledge of how analogous situations were handled.
- An **explicit** diagnosis lets you evaluate the rest of the strategy and revisit it when circumstances change.
- Most real challenges are **ill-structured**: nobody is sure how to define the problem, there is no obvious list of options, and links between actions and outcomes are unclear. So a diagnosis cannot be deduced; it is an educated **judgment about the meaning of facts**.
  - Example frame: a slowing retail chain could be 'a problem of managing expectations', 'a search for new growth platforms' or 'an eroding competitive advantage'. None is an action, each points to different actions and sets others aside, and none can be proven.
- A diagnosis replaces complexity with a **simpler story** that calls attention to crucial aspects.
- A good strategic diagnosis **defines a domain of action**. A social scientist wants the explanation that best predicts; a strategist wants the explanation that promises **leverage** over outcomes. A factor that explains only part of the result but can be changed by policy is more useful than a bigger factor no one can touch.
- Diagnoses are usually expressed by **metaphor, analogy or a known framework** (containment of a spreading virus, 'we are a utility', 'this is a land grab').
- Most deep strategic change comes from a **change in diagnosis**. Rumelt's example: a computer giant everyone diagnosed as 'too integrated for a fragmented industry' (so break it up) was rediagnosed as 'the one firm with expertise across all of IT that fails to use it' (so integrate around customer solutions). Same facts, opposite directions.

## The guiding policy

- Channels action in certain directions without defining exactly what will be done: **guardrails on a highway**.
- It is not a goal, vision or desirable end state. It defines a **method of grappling with the situation** and rules out a vast array of possible actions.
  - 'Satisfy all our customers' financial needs and be the premier provider' is a vision. 'Use the network effects of cross-selling: more products per customer gives more knowledge, which creates and sells more products' is a guiding policy.
- Do not call the guiding policy alone 'the strategy'. Without a diagnosis you cannot evaluate alternative policies; without working through at least the first round of action you cannot know it is feasible. Strategy is **what, why and how**.
- A good guiding policy tackles the diagnosed obstacles by creating or drawing on **advantage**, which multiplies the effectiveness of resources like a lever. Not all advantage is competitive; in nonprofits and public policy it magnifies the effect of resources.
- The guiding policy itself creates advantage by:
  - anticipating the actions and reactions of others;
  - reducing complexity and ambiguity (resolving what to do, how to compete, how to organize);
  - concentrating effort on a pivotal or decisive aspect;
  - making policies and actions coherent, each building on the others.
- **Choosing a customer group is a classic guiding policy.** Rumelt's corner-grocer example: instead of facing hundreds of separate decisions (prices, organics, hours, checkouts, parking, ads, sales), she diagnosed competition with a 24/7 supermarket, saw a choice between price-sensitive students and time-pressed professionals, and chose 'the busy professional who has little time to cook'. That one choice organized the rest: a second checkout for the 5 p.m. rush, alley parking, prepared meals replacing snacks, no midnight hours, staffing at lunch and after work. A dichotomy is only useful if the segments need different policies.

## Coherent action

- Strategy is about doing something. The kernel must contain enough action to bring concepts down to earth, though not every future action.
- The main impediment to action is often the hope that painful choices can be avoided and the whole priority list achieved. **Nothing sharpens strategy like the necessity to act.**
- A useful prompt when a leader is stuck: 'Suppose this really were critical and had to succeed in eighteen months. What would you do?' The answer usually exists (a dedicated team with full P&L responsibility, budget overrides, consequences for blockers); the obstacle is the pain of doing it. Rumelt's example: a consumer goods firm's pan-regional product initiative stalled for years because it was assigned to country organizations whose job was running the country system ('using a shoe to hammer a nail').
- **Incoherent actions** come in two kinds:
  - **Conflicting**: an automaker bought distinctive premium brands for brand-led profits while also pushing them onto shared mass platforms for scale, diluting each brand's equity.
  - **Unrelated**: 'close a plant, spend more on advertising, start 360-degree feedback' may each be sound management, but they do not address one challenge together. They are 'strategic' only because executives approve them.
- Even a simple-looking advantage (low cost) rests on a raft of mutually supporting policies and applies only to certain products under certain conditions; exploiting it needs many aligned actions.

## Coordination is imposed, and costly

- Coherence is **imposed on a system by policy and design**, not ad hoc mutual adjustment. It is an exercise of central power to overcome the natural workings of a system.
- Decentralized decisions work well for many things, but fail when costs and benefits fall on different units or on present vs future, or when benefits arise only if decisions are coordinated (sales loves rush orders, manufacturing loves long runs; only a whole-company policy resolves it).
- Coordination fights **specialization** and interrupts people. Seek it only where gains are large. Good organization is specializing in the right activities and imposing **only the essential coordination**; connecting everything to everything produces frozen stasis.
- Two main tools for coherence: **design** (engineering fit among parts) and **proximate objectives** (a clear, feasible target that coordinates problem solving).

## Worked example: an HR-software company losing mid-market deals

- **Diagnosis**: Pipeline is healthy but win rate fell from 32% to 19% over a year. Loss interviews show the deciding moment has moved: buyers now shortlist after a free trial, and our product needs a two-week implementation before it shows value, while two new rivals show value in a day. This is a time-to-value problem, not a lead-generation problem.
- **Guiding policy**: Compete on 'value on day one' for 100–1,000-employee companies; stop trying to win demos with feature breadth.
- **Coherent actions**:
  1. Product: build a guided self-setup that imports the HRIS file and shows the first report within an hour (engineering re-prioritized for two quarters).
  2. Marketing: shift budget from top-of-funnel paid search to trial activation (in-app guidance, lifecycle email, a 'first-day value' benchmark page).
  3. Sales: reps run a 30-minute 'live import' call within 48 hours of trial signup; comp includes trial-to-paid conversion.
  4. Customer success: a 14-day activation playbook replaces the 6-week onboarding plan.
  5. Stop: the enterprise RFP team and the annual feature-launch event.
- Coherence check: each action reinforces time-to-value; nothing pulls toward enterprise breadth.

## Workflow

1. Gather facts: performance data, customer and loss research, competitor moves, internal constraints.
2. Ask 'what's going on here?' and write two or three candidate diagnoses as short stories; choose the one that offers most leverage, and record the rejected ones.
3. Make sure the diagnosis names a cause, not just a result (not 'growth slowed' but why).
4. Draft a guiding policy in one sentence; list what it rules out; check it draws on or creates an advantage.
5. Look for a choice that collapses many decisions (often a target customer group) and test whether the alternatives truly need different policies.
6. Specify the first round of coordinated actions with owners and resources; run the 'if this were critical, what would we do?' test.
7. Check coherence: remove conflicting actions and unrelated 'strategic' items; add only the coordination that pays for itself.
8. Present the kernel on one page, plus the 'stop doing' list.

## Red flags

- No diagnosis, or a diagnosis that only restates the target ('we need more revenue').
- A guiding policy that is really a vision or end state ('be the leading platform').
- A guiding policy with no actions, or a list of actions with no unifying policy.
- Actions that undercut each other (premium positioning with discount-led acquisition; brand distinctiveness on a shared generic template).
- A bundle of unrelated initiatives labelled 'strategy' because leadership approved them.
- Everything coordinated with everything; committees for every decision.
- A key initiative given to the part of the organization whose job and incentives work against it.
