# Marketing Planning and Management

Plan marketing as a chain: a clear goal, a strategy (target market plus value proposition), tactics that make the strategy real (the seven Ts), an implementation plan, and controls that tell you whether to stay the course, adjust or abandon. Use the 5-C framework to define the market around customer needs, and test every offering against the 3-V principle: it must create value for target customers, for collaborators and for the company, or the business model is not viable. Write the plan so it is actionable, relevant, clear and succinct, and keep revising it as the market moves.

## Three levels of planning

- **Corporate**: mission, culture, which businesses to start, grow, harvest or exit, and resource allocation across business units.
- **Business unit**: each unit's own mission and plan for a profitable future.
- **Offering**: a marketing plan for each product or service.

Market-oriented strategic planning keeps a viable fit between the organization's objectives, skills and resources and its changing market opportunities.

## Corporate and business-unit planning

### Mission

A mission is a clear, concise, enduring statement of why the organization exists (its core purpose). Develop it with managers, employees and often customers by answering Drucker's questions: What is our business? Who is the customer? What does the customer value? What will our business be? What should it be? Keep asking them as markets change.

Good missions:

- Focus on a limited number of specific goals (no laundry list).
- Stress the major policies and values, narrowing individual discretion on important issues.
- Define the major markets the company serves.
- Take a long-term view; change only when the mission stops being relevant.
- Are short, memorable and meaningful (a three- or four-word mantra beats a paragraph).

A business-unit mission is narrower and says what the unit will not do as much as what it will. Example: "Become the default analytics partner for mid-size e-commerce brands" excludes enterprise, agencies and non-analytics work.

### Culture

Corporate culture (shared experiences, stories, beliefs and norms) is far harder to change than structure or policy and is often the key to market success. A culture that treats employees well tends to produce employees who treat customers well; empowering frontline staff to fix problems is a visible expression of customer focus.

### Strategic business units (SBUs)

An SBU (1) is a single business or set of related businesses that can be planned separately, (2) has its own competitors, and (3) has a manager responsible for strategy and profit who controls most profit factors.

- **Specialized portfolio**: SBUs with one or a few narrow product lines.
- **Diversified portfolio**: broad assortments across many lines, usually to reach growth where the company is not yet present.
- Identify SBUs so each gets its own strategy and funding; every portfolio holds some of yesterday's has-beens and some of tomorrow's winners.

### Allocating resources across SBUs

- Assess each unit on **market attractiveness** (size, growth, profitability) and **competitive position / company resources** (strategic assets, core competencies, market share), plus synergies in processes and people.
- Decide per unit: **grow, hold or harvest** (draw cash). Units with different growth profiles may even be split into separate companies so each can pursue its own goals.
- Portfolio models (the BCG growth-share matrix is the classic, simple and subjective one) give generalized rules; newer methods also weigh growth from global expansion, repositioning or retargeting, and strategic outsourcing.

## The business model: strategy plus tactics

- **Strategy** = the target market in which the company will compete + the value it intends to create there.
- **Tactics** (the marketing mix) = the concrete attributes of the offering that make the strategy a market reality.
- Tactics follow from strategy. You cannot set features, price, messages or channels without knowing the target customers' needs and their alternatives.

### Target market: the 5-C framework

Picture concentric layers: target customers at the center; collaborators, competitors and the company around them; context on the outside.

- **Customers**: the people or organizations whose needs you will fulfil (end users in B2C, businesses in B2B). Choose them on two principles: you and your collaborators can create superior value for them versus competitors, and they can create value for you and your collaborators.
- **Collaborators**: entities that supply complementary resources you lack instead of you building them: suppliers, manufacturers, distributors, R&D partners, service providers, external sales forces, agencies, research firms.
- **Competitors**: anyone fulfilling the same need of the same customers, defined by need, not by industry label (avoid myopia). Ask what each competitor seeks and what drives its behavior (size, history, management, finances; is it run for growth, for profit, or being milked).
- **Company**: the business unit that develops and manages the offering, its resources and competencies.
- **Context**: sociocultural, technological, regulatory, economic and physical environment (including climate and health conditions).

Choosing target customers determines everything else: change the customer and you change the competitors, collaborators, required resources and relevant context.

**5 Cs versus Porter's Five Forces**: Five Forces (supplier power, buyer power, threat of entrants, threat of substitutes, rivalry) analyzes an industry's competitiveness. The 5-C view defines the market by customer need, so substitutes are simply cross-category competitors. Use Five Forces to assess industry structure; use 5 Cs to judge whether a specific offering can create market value.

### Value proposition: the 3-V principle

| Value | Question to answer |
|---|---|
| Customer value | What value does the offering create for target customers, and why would they choose it over the alternatives? (Depends on their needs, the offering's benefits and costs, and the alternatives' benefits and costs.) |
| Collaborator value | Why would suppliers, distributors and co-developers partner with us rather than someone else? |
| Company value | Why should the company invest in this offering rather than other opportunities, given its goals? |

The **optimal value proposition** balances all three: it creates value for customers and collaborators in a way that lets the company reach its goals. Failing any one makes the business model unsustainable. A value proposition is often paired with a positioning statement that names the key benefit in its competitive context.

## Tactics: the seven Ts

| Tactic | What it defines |
|---|---|
| Product | Features of the tangible or intangible good; purchase confers ownership |
| Service | Value delivered without ownership (support, repair, access, subscription); the same offering can be framed as product or service |
| Brand | Identifies and differentiates the offering and adds value beyond product and service (meaning, emotion, status) |
| Price | The monetary charge to customers and collaborators |
| Incentives | Targeted tools that raise benefits or cut costs: volume discounts, coupons, rebates, premiums, contests, loyalty rewards, trade incentives |
| Communication | Informs customers, collaborators and stakeholders about the offering and where to get it |
| Distribution | Channels that deliver the offering to customers and collaborators |

- Product, service, brand, price and incentives **design** value; communication **communicates** it; distribution **delivers** it.
- The customer experiences the same mix as **attractiveness** (benefits and costs), **awareness** (do I know about it) and **availability** (can I get it).

### Mapping the 7 Ts to the 4Ps

The 4Ps (product, price, promotion, place) are simple but blur key distinctions: they fold service and brand into product, and they lump incentives (which change value) together with communication (which informs about value). Mapping: Product = product + service + brand; Price = price; Promotion = incentives + communication; Place = distribution. Use the 7 Ts for design, and translate to 4Ps when stakeholders expect them.

## The market value map

A one-page view of the business model with three blocks and their key questions:

| Block | Element | Key question |
|---|---|---|
| Target market | Customers | What need, and who has it? |
| | Collaborators | Who will help fulfil the need? |
| | Company | What resources let us fulfil it? |
| | Competition | What else fulfils the same need for the same customers? |
| | Context | What sociocultural, technological, regulatory, economic and physical factors apply? |
| Value proposition | Customer / collaborator / company value | What value does the offering create for each? |
| Market offering | Product, service, brand, price, incentives, communication, distribution | What are the key features of each, and how will customers learn about and obtain it? |

Because the three entities need different propositions, consider separate customer, collaborator and company value maps.

## G-STIC action planning

**G**oal → **S**trategy → **T**actics → **I**mplementation → **C**ontrol.

### Goal

- **Focus** (what): monetary (net income, margin, EPS, ROI) or strategic (sales volume, awareness, social welfare, culture, recruiting). Strategic goals fit nonprofits and offerings that support a bigger profit engine (a device sold at break-even to feed a platform). Consider the triple bottom line: people, planet, profit.
- **Quantitative benchmark** (how much): relative (+5 points of share, +15 percent retention) or absolute (one million units a year).
- **Temporal benchmark** (by when). The time frame changes the strategy: next-quarter profit and long-term profitability call for different plans.
- Translate the ultimate goal into **market objectives**: the customer, collaborator, company, competitive and context changes that must happen for the goal to be met, each with its own focus and benchmarks.

### Strategy and tactics

As above: target market (5 Cs), value proposition (3 Vs), then the seven Ts.

### Implementation

- **Resource development**: secure competencies and assets (infrastructure, suppliers, skilled people, platform products and brands, channels, capital).
- **Offering development**: turn strategy and tactics into the actual offering (procurement, production, service specification, brand building, prices and incentives, message, media and creative, channel set-up).
- **Commercial deployment**: timing, resources and scale of launch. Either a selective launch in a **primary market** to read reaction, followed by a planned expansion sequence, or a broad rollout.

### Control

Controls tell you whether to stay the course, modify strategy and tactics, or abandon the course. Two parts:

- **Evaluate performance** against benchmarks. Common metrics:
  - Sales: volume, growth, market share.
  - Readiness to buy: awareness, preference, purchase intent, trial, repurchase.
  - Customer value: satisfaction, acquisition cost, churn, lifetime value, profitability, return per customer.
  - Distribution: outlets, stock, out-of-stocks, share of shelf, sales per channel.
  - Communication: brand awareness, gross rating points, response rate.
  A gap between desired and actual performance triggers a revision of the plan.
- **Monitor the environment** for opportunities (favorable regulation, weaker competition, rising demand) and threats (adverse rules, new competitors, falling demand) and adjust early.

## The written marketing plan

Narrower than a business plan (which also covers finance, operations, HR and technology). Its three jobs: describe the goal and course of action, inform stakeholders, and persuade decision makers it is viable.

Template:

```markdown
# Marketing plan: <offering>, <period>

## Executive summary
One or two pages: the issue (opportunity, threat or performance gap), the goal, the proposed action.

## Situation overview
- Company: history, culture, resources (competencies, assets), current offerings.
- Market: current and potential customers, collaborators, competitors, context; key opportunities and threats.

## Goal
- Primary goal: focus, quantitative benchmark, temporal benchmark.
- Market objectives: customer, collaborator, company, competitive and context objectives, each with focus and benchmarks.

## Strategy: target market
- Customers: needs to fulfil and profile of customers with those needs.
- Collaborators: suppliers, channel and communication partners, and their goals.
- Company: responsible unit, people, competencies, assets, product line, market position.
- Competitors: offerings giving similar benefits to the same customers and collaborators.
- Context: economic, technological, sociocultural, regulatory, physical.

## Strategy: value proposition
- Customer value proposition, positioning strategy and positioning statement.
- Collaborator value proposition and positioning.
- Company value proposition (for stakeholders and personnel).

## Tactics
Product, service, brand, price, incentives, communication, distribution.

## Implementation
Resource development, offering development, commercial deployment (primary market, timing, expansion).

## Control
- Performance evaluation: metrics and targets.
- Environment analysis: what is monitored and how the plan will be modified.

## Exhibits
Research data, financial analyses, offering specifics, implementation details.
```

Specialized plans (product development, service, brand, sales, promotion, communication, and within communication the advertising, PR and social plans; segment-specific plans) must align with the overall plan.

## Updating the plan

Update whenever the course of action changes: a revised goal, a new target market or value proposition, improved tactics, streamlined implementation or new controls. The commonest trigger is change in one of the 5 Cs:

- Customers: demographics, buying power, needs, preferences.
- Competitors: new entrant, price cuts, aggressive campaign, wider distribution.
- Collaborators: threat of backward integration, higher trade margins, retailer consolidation, private labels.
- Company: loss (or gain) of strategic assets and competencies.
- Context: recession, new technology, new regulation.

Firms that do not adapt their business model and plan get displaced by firms with better ones.

## The marketing audit

A comprehensive examination of an offering's or a department's marketing to find overlooked opportunities and problems and recommend action. It must be:

- **Comprehensive**: all major marketing activities, not just trouble spots (a functional audit of pricing or sales may misread causes; sales-force turnover may come from product, price or distribution).
- **Systematic**: follow G-STIC (goal audit, strategy audit, tactics audit, implementation audit, controls audit).
- **Unbiased**: preferably external; self-audits miss what an outsider sees.
- **Periodic**: run it when things are going well too, to catch problems early and spot growth opportunities.

The plan looks forward; the audit examines past and present performance to set the right future course.

## Worked example: G-STIC for a regional coffee-roaster brand entering grocery

- **Goal**: strategic focus on distribution-led volume; 120 grocery doors and 9,000 units per month within 12 months, at a contribution margin of at least 30 percent. Market objectives: 25 percent aided awareness among target shoppers in the region; listing at two regional chains; repeat rate of 35 percent.
- **Strategy, 5 Cs**: customers are home brewers who buy whole beans, care about freshness and buy weekly; collaborators are two regional chains and a distributor; company assets are a roastery with spare capacity and a cafe following; competitors are national premium brands and store brands; context includes rising green-coffee prices and packaging-waste rules.
- **3 Vs**: customers get roast-dated beans under 14 days old at a mid-premium price; retailers get a local brand with higher margin than national brands and in-store sampling support; the company uses spare capacity and builds a brand beyond its cafes.
- **7 Ts**: three core roasts (product); freshness rotation by the distributor (service); local-roaster brand with roast date as the hero cue (brand); price 15 percent below national premium (price); first-purchase coupon and retailer slotting support (incentives); in-store sampling, local social content and cafe cross-promotion (communication); two chains plus the e-shop (distribution).
- **Implementation**: packaging line and second roaster shift (resources); packaging, price list, sampling kits (offering); launch in 30 doors of one chain as the primary market, expand to the second chain in month 4 if repeat rate exceeds 30 percent (deployment).
- **Control**: weekly sell-through per door, out-of-stocks, repeat rate from loyalty-card data, awareness survey at months 3 and 9; monitor green-coffee prices and competitor price cuts, with a pre-agreed price-response rule.

## Workflow

1. Clarify the level: corporate (portfolio, mission), business unit, or a single offering.
2. For corporate questions, test the mission against the five traits and rate each SBU on market attractiveness and competitive position to recommend grow, hold or harvest.
3. Write the goal with focus, quantitative benchmark and temporal benchmark, then derive the market objectives.
4. Define the target market with the 5 Cs, starting from the customer need.
5. Draft customer, collaborator and company value propositions; reject the plan if any one is weak.
6. Specify the seven Ts so each one serves the strategy; map them to 4Ps if the audience expects it.
7. Lay out implementation (resources, offering development, deployment with a primary market and expansion triggers).
8. Define controls: metrics with targets, review cadence, environmental signals and the decision rules (stay, adjust, abandon).
9. Assemble the written plan from the template; keep supporting data in exhibits.
10. For an existing plan, run the marketing audit through G-STIC and list the revisions triggered by changes in the 5 Cs.

## Red flags

- A goal with no number or no deadline ("grow awareness"), or a number with no focus metric.
- Tactics chosen before the target customer and value proposition are defined (a campaign idea in search of a strategy).
- Competitors listed only from the company's own industry; substitutes ignored.
- A value proposition that works for customers but not for channel partners, or that the company cannot afford.
- Price promotions treated as "communication", or communication expected to add value it only describes.
- No implementation detail: no resources, no launch sequence, no primary market.
- No controls or no decision rule for what happens when metrics miss.
- A mission that is a list of everything the company does, or so generic it could belong to anyone.
- Every SBU or product funded equally regardless of attractiveness and position.
- A plan written once a year and never revisited after a competitor move or market shock.
- Audits that happen only in a crisis or are done by the managers being audited.
