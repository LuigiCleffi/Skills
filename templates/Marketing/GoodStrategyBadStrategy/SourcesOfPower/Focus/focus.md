# Focus

Focus means attacking a segment of the market with a business system that delivers more value to that segment than other players can. It has two meanings at once: **coordinated policies** whose interacting, overlapping effects create extra power, and the **application of that power to the right target**. To see focus, and to see any company's real strategy, you must do your own disciplined analysis rather than repeat the official story.

## Identify the strategy yourself

- A strategy is not necessarily what the CEO intended or what analysts and case writers say. Executives may hide it, misstate it, or lead without knowing why they succeed.
- Most people stop at the conventional description (Rumelt's example: a can maker 'specialized in hard-to-hold products'). It is true but neither complete nor useful.
- Analysing unstructured, qualitative information is hard, slow work requiring rich facts and skill in deduction and induction. That gap between casual and disciplined analysis is where insight lives.

### The method

1. **Start with the competitive environment**: how do the major competitors make their living? What are the industry's basic economics and structure?
2. **List the company's policies that differ from the industry norm.**
3. For each, ask **who or what it targets**: not every customer needs or benefits from it.
4. **Replace general nouns with specific examples** ('what kind of customer needs technical help?' becomes 'does a major brewer need help from a can supplier?').
5. **Do not grab the first reasonable answer**; test it against all the evidence. If it explains only some facts, keep going.
6. **Induce the common target** that ties the distinctive policies together, and connect it to the fundamental economics of the industry.
7. Test the insight against further policies, then work out **how it produces profit**.

## Rumelt's can-maker analysis in brief

- Industry structure was terrible: big suppliers built dedicated plants for giant buyers who dual-sourced and could self-manufacture. The majors accepted being captive for the sake of long production runs (line changeovers are costly) and earned low returns.
- The smaller firm's distinctive policies: heavy technical assistance (targets smaller customers without canning expertise), rapid response (targets rush orders: seasonal spikes, new products), smaller plants each serving several customers with spare lines waiting (targets speed and lower volume per customer), and higher prices.
- The common target: **shorter runs**, the very thing the industry's economics made the majors avoid.
- Its unit cost was higher, not lower (more changeovers, spare capacity, service), but prices were higher too.
- The profit mechanism: no plant was captive, so instead of one buyer squeezing several suppliers, one supplier served many small buyers. Focus **raised bargaining power** and let the firm capture more of the value it created. Not the biggest, but the most profitable.
- Lesson: the 'low-cost producer' explanation offered first was simply wrong. Focus does not automatically mean profit; you must find the mechanism.

## Principles for marketers

- **Find what the majors' economics make them avoid.** Large players optimized for scale often cannot serve small, urgent, custom, new or volatile demand profitably. A business system built for that demand can win it.
- **Higher cost can be fine if value and power are higher.** Do not assume the profitable niche player is the low-cost one.
- **Watch buyer power.** Selling to a few giant customers who can dual-source or insource leaves you captive. Serving many customers per unit of capacity preserves pricing power.
- **'Good things' are targeted things.** Great service, technical support and speed are not generic virtues; they serve particular customers. Name them.
- **Most large companies do not have a focus.** They pursue several goals at once without concentrating enough resources for a breakthrough in any.

## Worked example: a specialist creative-production agency

A 60-person agency outearns much larger holding-company agencies in margin. The official story: 'great creative and great service'.

- Distinctive policies: 48-hour turnaround guarantee; senior producers on every account; flat project pricing; no retainers; a bench of freelancers on standby; clients are mostly in-house brand teams at mid-size consumer companies.
- Targets: urgent requests (launch slips, social moments), clients without large internal production teams, work too small for big agencies' minimum fees.
- Common target: **fast, small, unplanned production jobs**, which large agencies avoid because their staffing and retainer economics need large, planned scopes.
- Cost per hour is higher (standby capacity, senior staff), but project prices are higher too and no client exceeds 8% of revenue, so no client can squeeze it.
- Implication for its marketing: message 'when it has to be out this week', target in-house teams at brands with frequent launches, and do not chase big retainer pitches, which would make it captive.

## Workflow

1. Describe the industry: main competitors, how they make money, cost structure, buyer concentration.
2. Write down the official explanation of the target company's strategy, then set it aside.
3. Collect the company's policies from all sources (pricing, channels, plant or service setup, customer mix, product range, service levels, hiring).
4. Mark which policies differ from the industry norm; for each, name the concrete customer or situation it serves.
5. Induce the common target; test it against every remaining fact; revise until it explains them.
6. Explain the profit mechanism: unit cost, price, bargaining power, value capture.
7. For the user's own business, design or sharpen a focus: a segment the majors' economics make them neglect, and a coordinated set of policies to serve it.

## Red flags

- Repeating a company's self-description or an analyst summary as its strategy.
- Explaining a rival's success with 'low cost' or 'great service' without evidence of the mechanism.
- Treating service, speed or support as universal goods rather than targeted policies.
- Pursuing the largest buyers whose power will make you captive.
- A 'niche' that is just a smaller version of the mass market with the same policies.
- A large company claiming focus while spreading resources across many goals.
