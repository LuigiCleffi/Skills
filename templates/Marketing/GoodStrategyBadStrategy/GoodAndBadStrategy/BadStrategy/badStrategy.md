# Bad Strategy

Bad strategy is not merely the absence of good strategy or a strategy that failed. It is a recognizable way of thinking and writing: long on goals, short on policy and action, hiding its emptiness behind high-sounding language. Learn its four hallmarks and you can judge, influence and repair strategy far more effectively.

## The four hallmarks

1. **Fluff**: gibberish masquerading as strategic thought. Inflated 'Sunday words' and esoteric-sounding concepts create the illusion of high-level thinking.
2. **Failure to face the challenge**: the plan does not recognize or define the obstacle. Without a defined challenge you cannot evaluate the strategy or improve it.
3. **Mistaking goals for strategy**: statements of desire instead of plans for overcoming obstacles.
4. **Bad strategic objectives**: objectives that fail to address the critical issues or are impracticable (dog's dinner and blue-sky).

## Fluff

- Superficial restatement of the obvious plus buzzwords. Strip the words and see what is left: Rumelt's example of a bank whose 'fundamental strategy' turned out to mean 'being a bank'.
- Labels are not strategies. 'Be an information provider', 'platform play', 'omnichannel leader' are names of businesses, like butcher or baker, not ways of winning.
- Elaborate diagrams of how markets 'evolve' can be a stew of half-truths that avoid the real question: can we actually do this here, given the specific economics?
- Test: true expertise makes a complex subject understandable; mediocrity adds unnecessary complexity.

Marketing fluff to catch: 'customer-centric omnichannel engagement', 'leveraging synergies to drive best-in-class experiences', 'a holistic, data-driven, AI-first growth engine'. Rewrite each into what the company will concretely do differently, for whom, and why that wins.

## Failure to face the challenge

- A strategy is a way through a difficulty. If the difficulty is not named, the document is a stretch goal, a budget or a wish list.
- Rumelt's example: a corporate plan that aggregated divisional plans to gain share and cut costs, with a hockey-stick profit chart, while never mentioning the real obstacle: a grossly inefficient work organization and terrible labour relations. You could not find the 'elephant' by reading the plan, because the plan did not mention it.
- Modern template planning (vision, mission, values, strategic goals, 'strategies' per goal, initiatives) usually repeats the same failure in nicer words.
- Counter-example: a research agency that defines its niche precisely (problems too hard and too risky for others) and then sets concrete policies tied to the obstacles to innovation (short tenures for programme managers to stop empire building, minimal fixed facilities to prevent entrenched interests). Concrete policies follow from a named challenge; 'retain the best talent' does not.

## Mistaking goals for strategy

- 'Grow 20% a year at a 20% margin' plus 'be the firm of choice' and 'delight customers' is a set of performance goals, however much buy-in it has.
- Ask **'What has to happen?'** A strategy is like a lever: it needs a point of leverage, a key strength to build on or a change in the industry that opens a window. If the answer is 'will, push and motivation', there is no strategy.
- Motivation matters, and a leader can ask for one last push, but the leader's job is to create the conditions that make the push effective. Rumelt draws the parallel with First World War offensives where willpower was flung at machine guns.
- Most corporate 'strategic plans' are three- to five-year rolling budgets with share projections. Resource planning is valuable (it makes sure resources arrive when needed) but it is not strategy and cannot deliver a path to much higher performance.
- Strategy work is **episodic**, driven by opportunities and threats, not by the annual calendar.
- The remedy Rumelt gives an ambitious client: form a small team, take a month to study buyers, competitors and what is changing, look for internal bottlenecks and external openings, and end with a very short list of the most important things to do.

## Goals versus objectives

- Use **goal** for overall values and desires (profit, being a great place to work, being known for X). Goals constrain choices, like rules of a game, but do not imply action.
- Use **objective** for specific operational targets that the strategy sets (win one national account, halve response time, land five Fortune 500 clients).
- Strategy is the bridge that turns vague goals into a coherent set of actionable objectives. Senior leaders design objectives; they do not just chase targets handed to them.
- Rumelt's example of a specialty food distributor whose retail customers were being squeezed: its goals stayed the same, but its objectives changed completely, from tiered penetration targets to one cross-functional team focused on landing one distinctive product line with one national chain. Once achieved, new objectives would follow.

## Bad strategic objectives

### Dog's dinner objectives

- A long list of things to do, mislabelled as strategies or objectives, collected from every stakeholder in a planning meeting. Often tagged 'long-term' so none must be done now.
- Rumelt cites public plans with dozens of 'strategies' and hundreds of action items, one of which was to create a strategic plan.
- Good strategy focuses energy on one or very few **pivotal objectives** whose accomplishment cascades into favourable outcomes.

### Blue-sky objectives

- A restatement of the desired state or of the challenge itself, skipping the fact that nobody knows how to get there ('build transformational leadership', 'create an empowered community', 'become the category-defining brand').
- Objectives should stand a good chance of being achieved with existing resources and competence (see proximate objectives). If an objective is as hard as the original challenge, the strategy added nothing.
- **Underperformance is a result, not a challenge.** Defining the challenge as 'low scores', 'missed pipeline' or 'falling share' sets up bad strategy. The real challenge is the reason for the underperformance; offer a theory of why things have not worked.
- Watch for metrics that can be gamed in ways that dodge the real problem (improving a score by excluding the hardest cases).

## Worked example: a consumer skincare brand's annual plan

Found: 'Our strategy: be the most loved clean-beauty brand; grow revenue 35%; launch in 4 countries; achieve 1M followers; build a culture of innovation; 26 initiatives across brand, retail, DTC, influencer, product.'

Audit:

- Fluff: 'most loved clean-beauty brand' is a goal and a label.
- No challenge: repeat purchase is 18% versus 35% for peers (that is a result). Cause, after interviews: the hero serum takes six weeks to show results while customers judge it at three.
- Goals as strategy: 35% growth, 1M followers.
- Dog's dinner: 26 initiatives, four country launches.
- Rewrite: focus the year on the 'first six weeks' problem: a 3-week starter size priced to encourage a second purchase, a week-by-week results guide and reminder sequence, subscriptions offered at day 21, influencer briefs that show week-6 results. Defer international expansion.

## Workflow

1. Collect the strategy artefacts (deck, plan, OKRs, positioning statement, campaign brief).
2. Highlight every buzzword phrase; rewrite each in plain words; flag those that collapse into the obvious.
3. Find the stated challenge. If missing, or if it is only a result (low growth, low share), ask why it is happening and gather evidence.
4. Separate goals, objectives and actions; list which items are only desires or targets.
5. Ask 'what has to happen?' and look for the point of leverage. If none, say so plainly.
6. Count the objectives; mark the one to three pivotal ones and move the rest to a parking list.
7. Test each remaining objective for feasibility with current resources; replace blue-sky objectives with proximate ones.
8. Deliver the audit table and a rewritten kernel (diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions).

## Red flags

- Strategy statements that would fit any company in the category.
- A profit or pipeline forecast shaped like a hockey stick with no explanation of what changes.
- 'Key strategies' that are values, targets or slogans.
- More than a handful of strategic objectives, or hundreds of action items.
- 'Long-term' attached to items so they need not be started.
- Objectives such as 'transform culture', 'become the leader', 'build an empowered community'.
- The challenge stated as missed numbers rather than their causes.
- A strategy process that runs only on the budget calendar.
