# Managing Retailing

Retailers and wholesalers are marketers in their own right: they must choose a target market and build a consistent offer (assortment, service, atmosphere, price, incentives, communications) that serves it better than other formats and channels. Start every retail decision from a clearly profiled target shopper, make each lever reinforce the same positioning, and treat online and physical touchpoints as one system. For manufacturers, understand the retailer's logic before asking for shelf space, and decide whether to fight or partner with the retailer's own brands.

## What retailing is

**Retailing** is every activity involved in selling goods or services directly to final consumers for personal, nonbusiness use. Whoever does it (manufacturer, wholesaler or retailer), and however (store, mail, phone, vending, online, in the home), it is retailing. A **retailer** is a business whose sales come mainly from retailing.

## The modern retail environment

- **New forms and combinations**: hybrid formats (bookstore plus café, grocery plus bar or gym), carts and kiosks, **pop-up stores** that create buzz in busy areas for a few weeks, online giants opening physical stores.
- **Retailer consolidation**: giant chains with superior information, logistics and buying power dictate terms to suppliers (what to make, price, promotion, shipping) and charge listing, slotting and promotion allowances.
- **Mobile retailing**: shoppers use phones in stores to ask friends, check reviews and compare prices; in some markets mobile is the main shopping device.
- **Omnichannel retailing**: from separate physical and online stores that cannibalize each other to an integrated model where channels complement one another (check store stock online, ship to store or home, return anywhere).
- **Fast retailing**: supply chains built for constantly changing assortments; requires coordinated product development, sourcing, manufacturing, inventory and selling.
- **Technology**: forecasting, inventory and ordering systems that reduce forced markdowns; electronic shelf labels, in-store screens, QR codes, cashier-less stores; integrated web, email, search and social.
- **Decline of the middle market**: an hourglass market, with growth at the luxury top and the discount bottom; mid-market chains get squeezed as discounters improve quality and image.

## Key retailing decisions

### Target market

Nothing else can be decided consistently (assortment, décor, messages, media, price, service) until the target is defined and profiled. Repositioning to a different target is risky: Kotler cites a mass-market jeweler that chased upscale buyers, lost its base and failed to win the new one. Retailers increasingly slice markets finer and launch separate store lines for niches.

### Product assortment and procurement

- Match the target's expectations on **breadth** (how many categories) and **depth** (how many options per category): narrow-shallow, narrow-deep, broad-shallow, broad-deep.
- Build a **product-differentiation strategy**; give special attention to **destination categories**, the ones that most influence where households choose to shop and how they see the store (e.g. the best produce in town).
- Set merchandise sources, policies and practices; buyers (merchandise managers) build brand assortments and hear supplier pitches.
- Use data for forecasting, selection, stock control, space allocation and display (scanner data per store, RFID tracking).
- Measure **direct product profitability (DPP)**: all handling costs from warehouse receipt to sale. Gross margin can bear little relation to DPP; some high-volume items deserve less space unless they pull profitable baskets.

### Services

Three service levels:

- **Self-service**: the shopper does the locate-compare-select process to save money (discounters).
- **Limited service**: more shopping goods, plus credit and returns; help on request.
- **Full service**: staff assist at every step; high cost, more specialty and slow-moving goods.

Then choose the services mix: **pre-purchase** (information, trial and experience) and **post-purchase** (shipping, delivery, installation, gift wrap, alterations, adjustments, returns).

### Store atmosphere and experience

- Design for all the senses: music tempo, scent by department, lighting, layout.
- Match the atmosphere to shopper motivation: task-focused, functional shoppers want a simpler, restrained environment; experiential categories benefit from in-store entertainment and trial (climbing walls, test ranges, simulated conditions, classes).
- Physical stores' natural advantages over online: see, touch and test; real people; no delivery lag; and experience as a differentiator. Kotler uses outdoor-sports superstores that function as tourist attractions to show the extreme.

### Pricing

- Set prices relative to target, assortment and competition; formats compete mostly within their own type.
- **Turns versus earns**: everyone wants high volume and high gross margin, but they rarely coexist. Choose high-markup/lower-volume (specialty) or low-markup/high-volume (mass and discount).
- Tactics: **traffic builders (loss leaders)** to pull visits or signal price policy; planned markdowns on slow movers (expect only part of stock to sell at full markup).
- **Price image**, the shopper's overall categorical sense of whether a store is cheap or expensive, matters as much as actual prices because shoppers do not know most prices. Drivers:
  - **Average price level**: if prices are well above rivals, nothing else fixes the image.
  - **Known-value (signpost) items**: frequently bought items shoppers can compare; price them sharply.
  - **Price range**: a few extreme high-priced items raise the image; a few extreme lows lower it.
  - **Sales promotions**: high-low can create a lower price image than EDLP at the same average level; no consistent evidence that EDLP signals lower prices.
  - **Price communication**: messages about savings (reference prices, amount saved) lower the image but also raise price sensitivity.
  - **Physical attributes**: prime locations and lavish décor signal high costs; big boxes and big parking lots signal volume.
  - **Service level**: more service reads as higher prices.
  - **Store policies**: price-match guarantees lower the image; very generous returns can raise it.
- Price image affects price evaluation, perceived fairness, store choice, purchase deferral, quantity and product choice. Align all drivers with strategy; do not try to fix image by cutting prices alone.

### Incentives

Sales promotions (discounts, BOGO, bonuses, coupons) build traffic and nudge purchase. Two poles:

| | **EDLP (everyday low pricing)** | **High-low** |
|---|---|---|
| How | Constant low price, few or no promotions | Higher everyday price, frequent temporary deep discounts |
| Strengths | Price stability, image of fairness and reliability, lower promotion costs, often higher profit with large-basket shoppers | Excitement, traffic, sense of a deal; works for image-oriented and fashion categories |
| Risks | Can remove the thrill shoppers expect; fails if rivals keep running sales | Promotion costs; trains shoppers to wait for deals and distrust shelf prices |

Kotler uses a department store's abrupt switch from constant sales to flat everyday pricing, which collapsed sales, to show that EDLP suits functional products and large baskets better than fashion, and that shoppers often want a deal, not merely a fair price.

Mobile tools: mobile coupons redeem far better than paper and increase in-store travel and unplanned purchases; in-store apps and Wi-Fi blend online information with physical trial; **geofencing** triggers offers or alerts staff when opted-in customers are near or in a store. Cap message frequency (e.g. a few per month) and get explicit opt-in.

### Communications

- Tools: ads, sales events, coupons, email (timed, personalized, tested down to which words and images get clicks), social communities, sampling, shelf and checkout coupons, co-designed point-of-sale materials.
- **Loyalty programs**: a small share of loyal customers can drive a large share of sales; offer discounts, early or private sales, exclusives and credits in exchange for data that customers choose to share.
- **Shopper marketing**: because many decisions are made in-store, manufacturers and retailers jointly use stocking, displays and promotions to influence active shoppers. The store encounter is the "first moment of truth" (a phrase associated with P&G); use is the second. Example moves: consolidating a category into one destination aisle; kits tied to a trend plus social how-to content.
- In-store tech: virtual mirrors, immersive screens, smart carts, smart shelves.

## Store formats and locations

Brick-and-mortar types:

- **Department stores** (several lines), **specialty stores** (one or a few related lines), **supermarkets** (large, low-cost, low-margin, high-volume self-service), **convenience stores** (small, long hours, high-turnover items), **drugstores**, **mass merchandisers**, **category killers** (narrow, deep), **extreme value / hard discounters** (very limited mix, heavy discounts), **off-price retailers and factory outlets** (overruns, irregulars), **warehouse clubs** (bulk at low prices), **automatic vending**.

Location options: central business districts, regional shopping centers (large malls with anchors), community shopping centers, shopping strips, a location within a larger store (concessions, store-within-a-store), stand-alone stores. Choose region, then city, then site, trading traffic against rent, using traffic counts, shopping-habit surveys and competitor-location analysis.

## Online and omnichannel retailing

Three types: **brick-and-mortar**, **online (pure-click)**, **omnichannel (brick-and-click)**, including catalog, telemarketing and TV-shopping firms that added e-commerce.

Online retailers:

- Save floor space, staff and inventory, so they can serve niches profitably; personalize at scale.
- Compete not only on price but on assortment, convenience, experience, delivery speed, returns and problem resolution.
- Main inhibitors for shoppers: no pleasurable experience, no social interaction, no personal consultation; plus security and privacy.
- Drive traffic with **affiliate marketing** (paying content providers for referrals). Flash-sale sites let brands move excess stock in a controlled, brand-safe way; Kotler's flash-sale example shows the risk of over-expanding beyond the core category.

B2B e-commerce: supplier sites, **infomediaries** (aggregate information on alternatives), **market makers** (link buyers and sellers: auctions, spot exchanges, catalogs) and **customer communities** make prices transparent. Undifferentiated suppliers face price pressure and must cut costs; differentiated suppliers should answer price transparency with **value transparency**.

Omnichannel moves: ship-from-store (stores as mini-warehouses), buy online pick up or return in store, an app that switches to "store mode" (lists, aisle location, circulars), consistent information across channels, and third-party delivery partners for smaller retailers. Manufacturers also open direct e-stores partly to observe shopping behavior.

## Private labels

A **private label** (reseller, store or house brand) is a brand a retailer or wholesaler develops. Distinguish from **generics**: unbranded, plainly packaged, cheaper versions of common products with standard or lower quality.

Why retailers build them:

- Higher margins (low-cost production on excess capacity; little R&D, advertising, promotion or distribution cost).
- Differentiation through exclusive products.
- Appeal to price-sensitive shoppers; more bargaining power with national brands.
- Increasingly premium: better quality, attractive packaging, story-led "good, better, best" tiers. Kotler cites a Canadian grocer whose premium store line became strong enough to license abroad.

Why they are gaining: price-sensitive shoppers trained by constant promotions, quality copying that erodes physical differentiation, cut communication budgets that erode brand image, and line-extension clutter. Recessions boost store brands and some switchers never return; there is likely a ceiling because shoppers still want certain national brands and some categories do not suit store brands.

How manufacturers respond (four strategies):

1. **Fight selectively** where you can win and add value for consumers, retailers and shareholders, typically as the number one or two brand or in a premium niche; prune weaker brands.
2. **Partner effectively**: complement the retailer's labels, e.g. retailer-exclusive brands, or use hard discounters to reach new buyers.
3. **Innovate brilliantly**: a steady flow of incremental news plus periodic radical innovation, with intellectual property protected.
4. **Create winning value propositions**: symbolic imagery plus functional quality that beats the store brand, with a price premium that perceived benefits justify.

Also: invest in pull advertising and seek joint logistics savings with major retailers. Strong consumer demand is the ultimate protection; when a dominant retailer delisted two national brands, shopper complaints and defections brought them back.

## Wholesaling

**Wholesaling** is selling goods or services to those who buy for resale or for business use. Wholesalers differ from retailers: less attention to promotion, atmosphere and location; larger transactions and trade areas; different laws and taxes.

Types:

- **Merchant wholesalers** take title. **Full-service** ones add a sales force, credit, delivery and management help; **limited-service** ones (e.g. cash-and-carry) offer few services and lower prices.
- **Brokers and agents** do not take title. Brokers bring buyers and sellers together and are paid by whoever hires them; agents represent a buyer or seller on a more permanent basis, on commission.

Functions wholesalers can perform more efficiently than manufacturers:

- Access to many small retailers (more contacts, more trust).
- Buying and assortment building.
- Bulk breaking.
- Warehousing.
- Transportation (closer to buyers).
- Financing customers (credit) and suppliers (early orders, prompt payment).
- Risk bearing (title, theft, damage, spoilage, obsolescence).
- Market research and competitive information.
- Management services and consulting (training, layouts, accounting and inventory systems, technical services).

Pressures: digital platforms, demanding customers, direct-buying programs and vertical integration by manufacturers. Manufacturers complain that wholesalers act as order takers, carry too little stock, share too little market information and overcharge. Winning wholesalers add value with services (financing, on-site inventory management, tracking software, technical services), raise asset productivity (inventory and receivables), cut operating cost with technology, and make sharper choices on target, assortment, services, price, communication and distribution.

## Worked example: price image for a regional pet-supply chain

The chain's prices are on average at par with a big-box rival, but shoppers think it is expensive.

| Driver | Diagnosis | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Known-value items | Top dog and cat foods priced 4 percent above rival | Match on the 30 signpost SKUs; recover margin on accessories and private label |
| Price range | Premium aquariums placed at entrance | Move high-ticket items deeper; feature value bundles at entry |
| Promotions | Rare, shallow discounts | A monthly high-low event on one destination category |
| Communication | No savings messaging | Shelf tags showing loyalty savings; receipts that show total saved |
| Policy | None visible | Publish a price-match promise on known-value items |
| Service | Grooming and advice are a strength | Keep, but present as included value, not a premium |

Track: perceived price image (survey), basket size, visit frequency, margin mix.

## Workflow

1. Profile the target shopper and their shopping goals; confirm the retailer's position in the hourglass (premium, value, or a defensible niche in the middle).
2. Audit each lever (assortment, service, atmosphere, price, incentives, communications, channels) against the target; flag inconsistencies.
3. Define category roles, especially destination categories, and check DPP before allocating space.
4. Map price-image drivers and set the known-value item list; choose EDLP, high-low or a hybrid based on category type and basket size.
5. Design the omnichannel journey: stock visibility, fulfillment from stores, pickup and returns, app in-store mode, consistent content.
6. Decide private-label strategy (retailer) or the fight/partner/innovate/value response (manufacturer).
7. For a manufacturer pitching retail: bring evidence of consumer demand, a pull-marketing plan, a shopper-marketing proposal and incentives.
8. For a wholesaler: list the functions you perform better than direct sourcing, price them, and add services that defend against disintermediation.
9. Set KPIs and test changes store-by-store before chain-wide rollout.

## Red flags

- Assortment, décor and pricing decided before the target shopper is defined.
- Abrupt repositioning to a new target that abandons the existing base.
- Cutting prices across the board to fix a price image while known-value items stay uncompetitive.
- Switching wholesale from high-low to EDLP in a fashion or image category without testing.
- Allocating shelf space by gross margin or volume only, ignoring handling cost.
- Online and store teams with separate inventory, prices and returns; the website cannibalizing stores instead of feeding them.
- Geofenced or loyalty messaging without explicit opt-in or with no frequency cap.
- A national brand competing with a store brand on functional parity and a premium it cannot justify.
- A wholesaler that only takes orders and cannot say which functions it performs better.
- Loyalty programs that collect data but return no visible value.
