# Tapping into Global Markets

Going abroad multiplies both opportunity and risk. Decide deliberately, in sequence: whether to go, which markets, how to enter, how much to adapt the marketing program, and how to organize. Prefer fewer markets entered deeply where attractiveness is high, risk is low and you hold an advantage; choose an entry mode whose commitment and control match what you know about the market; and adapt every element that adds more revenue than it costs, while keeping the brand's core consistent. The guiding phrase is "think global, act local".

## Deciding whether to go abroad

Reasons to go:

- Better profit opportunities than at home.
- A larger customer base for economies of scale.
- Less dependence on one market.
- Counterattacking global competitors in their home markets.
- Serving customers who themselves operate internationally.
- Transferring ideas and products between markets (products built for one region can succeed with diaspora segments elsewhere).

Two kinds of risk:

- **General new-market risks** (domestic or foreign): misreading customer needs, missing competitive threats, failing to build supply and distribution, ineffective or costly promotion.
- **Country-specific risks**: misreading business culture and regulation, lacking managers with international experience, and commercial and political disruption (tariffs, currency swings, government change, even expropriation).

Watch the home base: aggressive international expansion often starves the domestic business of attention (Kotler uses a UK grocer whose costly US format failed while its home stores deteriorated). Also expect differences in shopping habits, the need for social acceptance, and missing communication or distribution infrastructure.

## The five major decisions

1. Whether to go abroad.
2. Which markets to enter.
3. How to enter.
4. The marketing program (product, brand, price, communication, distribution).
5. The marketing organization.

## Deciding which markets to enter

First set objectives: the target ratio of international to total sales, and whether you plan to stay small or grow big.

### How many, how fast

| Approach | What it is | Use when | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Waterfall** | Enter countries one after another | You want careful planning without straining people and money | Slower; rivals may preempt |
| **Sprinkler** | Enter many countries at once | First-mover advantage is crucial and competition intense | Heavy resources; hard to plan for diverse markets |
| **Born global** | Market to the world from the outset | Technology-intensive and online ventures | Thin local adaptation |

### Evaluating potential markets

- **Product fit, geography, income, population, political climate.**
- **Competitors**: entering markets where rivals already are can force them to defend and lets you learn from their approach.
- **Market growth**: a toehold in a fast-growing market can be worth it even if it will crowd; Kotler cites a fried-chicken chain that pioneered many countries through franchising and culturally relevant menus and mascots, while still facing supply-chain and food-safety crises.
- **Physical proximity**: neighbors are better understood and cheaper to serve.
- **Cultural proximity**: familiar language, law and culture lower risk, but can lead you to overlook bigger markets and to underestimate real differences.
- **Attractiveness, risk and advantage**: prefer high attractiveness, low risk and a real competitive advantage; operating in fewer countries with deeper commitment often beats spreading thin.
- **Underserved ('invisible') consumers**: pioneers reach them with new models, e.g. village agents leasing phone time per call, video vans demonstrating hygiene benefits, modular expandable low-income housing.
- **Collaborators**: strong retailer, influencer and media partners can make a launch (Kotler cites a shampoo launch in Brazil that combined dozens of retail partners, bloggers, mass sampling and a single-day online blitz).

### Emerging-market tactics

- Smaller packs and lower price points where income and storage are limited (single-use sachets).
- Distribution through tiny 'high-frequency' stores, stalls and neighborhood shops that offer convenience, credit and delivery.
- Do not run 'business as usual': infrastructure may be thin and local competitors strong.
- **Reverse innovation**: products and lessons from developing markets increasingly flow back to developed ones; design platforms that customize for both (Kotler's example is a tractor line designed for farmers in about 130 countries).

## Deciding how to enter

Five modes, in rising **commitment, risk, control and profit potential**:

```
Indirect exporting -> Direct exporting -> Licensing -> Joint venture -> Direct investment
```

Typical evolution: independent agent in a nearby or similar country, then an export department, then own sales subsidiaries in big markets, then an international division, then local production where markets are large and stable or local production is required, ending as a multinational optimizing sourcing, finance, manufacturing and marketing globally.

### Indirect and direct exporting

- **Indirect exporting** through independent intermediaries: domestic-based **export merchants** (buy and resell abroad), **export agents** and trading companies (negotiate foreign purchases on commission), **cooperative organizations** (export for several producers, often of primary products), **export-management companies** (manage exports for a fee). Benefits: low investment and lower risk, since intermediaries bring know-how.
- **Direct exporting**: a domestic export department (which may become a profit center), an overseas sales branch or subsidiary (sales, distribution, sometimes warehousing, promotion and service), or traveling export reps. More investment and risk, more return.
- Exporting is a way to test the waters before building plants. The web lets firms attract and support foreign customers, source abroad and build awareness without trade shows; localize the site with country-specific content and language for the highest-potential markets; use public export-promotion resources.

### Licensing and its variants

- **Licensing**: a foreign firm uses your process, trademark, patent or trade secret for a fee or royalty. Low-risk entry; the licensee gains expertise or a famous brand. Risks: less control, forgone profit if the licensee thrives, and creating a future competitor. Protect yourself by supplying a proprietary ingredient or component, and by staying ahead in innovation so the licensee keeps depending on you.
- **Management contracts**: run foreign-owned businesses (e.g. hotels) for a fee, sometimes with an option to buy equity.
- **Contract manufacturing**: local manufacturers produce for you; faster start, less control and some lost profit, with the option to partner or buy later.
- **Franchising**: a complete brand concept and operating system for fees; common in quick service, retail, car rental and hotels.

### Joint ventures and alliances

Share ownership and control with local investors, often because of missing resources or legal requirements (domestic content, mandatory local ownership). Global strategic networks (e.g. airline alliances) can be the real competitive unit. Good partners share brand values, which keeps standards consistent across markets; choose them carefully. Drawbacks: disagreements on investment, marketing or dividends, and constraints on running worldwide policies. Open platforms that let outside developers build on your network are another partnering route.

### Direct investment and acquisition

Buy part or all of a local company or build your own facilities. Advantages: cost economies (labor, materials, incentives, freight), a better image as a local employer, deeper relationships with government, customers, suppliers and distributors that help adaptation, full control, and secure access where domestic-content rules apply. Disadvantages: large exposure to currency blocks or devaluation, market decline, expropriation and costly exit (severance), and the risk that you still fail to adapt (Kotler cites a coffee chain whose sweeter, pricier drinks did not suit a coffee culture with strong espresso traditions).

**Acquiring local brands** taps local sentiment that imported brands struggle to reach (Kotler describes a brewer that built a portfolio of 'local jewels' and invested in anthropological and segmentation research per market, versus a rival pushing one global brand). Acquisition can also bring technology and know-how, or a platform to upgrade (a car maker that turned an acquired brand from a joke into a global value option). Downsides: the purchase cost, culture clashes, and a long-term commitment.

## Deciding on the global marketing program

**Standardized** programs bring scale economies in production and distribution, lower marketing costs, consistent brand image, reuse of good ideas, and uniform practices. They ignore differences in needs and usage, responses to marketing, competition, and legal, cultural and political context. Most firms land in between: a consistent global positioning with local formulation and execution. Kotler's cookie example: one global positioning, a brand book specifying what must stay common, what may change and what may not, local flavors and less sweetness where tastes demanded, local celebrities in ads, and successful local flavors exported to other markets.

### Product strategies

- **Straight extension**: no change. Cheapest (no R&D, retooling or promotional change); works for cameras, electronics, machine tools and many luxury goods. Fails when knowledge, preferences or usage differ: products requiring preparation customers do not understand, packs too big for local fridges, appliances too big for local kitchens or hands, positioning for an occasion that does not exist locally, products needing appliances few homes have (ovens, toasters), or benefits that do not apply (a floor polish too slippery for homes where people go shoeless).
- **Positioning differently** without changing the product: the same beer can be super-premium abroad and mid-market at home; the same car can be middle-class in one country and premium in another.
- Build **portfolios for income tiers** in emerging markets (premium health brands alongside very low-priced lines).
- **Product adaptation**: change features, labeling, colors, ingredients, packaging, brand name, sales promotion, price, and advertising message, media and execution wherever the change adds more revenue than cost. Levels: **regional**, **country**, **city** and **retailer** versions. Treat the host culture's customs as a design input (Kotler's example is a theme park near Paris that struggled until it added local touches such as wine with meals).
- **Product invention**: create new products for global markets (low-cost high-protein foods; local menu items developed by each country).

### Counterfeits

Global supply chains raise risks of fraud, corruption and quality problems; virtually every successful product is copied, and online counterfeit sales are large. Defenses: automated and AI monitoring that spots copycat ads, unauthorized storefronts using your marks, suspicious keywords ('cheap', 'factory', 'authentic'), colors you never made, and implausibly low prices, plus legal action against platforms and sellers.

### Brand strategies

- **Brand adaptation**: choose between phonetic and semantic translation of names (ideally both: characters that sound like the name and carry a positive meaning). Check slang in each language before launch. Numbers and colors carry meanings (four is unlucky across much of East Asia; purple, white or green signal death, mourning or disease in some countries; red signals luck in China). Test slogans with native speakers; literal translations produce embarrassing or offensive taglines.
- **Country-of-origin effects**: mental associations triggered by a country; countries are known for categories (cars and electronics, high tech, wine and luxury). A brand seen as globally successful can gain credibility. Consumers often guess origins wrongly, and a foreign-sounding name can create an aura (Kotler's example is an American ice cream with an invented Scandinavian-sounding name). Do not imply a false origin through claims or labels; that is misleading and in many markets illegal.
- At home, origin can stir patriotism and job-protection sentiment; abroad, many brands work to be seen as local (a Chinese appliance maker entered the US through a neglected niche, built a local plant and sports partnerships, and aimed to be seen as a localized US brand).

### Pricing strategies

**Price escalation**: transportation, tariffs, importer, wholesaler and retailer margins, plus currency risk, can push foreign prices to two to five times the factory price for the same profit; willingness to pay also differs.

| Option | Pro | Con |
|---|---|---|
| **Uniform price everywhere** | Simple, consistent | Very different profit by country; too high for poor markets, too low for rich ones |
| **Market-based price per country** | Matches what each market can bear | Ignores cost differences; invites reshipping from low-price to high-price countries |

**Transfer prices** to foreign subsidiaries: too high raises tariffs (though it may lower foreign income tax); too low risks accusations of **dumping** (pricing below cost or below the home price to win a market). Governments watch and may force prices in line with competitors. Where copies and overcapacity drive prices down, local sourcing can make deep price cuts sustainable (Kotler's furniture-retailer example in China).

### Communication strategies

Degrees of adaptation:

1. One message everywhere, varying only language and name.
2. Same message and creative theme, execution adapted per market.
3. A global pool of ads from which countries choose.
4. Country managers create local ads within guidelines.

Check:

- **Legal and cultural acceptability**: some countries ban TV advertising to young children, sexist or gender-stereotyped ads, or underweight models; comparative advertising ranges from common to illegal.
- **Appeal**: usage habits differ (how often people wash their hair), so benefits must be framed locally.
- **Language**: local, English or a mix.
- **Selling style**: direct, get-to-the-point selling may fail where indirect, relationship-first approaches are expected.

### Distribution strategies

- Start with **local distributors** for their knowledge, but expect friction (they underinvest, ignore policy, share little information; you are seen as offering too little support, impossible goals, confusing policies). Choose carefully, invest in them, and agree on shared performance goals.
- Channel structures vary widely: some countries have many wholesale layers that multiply price; others run through import wholesalers to petty traders in local markets.
- Retail differs too: much of the world buys from small independents and open markets with haggling, daily shopping and tiny quantities; bulk breaking keeps channels long. Global retailers expanding abroad still often fail and withdraw.
- **Gray markets** divert branded products from authorized channels in-country or across borders to exploit price differences. They create free riders on legitimate distributors' investment, harm relationships and brand equity, and can endanger consumers (damaged, relabeled, obsolete, unsupported or counterfeit goods). Prevent by policing distributors, raising prices to low-cost distributors, varying product features or warranties by country, and above all by detecting violations quickly and enforcing severe penalties promptly.

## Global similarities and differences

Digital connectivity and media have converged lifestyles, especially among young middle-class consumers, creating markets for standardized products. Yet countries still differ on six cultural dimensions (from Hofstede's research):

| Dimension | Low end | High end | Marketing implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Power distance** | Equalize power, justify inequality | Accept hierarchy | Status cues, authority endorsements, who decides in B2B |
| **Individualism vs collectivism** | 'We': self-worth from the group | 'I': self-reliance | Personal achievement versus family and group benefits |
| **Masculinity vs femininity** | Cooperation, modesty, care, quality of life | Achievement, assertiveness, material reward | Competitive versus caring appeals |
| **Uncertainty avoidance** | Relaxed, practice over principle | Rigid codes, low tolerance for ambiguity | Guarantees, detailed specs, trial and risk reducers |
| **Normative vs pragmatic** | Preserve tradition, skeptical of change | Thrift and preparing for the future | Heritage versus progress messaging |
| **Indulgence vs restraint** | Strict norms curb gratification | Free enjoyment of life | Hedonic versus functional framing |

These describe countries, not individuals; use them as starting hypotheses and validate with local research. The best global brands are consistent in theme while reflecting local behavior, brand development, competition and law.

## Worked example: a European e-bike brand choosing its first non-EU market

| Step | Analysis | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| Objectives | 15% of sales outside the EU within 4 years | Waterfall: one market at a time |
| Market screen | US (large, growing, distant, tariff risk), Canada (smaller, culturally close), Japan (dense cities, strict regulations, high uncertainty avoidance) | Canada first, US second |
| Entry mode | Little local knowledge; bulky product needing service | Direct export with an exclusive importer-distributor plus selected bike dealers for service; avoid direct investment until volume justifies it |
| Product | Different e-bike speed and power class rules; cold winters | Product adaptation: compliant motor settings, winter tires accessory, bilingual labels |
| Brand | Name tested in English and French; 'European engineering' origin is a genuine asset | Use real origin with a 'built for Canadian winters' local angle |
| Price | Freight, duty, distributor and dealer margins escalate price about 1.8x | Market-based price slightly below US-import competitors; same model numbers to deter gray imports from the EU price list |
| Communication | Comparative ads acceptable; Quebec has French-language requirements | Same creative theme, local execution |
| Gray market | EU prices lower; risk of parallel imports | Country-specific warranty registration; distributor agreements with penalties |

## Global market entry canvas

```markdown
- Why go abroad (profit, scale, diversification, counterattack, follow customers, transfer ideas):
- International sales objective and expansion pace (waterfall / sprinkler / born global):
- Market scores: attractiveness, risk, our advantage, growth, proximity, cultural distance, collaborators:
- Entry mode and evolution path (indirect export -> direct export -> licensing/franchise -> JV -> direct investment):
- Program per market: product (extension / adaptation / invention), brand elements, price (escalation, uniform vs market-based), communication (degree of adaptation, legal checks), distribution (partners, channel length, gray-market controls):
- Cultural dimension hypotheses and research to validate:
- Home-market safeguards:
```

## Workflow

1. State why the company should go abroad and the target international share; confirm the home base can be sustained.
2. List candidate countries and score them on attractiveness, risk, competitive advantage, growth, proximity, cultural distance and partner availability; prefer depth in few markets.
3. Choose pace (waterfall, sprinkler, born global) based on first-mover importance and resources.
4. Pick an entry mode per market matched to knowledge, required control and risk tolerance; plan the evolution path and partner criteria.
5. Decide the standardization line: what stays global (positioning, core identity), what adapts locally.
6. Review every product and brand element for adaptation (features, labeling, colors, ingredients, packaging, name, promotions, prices, ads); run linguistic and cultural checks on names and slogans.
7. Build a landed-cost model for price escalation; choose uniform or market-based pricing; set transfer prices that withstand tariff and dumping scrutiny; design against gray markets.
8. Check ad law and norms (children, gender, comparative ads, models), message appeal, language and selling style.
9. Select and invest in distributors with shared goals; map channel length and retail structure.
10. Set up counterfeit monitoring and enforcement.
11. Validate cultural assumptions with local research before scaling.

## Red flags

- Expansion justified only by 'the market is huge', with no assessment of risk or advantage.
- Entering many markets at once without the resources to adapt to any.
- Straight extension of a product whose usage depends on local habits, appliances or space.
- Brand names or slogans never checked by native speakers.
- Prices set by converting the home price, ignoring escalation and local willingness to pay.
- Large cross-border price gaps with no gray-market controls.
- Transfer prices set purely for tax advantage.
- Comparative or child-directed ads reused in countries that restrict them.
- A local distributor appointed with no performance goals or support plan.
- Implying a foreign origin the product does not have.
- International growth while home-market service and assortment slip.
