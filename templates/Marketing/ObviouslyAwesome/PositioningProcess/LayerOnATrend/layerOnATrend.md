# Step 9. Layer On a Trend (but Be Careful)

Once the market category is set, you may add a trend that helps target customers see why the product matters right now. Aim for the overlap of three circles: your product's strengths, your market context, and a trend your customers already care about. This step is optional: a trend that does not reinforce the positioning muddies it, and a plain but clear position always beats a trendy, confusing one.

## What a trend adds

- The category says what the product is; a trend says why it matters now and can connect the purchase to company priorities, making it more strategic and urgent.
- Buyers watch trends because they fear being left behind; media and analysts cover them because they are new.
- A trend can give a solid, "boring" product extra gloss. Dunford's Redgate story: the leader in database tools, in a settled category, wove "database DevOps" into its positioning through content and sales training aimed at senior development leaders, and saw multi-product purchases rise and inbound leads double.
- Dunford's retail example: tying a product for store associates to the "mobility" trend retailers were planning around, because cross-device access really was how retailers could use tablets.

## The three-circle test

| Circle | Question |
|---|---|
| Product strengths | Does the trend amplify one of our value themes, not a minor feature? |
| Market context | Does it fit the category we chose, without replacing it? |
| Customer relevance | Do our best-fit customers already know and care about this trend? |

Use the trend only where all three overlap.

## How trends go wrong

1. **Trend without a market.** Describing what makes you interesting before saying what you are. "The sharing economy for pets" or "Uber for cats" left Dunford baffled; the product was a marketplace for pet services. A trend is not a market, and a company name is not a market either (people hold inconsistent assumptions about it). Declare the category first, then the trend.
2. **Trend and market, no link to the product.** Content programs so busy riding a hot topic that readers never learn what the company sells.
3. **Hype with nothing behind it.** Dunford recounts a beverage company renaming itself around blockchain with no partnerships, plan or explanation; the gap was quickly noticed and the stock collapsed. Never borrow a trend you cannot connect to a real capability.

## No trend? Fine.

- Many companies thrive in markets without sizzle. If a trend fits, use it; if not, do not force one.

## Template: trend fit check

| Candidate trend | Value theme it amplifies | Real capability behind it (proof) | Do best customers care? (evidence) | Category + trend phrasing | Keep? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | | |

Good phrasing keeps the category as the noun: "AI-assisted accounts-payable automation", not "an AI company for finance".

## Worked example

HVAC dispatch SaaS (category: emergency dispatch for residential contractors).

- "AI": the routing uses a learned model of travel time and first-time-fix rates. Customers care about results, not models; use as supporting proof ("routing learns which tech fixes what"), not as the headline.
- "Heat-pump transition": contractors are scrambling to train techs on heat pumps; skill-tagged routing sends certified techs to heat-pump calls. Strong link to the "fix it first time" theme and a live customer concern. Keep: "emergency dispatch built for the heat-pump era".
- "Gig economy for trades": no real link; customers employ their technicians. Reject.

## Workflow

1. Confirm the market category from [Step 8](../MarketFrameOfReference/marketFrameOfReference.md) is clear and stated.
2. List trends the best-fit customers discuss (sales calls, industry press, conference agendas, their own initiatives).
3. Run the three-circle test and the fit-check table for each.
4. Require a real capability and proof behind any trend you use.
5. Phrase it as category plus trend; never let the trend replace the category.
6. If none fit, skip the step and say so.
7. When building content around the trend, make sure every piece connects to the product and its category.

## Red flags

- A buzzword in the headline and no category anywhere on the page.
- "The X of Y" metaphors ("Uber for", "Netflix of") as the main description.
- A trend added because investors or the board like it, not because customers care.
- A trend claim with no capability or proof behind it.
- Thought leadership with no path to the product.
- A rename or rebrand around a hot technology.
- Positioning that changes every quarter to chase the latest trend.

Current note: the same rule applies to today's generative-AI wave: "AI" is a trend, not a category, and claims of AI capability need a real capability and proof behind them.
