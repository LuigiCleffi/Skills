# Step 3. Align Your Positioning Vocabulary and Let Go of Your Positioning Baggage

You cannot see a new way to position a product while still thinking of it the old way. First give the team a common vocabulary so no time is lost arguing about what positioning is; then name the "baggage", the history baked into the current position, and get explicit agreement to suspend it for the rest of the exercise.

## Align the vocabulary first

Spend the opening of the workshop (Dunford sets aside about an hour) making sure everyone agrees on:

- **What positioning means and why it matters**: deliberately defining how you are the best at something a defined market cares a lot about; positioning as context (see [positioning as context](../../WhatIsPositioning/PositioningAsContext/positioningAsContext.md)).
- **The components and their definitions**: competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, who cares a lot, market category, relevant trends (see [five components](../../WhatIsPositioning/FiveComponents/fiveComponents.md)).
- **How market maturity and the competitive landscape affect the positioning style**: head to head, big fish in a small pond, or creating a new game (see [Step 8](../MarketFrameOfReference/marketFrameOfReference.md)).

Without this, senior people spend half the day debating definitions.

## Most products can be many things

- The goal is to find a market where the product easily wins because it has obvious benefits over the alternatives. If you have always seen it one way, that is hard.
- Products sold for one purpose often become better known for another as markets shift. Dunford's case: a baking-soda brand, facing declining home baking, embraced the odor-absorbing use some customers had already discovered, repackaged for the fridge, multiplied sales and later extended into deodorizing products of many kinds. The starting point was willingness to drop the old way of thinking.

## What positioning baggage is

- The product's history, from conception to launch, built into its current positioning: the market imagined at the start, the original terminology, features described the way they were first described.
- Customers carry none of it. They meet the product with no knowledge of its history. Market confusion starts with the gap between how creators understand the product and how customers first perceive it.
- Baggage is uneven across the team: founders and long-tenured staff see the product through its full history; newer employees much less.

## How to let go

1. Explain the concept of baggage so everyone can recognize it.
2. Call out where history shows up in today's positioning:
   - Is the current market the one chosen when the product was imagined?
   - Are features and terminology still described the way they were at launch?
   - Is the category the one the founders came from (their profession, previous company)?
3. Get explicit agreement: the product was built with a certain market and audience in mind, and that may no longer be the best way to position it.
4. Ask everyone to suspend their opinions about the product's positioning for the duration of the exercise.

Dunford's Clearpath story shows the payoff: a mobile industrial robot kept being compared to stationary factory robots until the team dropped "robot" and framed it as self-driving vehicles for industry, making mapping, dispatch and fleet management obvious strengths.

## Worked example

The HVAC dispatch SaaS was founded by ex-calendar-app engineers, and the homepage still says "smart scheduling for trades". The inventory shows "scheduling" came from the founders' background; feature names ("calendar sync", "slots") come from the first prototype; best customers talk instead about "taking the 2 a.m. call" and "getting a tech there before the competitor". The team agrees to set aside "scheduling" as a given for the rest of the workshop.

## Workflow

1. Run a short primer on definitions, components and positioning styles before any positioning debate.
2. Build the baggage inventory with the team, collecting current copy, deck, pricing and feature names.
3. For each item, mark its origin and whether best customers would recognize or care about it.
4. Note who on the team holds the most history and invite newer staff and customer-facing roles to speak first.
5. Record the agreement to suspend the current position; refer back to it whenever someone argues "but we are an X".
6. Proceed to [Step 4: competitive alternatives](../CompetitiveAlternatives/competitiveAlternatives.md).

## Red flags

- "We are a [category], that is what we built", said as a fact rather than a hypothesis.
- Copy and feature names unchanged since the first release.
- Category chosen because it is the founders' home industry.
- Workshop time lost to arguing over what positioning means.
- New hires and customers describe the product differently from founders, and founders' version wins by default.
- A repositioning rejected because "nobody would want an X that also does Y" without testing what customers already do with it.
