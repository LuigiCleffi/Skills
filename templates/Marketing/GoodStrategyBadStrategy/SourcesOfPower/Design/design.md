# Using Design

Many effective strategies are **designs rather than decisions**: constructed, not picked from a menu of options. A master strategist designs a configuration of resources and actions that fit one another and the target customer, anticipates how others will behave, and coordinates actions across functions and over time. The harder the challenge, the tighter that integration must be.

## Business is not war, but some lessons transfer

- Firms compete by putting offers in front of buyers, more like a dance contest than a battle. Size is usually the result of being preferred, not its cause. Borrow from military history only at the right level of abstraction.
- Rumelt uses a classic ancient battle to show three essential aspects of strategy in pure form:
  1. **Premeditation**: designed in advance. There is always some prior guidance; winging it is not a strategy.
  2. **Anticipation**: the opponent is not a generic rational player but an organization with history, doctrine, standard training and leaders with known biases. The trap worked because the opponent's own vigorous, predictable responses closed it. The behaviour was also shaped in advance (provoking an impetuous leader) and the speed of events gave no time to learn.
  3. **Design of coordinated action**: separate units executing a preplanned choreography in time and space, which nobody expected because no army had done it before.

## Design, not decision

- Decision theory assumes you are handed a clear set of alternatives. Leaders rarely are. Strategy is more often like designing a high-performance aircraft than choosing which forklift to buy.
- In design problems, gains are **sharply peaked**: getting the combination right pays a lot; getting it wrong costs a lot. Mutual adjustment among parts is the essence.

## Tuning the parts to a sweet spot

- Rumelt's car example: a 'driving machine' feel needs chassis, steering, suspension, engine and controls tuned to one another; off-the-shelf quality parts do not produce it. Vary dozens of parameters until the target driver smiles.
- Add cost: optimize **smile per dollar**. Add brand, dealer experience, reliability and resale value: more parameters, more interactions.
- That is still a monopoly view. Add rivals and you look for a **competitive sweet spot**: more smile per dollar than the buyer can get elsewhere. That usually means identifying the **target market where you have a differential advantage**, and competition pushes you to a much smaller set of products, setups and customers.

## The trade-off principle

From systems engineering under a tight weight budget: every attempt to optimize one subsystem created problems for others, so the work was mostly about interactions, clever multi-purpose components and timing.

- A design-type strategy is an adroit configuration of resources and actions that yields an advantage in a challenging situation.
- **Given fixed resources, the greater the challenge, the greater the need for clever, tight integration.**
- **Given a fixed challenge, better resources reduce the need for tight integration.**
- Resources and tight coordination are **partial substitutes**: a resource-poor firm can meet a hard challenge only through tight integration.
- Tight integration has **costs**: harder to create, narrower in focus, more fragile, less flexible (a racing car versus an all-purpose SUV). Accept them when the challenge is very high; otherwise a looser design that serves a broader market is often better.

## Strategic resources and the arc of enterprise

- Ordinary inputs (trucks, office equipment, warehouses, graduates, professional services) are not strategic; rivals get them on the same terms.
- A **strategic resource** is long-lasting, built, developed, designed or discovered by the firm, and cannot be duplicated by competitors without a net economic loss (patents, brands, franchises, installed bases).
- A powerful resource gives great strategic simplicity: with a protected breakthrough patent, the obvious thing (make and sell the product) is enough, and 'strategic plans' can be mere projections.
- Resources are to coordinated activity as capital is to labour: past clever activity accumulates into a resource that then yields returns for years without further effort.
- Spectrum: strong resource position needs little design; a moderate resource (a product idea, a customer relationship) needs a coherent strategy built around it; the cleverest strategies start with **few resources** and win through adroit coordination across functions and time.
- **Peril of a potent resource**: profits arrive without ongoing strategy work, breeding laxity. Leaders credit current actions for results that are the harvest of past seasons, and books copy the winner's dress code and parking policy.
- Existing resources can lever new ones but also block innovation. Rumelt notes a copier pioneer that built a service network around failure-prone leased machines, and therefore failed to build the paper-handling skill that would have led into personal copiers and printers, because that would have devalued its service resource.
- **Success leads to laxity and bloat, which lead to decline.** Upstarts with tightly integrated designs exploit this. Over time they too loosen, rely on accumulated resources, let fiefdoms and products multiply, bolt on acquisitions, and become prey. Learn design-type strategy from an upstart's early conquests, not from a mature company's posturing.

## A coherent premium design ('hold the high ground')

Rumelt profiles a premium heavy-truck maker earning above-average, unusually stable returns in a mature, cyclical industry. Obstacles to quality leadership: reputation for durability takes years and is lost quickly; design excellence is tacit knowledge built by stable teams; buyers resist paying upfront for future savings. The design that answers them:

- **Reframe quality through the eyes of the owner-driver**, for whom the truck is home and office and whose taste includes comfort and classic style, not only cost per mile.
- **Use the influencer**: fleet buyers care about cost per mile, but their drivers (whose turnover and idle time fleets care about) prefer the premium brand, and owner-drivers have the highest status among drivers.
- Experienced dealers configure custom builds; trucks are built to order with low inventory; parts are shared where practical; flexible manufacturing gives stable work for designers; higher margins keep dealers loyal.
- **Consistency over time and focus**: only large trucks, no economy line, no diversification, so everyone's knowledge is about truckers and heavy trucks.
- The niche is not big or fast-growing enough to justify a rival building new brands, designs and dealers to attack it.
- Pieces are **specialized to each other**: a 'Frankenstein' firm built from generic parts of other truck makers (fleet-oriented mid-price designs, dealers aimed at picky owner-drivers, cost-cutting engineers) would not last.
- Real threats are not rival products but **changes that undermine the logic of the design** (shifts in who drives, or sales tools that devalue dealer expertise).

## Worked example: a premium B2B cybersecurity services firm

- Target: mid-size hospitals (high challenge: low budgets, high stakes, a giant rival with broader products).
- Influencer insight: the CFO signs, but the clinical IT lead's trust decides; peers at regional hospital associations shape that trust.
- Design: incident-response retainers priced per bed (matches hospital budgeting); responders trained on clinical systems; content and events through hospital associations; a reference network of hospital CIOs; no other verticals; hires from hospital IT.
- Fit check: generic enterprise sales playbooks and a 'serves all industries' website conflict; remove them.
- Threat watch: consolidation of hospitals into large systems with central security teams would undermine the design's logic.

## Workflow

1. Define the challenge and how hard it is relative to the user's resources.
2. Identify the target buyer and any influencers, users or status groups who shape the decision.
3. Describe the 'sweet spot' for that buyer: what combination of product, price, experience and brand maximizes value per dollar versus alternatives.
4. Build the design fit matrix across functions; find conflicts and generic elements.
5. Tighten integration where the challenge is high; loosen where flexibility and breadth matter more.
6. Inventory strategic resources versus commodity inputs; check whether the plan leans on a fading resource.
7. Anticipate rivals as organizations (doctrine, incentives, leaders' biases) and how their predictable reactions could be used.
8. List structural changes that would undermine the design's logic and set up monitoring.

## Red flags

- Each function adopts its own 'best practice' with no reference to the others.
- Premium price with a generic product, mass channel or discount-driven promotions.
- Assuming the rival will respond 'rationally' rather than as its organization habitually does.
- A strategy chosen from consultant-provided options without designing how parts fit.
- An incumbent crediting current tactics for profits that come from an old resource.
- Bolt-on acquisitions and proliferating product lines diluting a once-tight design.
- Ignoring the users or influencers who sway the economic buyer.
