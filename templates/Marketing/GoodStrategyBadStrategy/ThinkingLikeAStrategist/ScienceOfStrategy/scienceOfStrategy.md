# The Science of Strategy

A strategy is a **hypothesis** about what will work, and implementing it is an **experiment**. Generally available knowledge is necessary but rarely decisive; advantage comes from **proprietary functional knowledge** your organization builds by exploring its arena empirically. Test new strategic ideas against principles and experience, try them cheaply in the real world, watch for anomalies and keep revising.

## Strategy is a hypothesis, not a calculation

- People trained in deterministic planning often want a machine that predicts the result of A versus B and then 'winds the crank' to the best plan. Deduction applies fixed rules to known facts; it produces no new ideas.
- If nothing new is needed, deduction suffices and the answer is 'keep doing more of the same'. In a changing world that is rarely right; a good strategy needs an **entrepreneurial component**: insight into new combinations of resources for new risks and opportunities.
- Competition pushes you to the **edge between the known and the unknown**. The unease of ambiguity there is real; it is the scent of opportunity. Asking for a strategy guaranteed to work is like asking a scientist for a hypothesis guaranteed to be true.
- A strategy is not a wild theory but an **educated judgment**, and the people closest to the business are usually the most educated about it.
- Assuming all important knowledge is already known (or available from authorities) deadens innovation. Generating strategy means leaving the comfort of deduction for induction, analogy, judgment and insight.

## Empiricism and refutation

- The Enlightenment's answer to 'what should we believe?' was **scientific empiricism**: believe evidence, test beliefs by experiment or real-world data, throw out what fails. An idea that cannot possibly be proven false by observable fact is not scientific.
- A new hypothesis cannot be deduced from existing knowledge (or it would not be new); it comes from insight. Its worth is judged by **data**, not by its author's rank or popularity.
- In business the test is whether it works. Business seeks to understand particular situations rather than universal laws, but science is a **method**, and the method of good businesspeople is intense attention to data and to what works.

### Two-stage test

1. Test the idea against **well-established principles and accumulated knowledge** of the business. Is it contradicted by basic economics or past results?
2. If it survives, **try it out** and see what happens, preferably in a way that is affordable if it fails.

## Anomalies

- An **anomaly** is a fact that does not fit received wisdom. Some minds see a blemish; strategists see an opportunity to learn something valuable. Anomalies are the frontier.
- Anomalies are not in nature but in the mind of the observer, revealed by **comparing facts with refined expectations**. You see them only if you know what you expected.
- Rumelt's example: a coffee-bean retail manager visiting a foreign city noticed that premium coffee there was the mass-market product, served fast but expensive in lively social settings, while at home premium coffee was a niche and 'fast' meant cheap and plastic. Customers at home were just as wealthy. That contrast was the anomaly that seeded a strategic hypothesis.

## Scientific induction: the learning loop

- The first test was a faithful copy of the foreign coffee-bar concept (standing bar, foreign-language menu, opera music, formal waiters). Had he stuck with it, it would have stayed one small shop.
- Instead the shop became a **living experiment**: watch customer response, change policies, retest. Foreign menu terms dropped, music dropped, formal dress dropped, chairs added, takeout cups introduced, non-fat milk allowed after much soul-searching. The concept was gradually localized.
- After hundreds of iterations the original hypothesis vanished, replaced by many new hypotheses covering each aspect of the business. **Hypothesis, data, anomaly, new hypothesis, data** is scientific induction, critical to every successful business.
- The hypothesis also required changing deep habits shaped by centuries of divergent history, and the underlying knowledge (anyone could visit such bars) was not privileged; only the insight was. That private insight was a blessing (others did not act) and a curse (hard to convince backers). Crucially, it could be **tested cheaply**: one small shop, not a huge investment.

## Proprietary information

- One of the most valuable resources a business can have is **privileged information**: knowing something others do not. It is generated every day by operations; alert businesspeople can know more about their own customers, products and processes than anyone else.
- Always ask why competitors did not respond. Rumelt's interview with a large espresso supplier: incumbents could not understand the newcomer. It was called a coffee company but was really a retailer; it fused roasting, branding, company-owned stores, chains and public financing; its drinks were mostly milk-based and unlike the original model. By the time incumbents understood, it was established.
- **Integration for learning**: the newcomer integrated vertically not to confuse rivals but to **mutually adjust multiple elements** and **capture the information each element generated**.
  - Integration is usually wasteful when you can buy good products and services outside.
  - It may be vital when the strategy requires mutual adjustment of several elements and when **important learning about interactions across elements** must be captured.

## Worked example: importing a concept into a new market

A Brazilian fitness-studio chain notices an anomaly: in its home market, group strength classes for women over 45 fill at premium prices, while in the US the category is marketed almost entirely to younger audiences. Customers in the target US city are wealthier on average.

- Hypothesis: a strength-class studio designed around women over 45 will reach 70% class utilization at a premium price within six months in one US city.
- Stage 1 test: is demand plausible given demographics and spending on fitness? Are there failed precedents, and why did they fail?
- Stage 2 test: one studio, three-month pop-up first, with paid social and partnerships with local physicians.
- Refutation: utilization below 40% after eight weeks with acquisition costs above the threshold.
- Loop: track which messages, class times, coach profiles and price points work; expect to localize (shorter classes, morning slots, a physiotherapy partnership).
- Integration: keep booking, coaching and content in-house to learn how schedule, coach and messaging interact; outsource payments and facilities management.

## Workflow

1. Restate the proposed strategy as an explicit, falsifiable hypothesis.
2. Identify the insight or anomaly behind it; check it really contradicts received wisdom and is not common knowledge.
3. Test the hypothesis against established principles, economics and the user's past data.
4. Design the cheapest meaningful market test and the result that would refute it.
5. Define what to observe; set up capture of first-party, proprietary data.
6. After each round, look for anomalies, revise the hypothesis and retest; expect the final form to differ from the original.
7. Decide which business elements to own because their interactions carry the key learning, and which to buy.
8. Ask why competitors are not doing this, and what they misunderstand about it.

## Red flags

- Requests for a strategy 'guaranteed to work' or a framework that outputs the answer.
- 'More of the same' in a changing market.
- A strategy that cannot fail any observable test.
- Rolling out a borrowed concept nationally without a pilot and iteration.
- Ignoring data that contradict the plan, or explaining them away.
- Outsourcing the parts of the business where the key customer learning happens.
- Decisions settled by seniority or authority rather than evidence.
