# Good Strategy Is Unexpected

Treat strategy as problem solving: find the critical obstacle in the situation and design a focused, coordinated way of overcoming it. Ambition, vision, values and targets matter, but none of them is a strategy. Because most organizations spread effort across many unconnected goals, a strategy that actually concentrates resources is rare, and that rarity is itself an advantage.

## What strategy is not

Keep the word from meaning everything. Strategy is not:

- **Ambition** (drive to excel), **determination** (grit), **innovation** (new ways of doing things) or **inspirational leadership** (motivating sacrifice). Strategy chooses the path along which these are applied.
- **Success.** 'Never quit until you win' is a slogan, not a way forward.
- **Goal setting.** 'Reach 20% market share and 15% margin' says where you want to be, not how you will get there. If leaders set targets and delegate the 'how', call it goal setting.
- **A label for senior decisions.** An acquisition, a pricing change or a new office is not 'strategic' because an executive made it.
- **A verbal tic.** 'Marketing strategy', 'content strategy', 'low-price strategy' often just rename an activity.
- **Big-picture direction divorced from action.** Leaving action out creates a fake gap between 'strategy' and 'implementation'.

## What strategy is

- A **cohesive response to an important challenge**: a coherent set of analyses, concepts, policies, arguments and actions aimed at a high-stakes problem.
- It identifies the **one or two critical issues**, the pivot points where effort is multiplied, and concentrates action and resources there.
- Good strategy usually looks simple and obvious once stated. It does not come out of a matrix, a template or a thick slide deck.
- It honestly names the challenge. The greater the challenge, the more the strategy must focus and coordinate to deliver a strong punch.
- It includes **feasible, coordinated near-term actions**. Those actions are the punch of the strategy, not 'implementation details'.

## The kernel (preview)

Every good strategy has a logical core, the **kernel**:

1. **Diagnosis**: what is going on here; names the critical challenge.
2. **Guiding policy**: the overall approach to dealing with the obstacles in the diagnosis; a signpost, not a route map.
3. **Coherent actions**: coordinated policies, resource commitments and actions that carry out the guiding policy.

Full treatment: see the Kernel of Good Strategy skill. Knowing the kernel also lets you spot bad strategy without specialist knowledge: if there is no diagnosis, there can be no focus of resources on a cure.

## Two natural sources of strength most frameworks miss

Standard strategy talk lists advantages (scale, network effects, patents, brand, first mover). Rumelt adds two more basic ones:

1. **Coherence**: a strategy that coordinates policies and actions creates strength through its design, while most organizations pursue unconnected or conflicting objectives. (This chapter.)
2. **Insight**: a fresh way of seeing the situation can create new advantages and expose new weaknesses. (See Discovering Power.)

## Good strategy is unexpected

- Most organizations, most of the time, have multiple goals and initiatives that **symbolize progress** without a coherent approach other than 'spend more and try harder'. Rumelt calls this look-busy 'doorknob polishing'.
- Rivals therefore do not expect you to focus. The surprise is rarely secrecy; it is that a complex organization actually concentrated its resources.
- Rumelt illustrates with a turnaround that cut a sprawling product line to a handful of products, cut most channels and redesigned the business logic around them, and with a military campaign whose winning manoeuvre was textbook doctrine, published openly, yet still surprised observers because nobody expected it to be carried out so purely.
- Note that the turnaround actions were 'Business 101'. Good strategy is often obvious in content and rare in execution because it requires saying no.
- Strategy can also be patient: when no window of opportunity is open, the right move may be to survive, stay lean and be ready to pounce when the next shift in technology or demand arrives. That is a judgment about one situation, not a general formula.

## Focus requires saying no

- Conflicting goals, resources spread across unconnected targets and accommodating every internal interest are luxuries of the rich and powerful; they make for bad strategy.
- Concentrating effort means **suppressing the ambitions of parts of the organization**: some teams, channels, segments or pet projects will be told to wait, support others, or stop.
- Strategy is at least as much about **what the organization will not do** as about what it will do.

### Applied to marketing and go-to-market

- A plan with 'grow enterprise, mid-market and SMB; launch in three regions; expand the partner channel; double brand awareness' has no focus; it has a wish list.
- Focus looks like: 'For the next three quarters we sell only to mid-market logistics firms in North America, through direct sales, on one use case. We pause SMB self-serve and the EMEA launch.'
- The surprise effect is real in markets: competitors calibrate on your past diffuse behaviour, so a sudden concentration (one segment, one message, one channel, all budget) often catches them unprepared.

## Worked example: a sprawling B2B SaaS portfolio

Situation: a project-management SaaS vendor has seven product editions, four ICPs, six paid channels and a 'strategy' slide that reads 'Become the #1 work platform; grow ARR 60%; delight customers'.

| Element | Bad version (as found) | Good version (rewritten) |
|---|---|---|
| Challenge | Not stated | Win rate against the category leader collapses when buyers compare on enterprise security; we lose 70% of deals over 200 seats. |
| Approach | 'Grow faster than the market' | Stop competing for large enterprise; own the 20–200-seat agency segment where speed of setup decides deals. |
| Actions | 'Increase marketing spend; launch more features' | Collapse to two editions; rebuild onboarding for under-one-hour setup; reallocate enterprise ABM budget to agency communities and partner referrals; sales comp only on agency deals. |
| What we stop | Nothing | Enterprise RFPs, the government edition, two of six paid channels. |

## Workflow

1. Ask for or reconstruct the current 'strategy'. Separate statements into goals, slogans/vision, activities, and genuine choices.
2. Ask: what is the critical challenge or obstacle? If the document does not say, flag it as the first gap.
3. Identify the one or two pivot points where concentrated effort would matter most (a segment, a channel, a buying moment, a constraint).
4. Draft a kernel: diagnosis, guiding policy, three to five coherent actions.
5. Write an explicit 'not doing' list: segments, channels, products, campaigns that will be paused or cut.
6. Check that the actions are feasible now, coordinated with each other, and resourced; replace any action that is only a target.
7. Note what rivals expect from you and whether the focused plan would surprise them.

## Red flags

- The 'strategy' is a set of numbers: ARR, share, MQLs, NPS targets with no 'how'.
- Vision, mission and values slides with no named challenge.
- Every segment, region and channel gets a share of budget; nothing is cut.
- Leaders say 'we have a great strategy, we just have an execution problem'.
- Every initiative is labelled 'strategic'; activity names are prefixed with 'strategy'.
- The plan needs a 60-slide deck to explain and still has no single critical issue.
- Initiatives that signal progress (rebrand, new dashboards, reorganization) without addressing the obstacle.
