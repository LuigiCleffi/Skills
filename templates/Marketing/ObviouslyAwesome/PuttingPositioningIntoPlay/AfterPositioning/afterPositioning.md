# After Positioning: What Happens Next?

A finished positioning only pays off when it becomes real across the company. Translate it first into a sales story the team agrees on, then into a master messaging document, then into roadmap and pricing choices that reinforce the chosen market. Review the positioning every six months, or sooner when competitors, regulation, the economy, technology or customer attitudes shift.

## 1. Sales story before messaging

For businesses with a sales conversation (most B2B), the positioning must show up in how sellers frame the product, especially on the first call. As a team, agree on the components of the story (not slide copy or design):

1. **Problem** — a crisp definition of the problem the solution was designed to solve; it bounds the conversation and makes the rest obvious. ("Mid-size contractors lose their most profitable jobs because emergency calls go unanswered or wait for a dispatcher.")
2. **Current approaches and where they fall short** — how customers try to solve it today (the competitive alternatives). ("Whiteboards break at night; big FSM suites need too many clicks for an urgent call; answering services take messages but cannot assign a tech.")
3. **The perfect world** — the features of an ideal solution given that problem and those gaps; these become the purchase criteria. ("Every emergency call answered, triaged and routed to the nearest qualified tech in under a minute, day or night.")
4. **Introduce the product in its market category.** ("DispatchNow is emergency dispatch software for residential HVAC and plumbing contractors.")
5. **Value themes** — each theme in turn, with how the product delivers it.
6. **Supporting material** — common objections, case study or customer list.
7. **Next step** — what you want the prospect to do.

The point: the team agrees on the problem, current solutions, the gap and the key purchase criteria, so positioning translates into one pitch.

|---|---|---|

## 2. Messaging

- Messaging is not done live in a group; once the story arc is agreed, marketing translates it into copy for materials, campaigns and the website.
- Dunford's one tip: write a **messaging document**. Every asset has a slightly different purpose, so without one agreed baseline, messages are built on modified messages and the language, and then the positioning, creep away from what was agreed.
- The document holds the accepted baseline messages, gives everyone a common starting point and keeps copy anchored.

Suggested structure: one-line description; category statement; problem statement; value themes with approved wording and proof points; segment-specific variants; words to use and to avoid; boilerplate.

## 3. Product roadmap and pricing

- Repositioning changes how the company sees itself, which changes feature priorities: the roadmap for "accounting software" differs from one for "financial services". Strong positioning is reinforced over time by feature choices.
- Each category carries price expectations. Align pricing with the chosen category to signal you belong there; Dunford raised prices for "CRM for investment banks" because those buyers did not expect general-CRM pricing.
- A positioning change acts like a business-strategy change; expect every department to be affected over time.

## 4. Tracking positioning over time

Check the positioning every six months, and whenever a major event could change the competitive landscape or how customers evaluate solutions.

| Trigger | What it can do | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| A credible, established competitor enters, or a large player acquires a small rival | Rapidly reshape what buyers think is possible, which features matter, expected prices | Do *target customers* expect the newcomer to be relevant to them, or a change in price or features? If not, hold. If yes, restart at Step 4 |
| Regulation (even the threat of it) | Forces buyers to care about previously minor features; hands some vendors an edge | Does a new requirement map to an attribute we have? |
| Economic climate | Downturns shift buyers from revenue growth to cost cutting and cash (and back) | Do value themes need re-weighting toward savings or growth? |
| New technology | Changes what is possible; once understood, purchase criteria shift fast | Does a previously minor feature become a key differentiator? |
| Customer attitudes and preferences | Even business buyers shift (Dunford's example: bland data-center hardware suddenly expected to look good after one startup's design caught on) | Are buyers starting to value something new? |

- A big new entrant does not automatically require change. If you target a subsegment, the newcomer may serve it no better than the old leader. Customer perception is what matters.
- When perceptions do change, restart at [Step 4](../../PositioningProcess/CompetitiveAlternatives/competitiveAlternatives.md): have the best customers' alternatives changed? If so, do your unique attributes and value change too?

## Closing takeaways (from the Conclusion)

1. Any product can be positioned in more than one market; it is not stuck where nobody understands it.
2. Great positioning rarely happens by default; deliberate, test, fail and try again.
3. Your best customers' true alternatives lead you to your differentiators.
4. Position in a market that makes your strengths obvious to the people you want to sell to.
5. Use trends cautiously: a clear, unglamorous position beats a fashionable, confusing one.

## Workflow

1. Run a sales-story session with the positioning team using the template; agree on problem, alternatives, gap and purchase criteria.
2. Have marketing draft the messaging document from the story; review it with sales and product; publish it as the baseline.
3. Rebuild the first-call deck, website and key assets from the messaging document.
4. Review the roadmap against the new category's expectations and the value themes; flag features that reinforce or contradict the position.
5. Check pricing against category price expectations.
6. Set a six-month review and list the triggers that would prompt an earlier one.
7. At each review, ask whether best customers' alternatives or evaluation criteria have changed; if so, rerun from Step 4.

## Red flags

- The team jumps from workshop to new homepage copy without agreeing on a sales story.
- The first-call deck opens with company history or features instead of the problem.
- No "perfect world" or purchase-criteria step, so prospects evaluate on the leader's terms.
- Each campaign rewrites the value proposition from the last campaign's copy.
- Roadmap still prioritized for the old category.
- Price anchored to the old category after moving to a more valuable frame.
- Repositioning in panic every time a competitor makes news, or never reviewing at all.
- Ignoring a recession, regulation or technology shift that changes what buyers value.
