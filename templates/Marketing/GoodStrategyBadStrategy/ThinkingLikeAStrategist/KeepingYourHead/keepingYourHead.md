# Keeping Your Head

Good strategy grows out of an **independent, careful assessment** of the situation. Bad strategy follows the crowd and substitutes popular slogans for insight. The hard balance is to be independent without being eccentric, and doubting without being a curmudgeon: assess fundamentals yourself, use the lessons of other times and places, and do not let market enthusiasm, peer behaviour or 'this time is different' replace judgment.

## Assess the fundamentals yourself

Rumelt's account of a privately built transatlantic fibre cable around 1998–2001:

- The first cable cost about $1.5 million per unit of capacity and sold units at $8 million (well below the old consortium price), recovering its cost quickly. The company went public and soon was valued above a major automaker.
- The story: internet traffic doubling yearly, undersea cables as the 'choke point', 'the fat pipe wins' through economies of scale.
- The fundamentals: capacity was a near-perfect **commodity**; one supplier's capacity was indistinguishable from another's; new private entrants were coming; the technology was not proprietary; each new cable added far more capacity at far lower unit cost; and capital was **sunk**, so existing cables would keep operating whatever prices did. It is hard to imagine a worse industry structure.
- Outcome: massive overcapacity, a resale market from buyers stuck with unused capacity, and prices that fell to a few percent of the original asking price. International internet traffic grew more slowly than expected (most traffic was local and content was replicated near users) and the traffic that did grow earned little because its growth was driven by near-zero prices.
- A simple Five Forces analysis predicted this. It was ignored because stock prices seemed to say otherwise.

### Costs belong to choices

- Products do not have 'a cost'; **choices** do. The cost of one more unit (marginal), of producing at a rate for a year (average), of building a plant and producing (long-run average), or of a rush order are all different.
- Once a cable (or a data centre, a content library, a software platform) exists, the cost of carrying one more unit is near zero. With competition and no differentiation, **nothing stops price falling toward that cost**.
- Marketing translation: digital goods, ad inventory, SaaS seats in a commoditized category and excess capacity of any kind tend to price toward marginal cost unless you have differentiation, lock-in or structural barriers.

### Break closed circles

- If executives invest because market prices are high, and prices are high because executives invest (and 'growth' is measured as installed capacity), the system becomes a **closed circle**. Inside it, the question 'is this an overcapacity commodity business?' cannot be decided; Rumelt borrows Gödel's incompleteness theorem as a metaphor. You must step **outside** the system to answer it.
- Similar circles: policy set by opinion polls; curricula set by student ratings; marketing plans validated by vanity metrics that the plan itself generates (impressions, followers, MQLs defined by the campaign's own scoring).
- Rumelt notes consultants of that era comparing companies' stock performance instead of evaluating the logic of their strategies, treating 'the market has spoken' as proof.

## Five errors behind man-made disasters

Rumelt links a major financial crisis (a credit bubble where easy credit inflated asset prices that then served as collateral for more borrowing, until a gentle decline triggered forced selling and debt deflation) to other engineered disasters. Five intertwined errors:

1. **Engineering overreach**: building systems whose failure modes and consequences exceed the designers' ability to understand them.
2. **Smooth-sailing fallacy**: assuming no recent tremors means no risk. Measuring risk from past price volatility misses a design flaw that shows no shakiness until collapse. Such collapses are not unforeseeable 'black swans'; given the flaw, they are near certain.
3. **Risk-seeking incentives**: when you gain if things go well and others bear losses if they go badly, you seek risk (bailout expectations, bonuses and fees with upside and no downside). Actors with such incentives actively add risk to the system.
4. **Social herding**: when uncertain, it is sensible to look at what others do, assuming some know more. But if everyone calibrates on everyone else, the whole group can take uninformed actions, each believing someone else checked the fundamentals.
5. **The inside view**: the tendency to ignore pertinent outside data and believe 'this case is different' (I am a good driver so the statistics do not apply; most new restaurants fail but not mine). In the crisis, such beliefs hardened into doctrines: other countries' history is irrelevant, the central bank has tamed cycles, deep markets absorb any shock, new risk tools make risk manageable.

- The **outside view** was simple: credit booms tied to property booms have repeatedly ended badly, across many advanced economies and throughout the country's own history (Rumelt traces a series of land and credit busts over two centuries, each driven by easy credit to finance settlement or ownership).
- Confidence in new instruments or methods needs long, real-world stress testing across many conditions, not just a period in which they have not yet failed.

## Applying to marketing and business strategy

- **Hype cycles**: when a channel, platform or technology is hot, run the industry-structure analysis anyway: differentiation, entry, proprietary technology, capacity growth, buyer power, price trends.
- **Do not take competitors' funding or valuations as proof** that their strategy works or that you should copy it.
- **Use base rates (outside view)** for launches, new channels, market entries and conversion forecasts: what share of comparable launches hit plan? How long did comparable categories take to adopt?
- **Smooth sailing**: low complaint volume, stable churn or no PR crises do not prove a risky practice (aggressive auto-renewal, borderline claims, dependence on one platform's algorithm) is safe.
- **Incentives**: check whether agencies, partners or internal teams are paid on volume or spend with no downside; they will push risk up.
- **Herding signals**: 'everyone in the category is launching X', 'analysts say', 'our board saw it at a conference'. Ask who actually checked the fundamentals.

## Worked example: entering a booming creator-tools category

A media company plans to launch an AI video-editing app because three startups in the category raised large rounds and downloads are 'doubling every quarter'.

- Crowd belief: huge, fast-growing market; valuations prove it.
- Structure: underlying models are available to everyone via APIs (non-proprietary); switching costs are low; dozens of entrants; platform owners can add the same features natively; marginal cost per edit is falling fast, so prices will compress.
- Outside view: in comparable app booms, a small share of entrants survive past two years, usually those with distribution or a distinctive workflow.
- Closed circle: 'growth' measured by downloads driven by paid installs.
- Judgment: do not launch a general-purpose editor. Instead embed editing into the company's existing creator network, where it has distribution and proprietary content rights that make it distinctive.

## Workflow

1. State the prevailing belief and its sources (valuations, peers, analysts, media).
2. Do your own industry-structure analysis; identify sunk capacity and the relevant marginal cost.
3. Find the outside view: a reference class of similar situations and its base rates.
4. Check whether success metrics are closed circles; add external measures (paid revenue, retention, margin).
5. Look for design flaws not visible in recent data and for risk-seeking incentives among participants.
6. Write an independent judgment and the evidence that would change it; revisit as data arrive.
7. Recommend actions that hold up if the crowd is wrong, and that profit if you are right.

## Red flags

- 'The market (or investors) is telling us this is huge' used instead of analysis.
- Forecasts that assume current growth rates continue indefinitely.
- 'Our launch is different' with no base rate considered.
- Success measured by capacity, activity or spend rather than paid outcomes.
- No incidents so far treated as proof of safety.
- Partners or teams paid on volume with no downside for bad outcomes.
- Copying the most-funded or highest-valued competitor during a boom.
