# Using Leverage

A good strategy draws power from focusing minds, energy and action. Channelled at the right moment onto a **pivotal objective**, that focus produces a cascade of favourable outcomes. Strategic leverage comes from three things: **anticipation**, insight into the **pivot point**, and **concentration** of effort.

## Why leverage matters

- A lever multiplies force only if applied where a small movement makes a big difference. Find the crucial pivot points and concentrate force on them.
- Examples of leveraged moments: knocking loose a keystone; turning a colleague's rough idea into the first draft that frames a whole negotiation; accepting a giant customer's contract while keeping the right to sell the same product to everyone else.

## Anticipation

- Insight into predictable aspects of others' behaviour that can be turned to advantage. In competitive strategy the key anticipations are **buyer demand** and **competitor reactions**.
- Rumelt's illustration: an automaker invested heavily in hybrid technology during a large-vehicle boom, anticipating (1) that fuel-economy pressure would make hybrids a major category and (2) that rivals, offered a licence, would license rather than build their own.
- The most critical anticipations concern rivals, including **anticipating their anticipations** (what they expect you to do and how they plan to exploit it).
- Sources of anticipation, none requiring psychic powers:
  - predictable **downstream effects** of events that have already happened ('a storm in the mountains today means floods on the plain tomorrow');
  - trends already at work and predictable economic or social dynamics;
  - **routines, habits, policies, inertias and constraints** of other agents (an incumbent will not respond if responding cannibalizes its core product).
- **Scenario trap**: a high/medium/low forecast makes people feel they have handled uncertainty, then they plan on 'medium'. The real risk is often a sequence: prices or demand rise, lure you into a large commitment, then collapse and strand your assets. Plan for the path, not the average.

Marketing anticipations to make explicit:

- How will the category leader respond to our free tier, price cut or bundle, given its revenue model?
- Which channel's costs will rise as competitors pile in (paid social auctions, marketplace fees)?
- What behaviour change is already under way among buyers (procurement rules, platform policy, regulation) whose downstream effects are predictable?

## Pivot points

- A pivot point is a **natural or created imbalance** where a relatively small adjustment unleashes much larger pent-up forces.
- Business pivots: **pent-up demand** not yet served; a **robust competence** built in one context that applies with force in another.
- Rumelt's illustration: a convenience-store chain saw that customers in one market were easily bored and sensitive to local taste, so it built rapid local-taste intelligence from store staff, quick-response merchandising teams and private-label production on small manufacturers' spare capacity. In another country the pivot was different: spotless stores and attentive service where customers were used to neither.
- In direct rivalry the pivot can be an imbalance between a rival's **position and its capabilities**, or between its **claims and reality**. A public challenge becomes powerful when the rival's stated story (openness, liberalization, 'customer obsession') visibly contradicts the facts.
- The pivot is an entrepreneurial insight into the situation, not a profit plan or set of financial goals.

## Concentration

- **Returns to concentration** arise when focusing on fewer, more limited objectives yields larger payoffs. They come from constraints and **threshold effects**:
  - limited resources (otherwise there would be no need to choose);
  - rivals who cannot instantly see and answer your moves (so temporary weaknesses can be exploited);
  - limited attention of leadership (a team cannot work five problems at once).
- **Threshold effect**: below a critical level of effort the system barely responds. Limit objectives to those your resources can push past the threshold.
  - Advertising has a threshold, so it can pay to **pulse** spend in concentrated bursts rather than spread it evenly, and to **roll out region by region**, concentrating spend where the product is new.
  - It is often better to **dominate a small segment** than to hold a sliver of a large market.
  - A clear benefit to a recognizable group beats a larger benefit spread thinly.
- **Psychological returns**: people ignore signals below a threshold (salience) and believe in momentum. Concentrated, visible wins (two transformed schools rather than a 2% gain across two hundred; three flagship customer stories rather than a small lift everywhere) change perceptions of what works and attract further support.
- **Scale the objective to the resources.** Rumelt describes a foundation with a large but finite annual budget that chose not to buy art (which would merely move it and raise prices) but to transform the study of art itself (cataloguing, teacher education, conservation research): an objective novel, sized to its resources and visibly affected by them.

## Worked example: a regional craft-beverage brand

The brand has a fixed annual media budget spread evenly over 12 months and 9 metro areas; awareness is flat everywhere.

- Threshold: media tests show no measurable lift below a certain weekly spend per metro; the current plan sits under it in every city.
- Anticipation: a national competitor's distributor contracts renew in spring in two metros, when shelf space is renegotiated.
- Pivot: strong on-premise relationships with bars (a competence) that the national brand lacks.
- Concentrated plan: two metros only, a six-week burst timed to the shelf resets, bar activations feeding retail demand, then the next two metros the following year. Visible wins become the story for distributors in the other metros.

## Workflow

1. List the user's resources honestly (budget, people, attention, time) and the candidate objectives.
2. Write explicit anticipations about buyers and rivals, including how rivals' routines and economics constrain their responses.
3. Hunt for pivot points: pent-up demand, transferable competences, gaps between a rival's claims and reality.
4. Estimate thresholds for each candidate (media, sales coverage, community presence) and drop objectives the resources cannot push past threshold.
5. Choose the pivotal objective whose success triggers further gains; prefer visible, salient wins.
6. Design the concentrated plan (timing bursts, sequencing regions or segments) and note what is deliberately left uncovered.
7. Stress-test with a path scenario (up then down), not just high/medium/low.

## Red flags

- Budget spread evenly across channels, regions and months 'to be safe'.
- Every segment gets a little attention; none reaches critical mass.
- Forecasts with three lines and a plan built on the middle one.
- No stated expectation of how competitors will react.
- Objectives too large for the resources (national awareness on a regional budget).
- Small, invisible improvements everywhere instead of a few visible wins.
