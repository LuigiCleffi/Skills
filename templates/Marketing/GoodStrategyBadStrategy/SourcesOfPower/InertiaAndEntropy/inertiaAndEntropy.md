# Inertia and Entropy

**Inertia** is an organization's unwillingness or inability to adapt to changed circumstances. **Entropy** is the tendency of weakly managed organizations to become less ordered and less focused even when nothing outside changes. Many successful strategies owe much to rivals' inertia, and an organization's greatest challenge is often its own inertia and entropy, making renewal itself the strategic problem: diagnose the causes, set a guiding policy for change, and design coherent actions that alter routines, culture and the structure of power.

## Three kinds of inertia

### 1. Inertia of routine

- Large organizations run on layers of standard procedures. Routines limit action to the familiar and also **filter how managers perceive issues**, preserving old categories.
- Sudden shocks (price spikes, new technology, deregulation) open a gap between old routines and the new regime.
- Rumelt's example: after airline deregulation, a carrier's 'planning model' predicted rising long-haul fares. It turned out to be cost plus a regulated markup at a regulated load factor, the routine once used to request fares from the regulator, not a market forecast. Other regulation-era rules of thumb ('long haul is where the money is', 're-equip when everyone else does', since the regulator once propped up prices) also persisted. Long-haul fares fell sharply while short, thin routes rose, as a simple cost-and-competition analysis had predicted.
- Aggressive speeches about a 'new competitive spirit' do not change routines: 'pure aggressiveness, unalloyed by craft'.
- **Fix**: obsolete routines can be fixed quickly once top management is convinced. Hire managers from firms with better methods, acquire such a firm, use outside help, or redesign routines; usually replace people invested in the old methods and reorganize around new information flows.

### 2. Inertia of culture

- Culture is the stable elements of social behaviour and meaning that strongly resist change. Organizational culture is more lightly held than national or religious culture, but it is dangerous to think it changes quickly.
- Rumelt's example: a telecom giant with world-class research had no competence at making consumer products; its research culture treated simple product prototypes as beneath it. A good product-market strategy was worthless because **the competences it assumed were absent and their development was blocked by culture**.
- **Breaking cultural inertia**, in order:
  1. **Simplify**: strip excess layers, halt non-essential operations (sell, close, spin off, outsource), disband coordinating committees and initiatives. Simplicity exposes obsolete units, waste and bad behaviour.
  2. **Fragment** separable units: breaks political coalitions, removes comfortable cross-subsidies, exposes more units to scrutiny.
  3. **Triage**: close some, repair some, use some as nuclei of the new structure, judged on **both performance and culture** (do not let a high-performing unit with a toxic culture infect others).
  4. **Repair** units by changing work norms. Norms are enforced daily by small groups that take their cue from the high-status member, the **alpha**; usually the alpha must be replaced by someone with different norms. A challenging goal speeds this up, its purpose being new habits rather than performance itself.
  5. Once units work well, **re-add essential coordination**, reversing some fragmentation.

### 3. Inertia by proxy

- Sometimes a firm does not respond because responding would undermine still-valuable profits that persist thanks to **its customers' inertia**.
- Examples from Rumelt: established banks that did not offer new high-interest accounts because their elderly depositors would not switch; phone companies that did not offer cheap broadband to businesses rather than cannibalize expensive leased lines, accepting a steady loss of share.
- Implication for attackers: you can poach customers **without triggering a response**, for now.
- Danger: inertia by proxy disappears **suddenly** when the incumbent decides adaptation matters more than old profits. Attackers can lose their profits overnight, made worse because the customers they won are self-selected as the most price-sensitive. Rapid attacker growth during the incumbent's sleep is not proof of superiority; investors and attackers were badly fooled in the broadband example.
- The attacker survives the awakening only if it has built **switching costs and loyalty** with the customers it acquired.

## Entropy

- Without constant maintenance, order fades: product lines blur, prices are set low to please sales, delivery schedules please only the factory, and bonuses reward winning internal contests over the fruits of luck and history.
- Much of every consultant's real work is undoing entropy. Planting a garden is more interesting than weeding it, but without weeding the garden disappears.

### Diagnosing entropy: the hump chart

- Assign profit (properly measured) to each separable unit: product, store, region, segment, customer, channel.
- Correct the measures first. Rumelt's garden-retail example: return figures mixed land bought decades apart (confusing retail with property gains) and excluded bonuses paid from company-wide profit, so strong stores subsidized weak ones. A corrected 'gain to operating' showed the chain earned far less than reported, and the best single store earned more than the whole chain.
- Rank units from best to worst and plot **cumulative profit**. With no cross-subsidies the bars rise smoothly. A **hump** (rise then sag) shows loss-makers dragging down the total; if units are separable and the hump persists over time, it signals a **lack of management**.
- Remedy: close the worst units, and fix the weak ones by finding why the best perform better and transferring those practices (in the example: store layouts that looked like gardens, plant descriptions and pairings, set-piece displays of materials, specialist staff by area). Earnings roughly doubled, with no innovation, 'just management'.

### Entropy in brand and product architecture

- Rumelt recounts how an automaker became the largest in the world partly through a coherent product policy: each brand owned a distinct price band, competing on quality against cheaper cars and on price against dearer ones, so there was no confusion between brands and little internal competition.
- Such a design needs constant maintenance. Decentralized divisions each gain by stretching up or down into sister brands' bands; corporate leadership must resist, or replace an obsolete design with a new way of directing competition outward.
- Decades later the lineup had decayed into **badge engineering** (the same vehicle under several names) and many models clustered at the same price point, versus a rival's two. Brands were eventually closed at great cost.
- **Internal competition is not a cleansing wind.** Spending to take business from a sister brand wastes advertising and development and pushes down both brands' prices; it can shrink the corporate pie.
- The hardest cases combine entropy with inertia of routine, culture and chain-linked systems.

## Template: hump chart worksheet

1. Choose the unit (product, SKU, location, customer segment, channel, brand).
2. Compute true contribution per unit: assign revenue and directly attributable costs, including incentives, capital at current value, support and marketing costs.
3. Rank units by contribution, best to worst.
4. Plot cumulative contribution; mark the peak and the units after it.
5. For units after the peak: close, fix (with best practices from top units) or reposition.

## Worked example: a consumer electronics accessories brand

- Hump chart by SKU (840 SKUs): the top 120 SKUs generate 140% of contribution; the tail of 400 SKUs destroys 45% through returns, warehousing and marketplace fees, hidden because fulfilment costs were averaged across all SKUs.
- Brand architecture: three sub-brands (value, mainstream, premium) now overlap at the same price points on marketplaces and outbid each other on the same search keywords.
- Rival assessment: the category leader sells mainly through big-box retail and has not matched our direct-to-consumer bundles, because doing so would upset its retail partners (inertia by proxy). Expect a response if our marketplace share passes a threshold that alarms retailers.
- Actions: cut 300 tail SKUs; give each sub-brand a non-overlapping price band and keyword set; build a subscription replacement programme (switching costs) before the leader responds.

## Workflow

1. For competitive moves, classify each rival's likely inertia (routine, culture, proxy) with evidence; estimate what it would cost them to respond and what might wake them.
2. Design attacks that exploit the inertia while building switching costs and loyalty before the incumbent responds.
3. For the user's own organization, look for routines, models and rules of thumb inherited from an earlier regime; test whether they still fit.
4. Check whether planned strategies assume competences the culture blocks; if so, plan renewal (simplify, fragment, triage, replace alphas) before or alongside the strategy.
5. Build a hump chart for products, customers, channels or locations with corrected measures.
6. Map brand and price architecture; find overlaps, badge-engineered duplicates and internal competition.
7. Recommend pruning, best-practice transfer and an architecture with clear boundaries, plus who will maintain it.

## Red flags

- Forecasts or pricing produced by a legacy model nobody has examined since the market changed.
- Leadership rhetoric about agility with unchanged planning, pricing and marketing routines.
- A strategy that relies on capabilities the organization has never shown.
- Attackers celebrating rapid share gains from a sleeping incumbent with no lock-in.
- Profit reported only in aggregate; incentives paid from company profit regardless of unit performance.
- Sub-brands or tiers at the same price point bidding against each other.
- A long tail of products or locations kept because 'they contribute to overhead'.
