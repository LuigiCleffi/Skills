# Using Your Head

The obstacle common to every strategic situation is our own **myopia**: limited attention and a tendency to seize the first idea. Being strategic largely means being **less myopic** than your undeliberative self and than rivals, working from facts on the ground rather than pretending to see the future. This skill, judging your own judgments, matters more than any single framework.

## Ask the obvious questions

- Rumelt recalls a manager who called a plain interview the most useful conversation of his year. Simply asking about purpose, competition, strengths, weaknesses and the hardest management issues forced him to reflect and bring his priorities forward.
- Use this in stakeholder interviews and discovery: open with 'what are you trying to accomplish here?', then competition, strengths and weaknesses, and the most difficult issues. Listen and write it down.

## Make a list

- A well-known anecdote has a consultant tell an industrialist to list the ten most important things he could do and start on number one. The value is in **constructing** the list.
- Attention is a flashlight: lighting one subject darkens another. People forget larger purposes under the pull of immediate events.
- The useful list sits at the **intersection of what is important and what is actionable**. It counters forgetfulness, forces you to weigh urgency and importance, and turns 'things to worry about' into 'things to do now'.

## Quick closure and its traps

- Asked for a recommendation on a complex case, most experienced managers pick a problem area, grab the **first solution** that comes to mind, never revisit the problem definition and never develop a second option.
- That is efficient for daily life, but strategy issues are important and ill-structured, so quick closure is risky.
- Why we do it: ill-structured problems are disorienting, like being dropped into choppy water; the first idea is a relief. Letting it go means losing orientation and risking coming up empty, so we spend effort **justifying** our first idea rather than **questioning** it, without noticing the dodge.
- **Intuition**: fast pattern-matching judgments can be excellent (especially about people), but instincts also wrongly tell us they are always right. People are reliably poor at judging likelihoods (vivid examples outweigh statistics), their own competence relative to others, and cause and effect (seeing patterns in randomness, causes in associations, ignoring conflicting data). Decide which situations need deeper reflection: is a rival or nature setting a trap, or can we set one?
- Experienced executives rightly sense that real strategy problems cannot be solved by formal decision analysis and need judgment; the fix is not more analysis machinery but **generating and testing alternative judgments**.

## Three habits

Knowledge and experience (patterns of what works, and sometimes causal theories) are necessary but not sufficient. Cultivate:

1. Tools for fighting myopia and guiding attention.
2. The ability to question your own judgment: if your reasoning cannot survive a vigorous attack, it will not survive real competition.
3. The habit of making and recording judgments so you can improve.

## Technique 1: the kernel as a completeness check

- Insight never arrives as a full strategy; it strikes one element of the kernel: an **action** (unbundle a costly component and charge heavy users), a **guiding policy** (shift focus to a different channel segment), or a **diagnosis** (the product is loved by consumers but a threat to the industry it depends on).
- A local insight is welcome, but a strategy is an internally consistent argument from facts to diagnosis to guiding policy to action. Use the kernel to **expand thinking to all three elements**.
- Rumelt prefers to work **problem-first**: when a client asks for an appraisal of an action, back up and build a better diagnosis before recommending.

## Technique 2: problem-solution

- Many strategies lack a diagnosis. Work **backward** from what is being done to **why**: what obstacle does this choice address?
- Shifting from directions to problems shows the full set of **competing solutions** and how changes in factors would alter which strategies work.
- Rumelt's example: a consumer device for recording and time-shifting TV. Framed as 'time-shifting', competitors are other recorders, on-demand services and box sets. Framed as 'content distributors control the set-top box through bundling', the real obstacle is industry structure, which suggests spending less on marketing and more on a legal or regulatory attack, with larger allies.
- Marketing use: reverse-engineer a competitor's campaign or product move into the problem it is solving; then ask what other solutions to that problem exist, including ones that change the structure rather than outspend.

## Technique 3: create-destroy

- When asked for alternatives, people add shallow ones: a tweak of the first idea, a straw man, or generic options like 'walk away' or 'more study'.
- A genuine new alternative should **flow from reconsidering the facts** and **address the weaknesses** of existing ones. That requires trying hard to **destroy** each alternative, exposing its fault lines and contradictions.
- **Virtual panel of experts**: imagine specific people whose judgment you value (mentors, executives, authors) and ask each 'what is wrong with this? what would you do?' Humans are better at modelling personalities than abstract frameworks, so imagined critics give richer feedback. Do this before showing ideas to others.
- Panel critiques in Rumelt's example converged on a lesson: good strategies are usually **corner solutions**, emphasizing focus over compromise, not trying to be all things to all people (license the software rather than make boxes; or integrate deeply with a platform; but stop doing conflicting things that compete with platform partners).

## Technique 4: practise judgment by pre-commitment

- Most important judgments in business are about people: yourself, individuals, small groups, and large groups and markets (the marketer's domain).
- Judgment improves with practice, but only if you **commit judgments to writing first**. Otherwise hindsight convinces you that you 'thought of that'.
- Committing to a diagnosis means choosing which issues are critical and an implied action. It raises the chance you will disagree with others, and so the chance of learning.
- Before any meeting, predict the issues, who will take which position, and your own view; compare afterwards.

## Template: strategy thinking worksheet

```text
1. Purpose: what are we trying to accomplish?
2. Ten-item list (important ∩ actionable), ranked; #1 is:
3. First insight (which kernel element is it?):
4. Fill the kernel: diagnosis / guiding policy / actions:
5. Problem-solution: what obstacle does the current or rival approach address?
   Other solutions to that obstacle (including structural ones):
6. Alternatives (at least 3 robust ones from the facts):
   A:            B:            C:
7. Destroy each: fault lines, contradictions, rival responses:
8. Virtual panel: critic 1 says... critic 2 says... critic 3 says...
9. Corner solution chosen (what we focus on, what we give up):
10. Pre-committed prediction (date, metric, expected result):
```

## Worked example: a regional bank's deposit-growth problem

- First insight (action): 'raise the savings rate'. Kernel check: no diagnosis.
- Problem-solution: rivals' high-rate accounts address rate-shopping customers; digital-only banks address convenience. The bank's own deposit loss is concentrated among small businesses moving to fintechs that integrate with accounting tools: an integration problem, not a rate problem.
- Alternatives: (A) match rates (costly, invites a price war); (B) integrate business accounts with the top accounting and payroll tools and market through local accountants; (C) partner with a fintech under the bank's charter.
- Destroy: A hands margin to the most rate-sensitive customers, who leave again; C risks losing the customer relationship; B needs IT work the bank has never done quickly.
- Panel: a former payments CEO would ask whether the bank can ship integrations in a quarter; a community-banking veteran would point out accountants already trust the bank's local bankers.
- Corner solution: B, with integrations bought from a vendor and accountants as the channel; rates unchanged.
- Pre-commitment: 'By Q3, 300 business accounts opened through accountant referrals; deposit outflow from small businesses halves.'

## Workflow

1. Interview stakeholders with the obvious questions; capture purpose, competition, strengths, weaknesses and hardest issues.
2. Have the team make a ranked list of important and actionable items.
3. Record the first insights and classify them by kernel element; fill the missing elements.
4. Run problem-solution on the user's and competitors' current approaches.
5. Generate at least three robust alternatives from the facts; run create-destroy on each, using a named virtual panel.
6. Choose a corner solution and state what is given up.
7. Write down predictions and the diagnosis; schedule a review to compare outcomes.

## Red flags

- One recommendation considered; 'alternatives' are tweaks, straw men or 'do nothing'.
- The problem area is never revisited once picked.
- Arguments spent defending the first idea; critics treated as opponents.
- Gut calls on big, complex, irreversible decisions (mergers, repositioning, market exits).
- Recommendations that compromise across everything instead of choosing a corner.
- No written predictions, so nobody learns whether judgment was good.
