# Building Strong Brands

A brand is a name, term, sign, symbol or design (or a combination) that identifies one seller's offering and differentiates it from competitors, creating value beyond the product and service themselves. Brands live in customers' minds, so build them by creating strong, favorable and unique associations through every contact, organize them into a deliberate hierarchy, extend and reposition them with care, and protect them when crises hit. Treat marketing spend as an investment in customer brand knowledge: its quality matters more than its quantity.

## Strategic brand management

Four steps:

1. Identify and establish the brand positioning.
2. Plan and implement brand marketing.
3. Measure and interpret brand performance.
4. Grow and sustain brand value.

## How branding works

- Branding endows products and services with the power of a brand: it tells customers 'who' and 'what' the product is and why they should care, and builds mental structures that simplify decisions.
- Branding works only if customers believe there are meaningful differences among brands in the category. Differences can be product-related (continuous innovation) or non-product-related (imagery around motivations and desires).
- Successful brands are seen as genuine and authentic and become an indispensable part of customers' lives.
- Anything with a choice can be branded: goods, services, stores, places, organizations, people, sports teams, ideas.

### The roles of brands

For consumers:

- A **promise** that sets expectations and reduces risk; in return for loyalty, the firm delivers a predictably positive experience (even 'predictably unpredictable', if that is what customers expect).
- The same product is valued differently depending on the brand on it.
- A shortcut that simplifies decisions in busy lives.
- A source of personal meaning and identity: brands express who people are or want to be, and can be treated almost like people. Brand relationships can strengthen or weaken with every word and action.

For firms:

- Simplify product handling, inventory and accounting.
- Signal quality so satisfied buyers can easily rebuy.
- Loyalty gives predictable demand, barriers to entry and willingness to pay a premium.
- Competitors can copy processes and designs but not years of accumulated impressions in customers' minds.
- Valuable legal property that can be bought, sold and licensed, securing future revenue. Firms may not see how strong loyalty is until they change a core element (Kotler cites a famous formula change that blind tests favored but loyal drinkers rejected because the research measured taste, not attachment).

## Brand equity and brand power

| Concept | What it is | How to measure |
|---|---|---|
| **Brand equity** | The monetary value of the brand: the premium on a company's valuation from owning it; NPV of the brand's lifetime financial returns. A subset of goodwill (which also includes patents, know-how, licenses, culture). | **Cost approach**: historical cost or replacement cost of building the brand. **Market approach**: revenue of the branded offering minus an identical unbranded one, net of brand-building cost. **Financial approach**: NPV of future cash flows attributable to the brand, adjusted for risk. Use several. |
| **Brand power** (customer-based brand equity) | The differential effect brand knowledge has on customer response to the brand's marketing. Positive if customers react more favorably when the brand is identified; negative if less. No difference means a commodity competing on price. | **Indirect**: track brand knowledge (sources of equity). **Direct**: measure the impact of brand knowledge on response to marketing. Use a **brand audit** (focused assessment of brand health and sources of equity, at planning time or before a strategic shift, ideally annually) and **brand tracking** (repeated quantitative measures over time, built from the audit). |

Benefits of brand power:

- Better perceived product performance; greater loyalty.
- Less vulnerability to competitors and crises.
- Larger margins; inelastic response to price rises, elastic response to price cuts.
- More trade cooperation; more effective communications.
- Licensing and extension opportunities; better recruiting and retention; better financial returns.

Customers' brand knowledge decides where the brand may go: they grant or withhold 'permission' for new products (extensions that felt wrong for the brand have failed even with strong parents).

Brand valuation (estimating total financial value) is distinct from brand equity management; for famous companies brand value is often over half of market capitalization.

## Designing the brand

### The brand mantra

A three-to-five-word internal articulation of the heart and soul of the brand, close to 'brand essence' or 'core brand promise'. It guides every employee and partner, filters out brand-inappropriate actions (from product launches to how phones are answered), and differs from the external slogan that expresses it.

Three criteria:

- **Communicate** what is unique; may also define the category and set brand boundaries.
- **Simplify**: short, crisp, vivid.
- **Inspire**: personally meaningful to as many employees as possible.

Examples from the chapter: 'authentic athletic performance' (behind the slogan 'Just Do It'), 'fun family entertainment'. For brands expecting growth, define the benefit space you will compete in; in stable categories, focus on points of difference. No competitor should excel on the whole combination.

### Choosing brand elements

Brand elements (names, logos, symbols, characters, slogans, jingles, packaging, URLs) identify and differentiate the brand and can be trademarked. Test: what would customers think or feel if this element were all they knew?

| Criterion | Question | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Memorable | Easily recalled and recognized, at purchase and consumption? | Brand-building |
| Meaningful | Evocative of the category or benefit? | Brand-building |
| Likable | Aesthetically appealing, fun? | Brand-building |
| Transferable | Works for new categories, geographies, segments? (A broad name allowed an online bookseller to sell everything.) | Defensive |
| Adaptable | Can it be updated over time? | Defensive |
| Protectable | Legally and competitively? Guard against becoming generic. | Defensive |

- Low-involvement buyers need elements that are easy to recall and inherently descriptive and persuasive; but a name with strong built-in meaning is harder to reposition later.
- Symbols often capture intangibles (strength, security, care).
- **Slogans** are efficient 'hooks'. Do not discard a good one lightly; replacements often fail and get reverted.
- Protect names (trademarks), processes (patents) and packaging (copyright, proprietary design).
- **Brand characters** (animated or live mascots) add likability and personality, break through clutter, communicate benefits softly, do not misbehave like celebrities, and work well in social media. Set usage rules for them (some brands issue a character 'passport' defining what it never does).

### Choosing secondary associations

Link the brand to other entities that already carry meaning in memory:

- **Company** (branding strategy), **country or region** (origin), **channels** (where sold).
- **Other brands** (ingredient or cobranding), **characters** (licensing), **endorsers**, **events and causes** (sponsorship), **third parties** (awards, reviews, ratings), **employees**, **extensions**.

These are efficient but risky: anything bad that happens to the linked entity rubs off (endorser scandals). Associations must fit the brand personality.

### Brand personality

The mix of human traits attributed to a brand; customers choose brands whose personality matches their own. Five dimensions (US research):

- **Sincerity**: down to earth, honest, wholesome, cheerful.
- **Excitement**: daring, spirited, imaginative, up to date.
- **Competence**: reliable, intelligent, successful.
- **Sophistication**: upper-class, charming.
- **Ruggedness**: outdoorsy, tough.

Dimensions vary by culture (peacefulness and passion appear in some countries; culture-specific factors in others), so validate locally.

## Brand hierarchy

### Brand portfolios

The brand portfolio is the set of all brands and brand lines a firm sells in a category or segment. Reasons for multiple brands: one brand can stretch only so far; different segments; more shelf presence and retailer dependence; retaining variety seekers; internal competition; scale economies.

- The optimal portfolio maximizes each brand's equity in combination with the others. If dropping brands raises profit, it is too big; if adding brands does, too small.
- Principle: **maximize market coverage, minimize brand overlap**. Each brand must be clearly differentiated and serve a segment big enough to justify its costs (a 'high-touch' brand and a 'no-frills' brand for the same material; entry, mainstream, premium and super-premium brands in one drink category).
- Monitor and prune weak, undifferentiated brands that cannibalize and dilute.

Three portfolio strategies:

| Strategy | What | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **House of brands** | Individual or separate family names | A failure does not taint other brands; fits very different products and quality tiers | Every brand must be built and funded separately |
| **Branded house** | Corporate umbrella name on everything | Lower launch cost, instant recognition, corporate associations (innovative, expert, trustworthy) transfer; more intangible value | Negative spillover if one product disappoints |
| **Sub-brand** | Corporate or family brand + product brand | Parent legitimizes, sub-brand individualizes | Must decide which part to emphasize |

These are points on a continuum. B2B groups sometimes keep strong subsidiary names in market and promote the parent only to investors and opinion leaders.

- **Flagship product**: the product that best embodies the brand (first famous product, best-seller, award winner). Marketing it pays twice: short-term sales and long-term equity for the range.
- **Branded variants**: specific lines supplied to specific retailers or channels, in response to retailer demand for exclusivity.

### Cobranding

Two or more brands marketed together. Forms: same-company, joint-venture, multiple-sponsor, retail (shared locations).

- Advantages: more convincing positioning, sales from existing and new customers and channels, lower launch cost, faster adoption, learning about partners' customers.
- Risks: loss of control; high expectations, so failure hurts both; overexposure if a brand co-brands too often.
- Requirements: both brands have equity (awareness and positive image); a **logical fit**; complementary, not redundant; fit in values, capabilities and goals; balanced equity; detailed contracts, finances (often licensing fee and royalty) and coordinated marketing.
- Questions to ask: What capabilities do we lack? What resource constraints do we face? What are our strategic goals? Does it strengthen or dilute equity? Does it bring knowledge transfer?

### Ingredient branding

A special case: building equity for materials, components or parts inside other branded products. Useful when host brands are weak. Self-branded ingredients (a hotel's named signature bed) give more control and can create a halo. Requirements:

1. Customers believe the ingredient matters to the end product's performance (ideally visibly).
2. Customers are convinced it is superior, through coordinated communication with manufacturers and retailers.
3. A distinctive symbol or logo signals the ingredient, working like a simple, versatile seal of quality.

Done well, customers refuse host products without it and manufacturers pay a premium for it.

## The brand value chain

How marketing investment becomes shareholder value:

| Stage | Contents | Multiplier to next stage |
|---|---|---|
| Marketing program investment | Product, communications, trade, employees, other | **Program multiplier**: distinctiveness, relevance, integration, value, excellence of the program |
| Customer mind-set | Awareness, associations, attitudes, attachment, activity | **Customer multiplier**: competitive reactions, channel support, customer size and profile |
| Brand performance | Price premiums, price elasticities, market share, expansion success, cost structure, profitability | **Market multiplier**: market dynamics, growth potential, risk profile, brand contribution (investors' view) |
| Shareholder value | Stock price, P/E, market capitalization | |

Use it to diagnose where value leaks: a great program that does not move mind-set, or mind-set that does not convert because competitors or channels block it.

## Brand dynamics

### Repositioning

1. Understand the original sources of brand equity. Are positive associations losing strength or uniqueness? Have negative associations attached?
2. Decide to keep the positioning or create a new one.

- **Back to basics**: when the marketing program failed to deliver the promise; return to the roots, product performance, iconic packaging and authenticity.
- **Reinvention**: when the old positioning is no longer viable; overhaul product and imagery. Reviving a brand that is alive but forgotten is easier than one that is disliked.
- Most repositionings sit between; change enough to attract new customers without alienating old ones (Kotler's heritage fashion example removed an overused signature pattern from most products, refocused on core luxury items and modernized digital content).

### Brand extensions

- **Brand extension**: established brand used for a new product in a different category or price tier.
- **Sub-brand**: new brand combined with the existing one. The existing brand is the **parent brand**; with many extensions it is a **master or family brand**.
- **Line extension**: parent brand on a new product within a category it already serves (flavors, forms, sizes).
- Most new products each year are extensions.

When to extend: judge how well the extension leverages parent equity and how well it contributes back. Ask: Does the parent have significant power? Is there a strong basis of fit? Will the extension have optimal POPs and PODs? How can the program build extension equity? What are the implications for parent equity and profitability?

Research insights:

- Success needs favorable parent associations and perceived fit (attributes, benefits, usage situations or user types).
- A brand that is prototypical of its category is hard to extend outside it; associations positive in one category can turn negative in another.
- Concrete attribute associations extend less well than abstract benefit associations.
- Vertical extensions usually need sub-brands: upward to avoid negative associations, downward to avoid dilution and cannibalization.
- A common mistake is judging fit on one association while ignoring the rest of customers' brand knowledge (a disposable-goods brand selling perfume on 'convenience and value' lacked any cachet).

Advantages:

- Positive expectations reduce risk; retailers are easier to convince.
- The launch campaign promotes only the new product, not the brand; there is no naming cost; packaging and labeling efficiencies; a shelf 'billboard' effect.
- Variety seekers stay in the brand family.
- Extensions can clarify the brand's meaning and core values, raise loyalty, and pave the way for further extensions.

Disadvantages:

- The name becomes less strongly identified with any one product.
- **Brand dilution**: customers stop associating the brand with a specific set of products and think less of it.
- Inappropriate extensions make customers question integrity or become confused about which version is right; retailers lack shelf space; the firm gets overstretched.
- **Cannibalization** of parent sales (acceptable if preemptive, taking sales that would otherwise go to competitors).
- The firm forgoes creating a new brand with its own equity.

### Managing a brand crisis

Assume one will happen. Crises reduce sales and marketing effectiveness and raise sensitivity to rivals.

- Strong brands with credibility and trustworthiness weather crises better; preparation matters.
- The response must be **swift and sincere**; customers must sense the company truly cares. Delay lets media and word of mouth fill the vacuum.
- Acknowledge the impact publicly and take the necessary steps (refusing a recall made one company look uncaring and cost share).
- Avoid shifting explanations; a long absence from shelves lets customers find substitutes.
- Show beyond doubt that the problem is solved (a visible fix, such as tamper-proof packaging).

For external crises (recession, disaster, pandemic), four guidelines:

- **Empathy**: get closer to customers; what are they thinking, feeling, doing differently, and is it permanent?
- **Value**: present the most compelling total value proposition, including savings in time, money, energy and stress.
- **Strategy**: stay authentic and true to the brand promise while meeting short-term needs.
- **Innovation**: run 'Stop, Start, Continue (but improve)' exercises; prune and focus; rethink budgets, go-to-market and targets.

## Luxury branding

- Luxury brands are priced well above category norms. Luxury has shifted from status to style, substance, personal pleasure and self-expression.
- Common denominators: **quality and uniqueness**; winning ingredients are craftsmanship, heritage, authenticity and history.
- Principles:
  - Align every decision (product, service, price, sales incentives, communication, distribution) so purchase and use match the brand image.
  - Create a premium, aspirational image.
  - Luxury brands span categories, so define competitors broadly.
  - Protect identity aggressively against infringement and counterfeits.
  - Align all identifiers (name, logo, symbols, packaging) and associations (personality, events, countries).
- Wrap experiences around products (workshops, designer meetings, driving schools, invitations to events).
- Digital: luxury brands must deliver an online experience matching their stores, blend physical and digital, and use owned e-commerce partly to fight fakes.
- Balance classic and contemporary, continuity and change.
- Luxury means different things in different countries; growth markets move from logo-driven to quality- and design-driven buying.

## The brand positioning bull's-eye

A fill-in framework (from the chapter's insight box), from the center out:

1. **Brand mantra** (center): the essence and core brand promise.
2. **Points of parity and points of difference**: specific but not narrow, stated as benefits customers actually get; they depend on the competitive frame.
3. **Substantiators (reasons to believe)**: factual or demonstrable support for the POPs and PODs (supply chain, training, proprietary technology).
4. **Outer ring**:
   - **Values, personality, character**: intangible tone.
   - **Executional properties and visual identity**: name, logo, colors, other tangible cues.

Worked example (B2B SaaS, an expense-management tool for mid-size firms):

| Ring | Content |
|---|---|
| Mantra | Effortless, trustworthy spend control |
| PODs | Receipts captured and matched with no manual entry; policy enforced before spend, not after |
| POPs | Integrates with major accounting systems; fair per-seat price; SOC 2 security |
| Substantiators | 99% automatic match rate in an independent audit; native ERP connectors; card issuing with real-time limits |
| Personality | Calm, competent, plain-spoken |
| Visual identity | Name, checkmark-shield logo, deep teal palette, no stock photos of handshakes |

Brand architecture decision for a new payroll product: the master brand carries trust and finance credibility, payroll fits the 'spend control' benefit space, so use a sub-brand ('[Brand] Payroll') rather than a new name; reject a proposed consumer budgeting app as outside the mantra.

## Workflow

1. Clarify the target, competitive frame and positioning; then write or check the brand mantra against communicate, simplify and inspire.
2. Fill in the bull's-eye: POPs, PODs, substantiators, personality and visual identity.
3. Audit brand elements on the six criteria; check trademark availability and the risk of becoming generic; set usage rules for characters and slogans.
4. List candidate secondary associations (endorsers, partners, origin, channels, events, reviews); check fit with personality and downside risk.
5. Map the brand hierarchy: house of brands, branded house or sub-brands; name the flagship; flag overlapping or weak brands.
6. For any cobrand or ingredient brand, check equity of both sides, logical fit, complementarity, contracts and the three ingredient-branding requirements.
7. For a new product, run the extension questions; choose master brand, sub-brand or new brand; estimate dilution and cannibalization.
8. Measure: run a brand audit, set up tracking on awareness, associations, attitudes, attachment and activity; use the brand value chain to locate leaks.
9. If the brand is tired, diagnose the lost sources of equity and pick a point on the back-to-basics to reinvention continuum.
10. Keep a crisis plan ready: who speaks, how fast, what the visible fix would be; apply the empathy, value, strategy, innovation guidelines in external crises.

## Red flags

- No brand mantra, or a mantra that is really an ad slogan and fits any competitor.
- A name that describes today's product so narrowly that it blocks future growth, or that cannot be trademarked.
- A beloved slogan, logo or pack replaced because the team was bored of it.
- An extension justified by one shared association while the rest of the brand image clashes.
- A downscale version under the master brand with no sub-brand; an upscale version without credibility.
- Many brands targeting the same segment and cannibalizing each other.
- Co-branding with a partner whose equity is weak or whose values conflict; the brand co-branded so often it means nothing.
- An ingredient claim customers do not believe matters, with no seal or explanation.
- Brand spend measured only by volume of impressions, with no audit or tracking of associations.
- A crisis response that is slow, defensive or keeps changing its story.
- A luxury brand discounted, over-distributed or with an online experience far below its stores.
- Brand personality copy or imagery that contradicts what customers actually experience (inauthentic brand).
