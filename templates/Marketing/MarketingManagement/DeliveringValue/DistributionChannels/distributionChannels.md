# Designing and Managing Distribution Channels

Value that is created but not delivered is wasted. Treat distribution as a designed system: decide which channel functions must be performed for each target segment, who performs each one most efficiently, and how the channels fit together without destructive conflict. Channel choices are long-term commitments that shape pricing, sales force, communications and brand, so make them from the customer's buying needs backward, and revisit them as the market and the product life cycle move.

## What channels are and why intermediaries exist

A **marketing (trade, distribution) channel** is a set of interdependent organizations that make a product or service available for use or consumption; the pathway the offering follows after production until the end user buys and uses it.

Three kinds of intermediary:

- **Merchants** buy, take title and resell (wholesalers, retailers, distributors).
- **Agents** find customers and may negotiate for the producer but do not take title (brokers, manufacturers' reps, sales agents).
- **Facilitators** assist without taking title or negotiating (carriers, warehouses, banks, payment providers, agencies).

Producers delegate selling because intermediaries' contacts, experience, specialization and scale usually make the product more available, more efficiently, than the producer could alone. Channels must not only serve markets, they must also **make** them: convert potential buyers into profitable customers.

## Channel functions and flows

Someone must perform each of these; the only question is who:

- Gather information on customers, competitors and the environment.
- Develop and spread persuasive communications.
- Negotiate price and terms so ownership can transfer.
- Place orders with manufacturers.
- Finance inventory at each level.
- Assume the risks of channel work.
- Offer buyers financing and facilitate payment.
- Transfer ownership.

Every function consumes scarce resources, can often be done better by a specialist, and can be **shifted** among members. Shifting a function to an intermediary lowers the producer's cost but the intermediary charges for it; shifting it to the customer (self-service, pickup, self-install) should lower the price further.

Five flows run through the channel: **physical, title, payment, information, promotion**. Some flow forward (storage and movement, title, communications), some backward (ordering, payment), and some both ways (information, negotiation, finance, risk).

## Channel levels

- **Zero-level (direct)**: producer to end customer (own website, app, own stores, direct sales force, door-to-door, home parties, catalogs).
- **One-level**: one intermediary (retailer, or in B2B an industrial distributor or rep).
- **Two-level and beyond**: e.g. wholesaler plus retailer; some markets have many more levels.
- The more levels, the harder it is to see end users and control the experience.
- **Reverse-flow channels** handle reuse, refurbishment, recycling and disposal (returns, take-back, trade-in, resale). Design them deliberately; they are part of the offer.

## Multichannel distribution

Use two or more channels to reach different segments, or different need states of the same buyer, in one market.

Benefits:

- **Coverage**: more customers in more places; multichannel customers tend to spend more than single-channel ones (the effect is stronger for hedonic than functional products).
- **Lower cost to serve**: online, phone or inside sales for small accounts instead of field sales.
- **Customized selling**: add a technical or specialist channel for complex needs.

Integrate the channels by function, not just in parallel. In B2B: marketing presells (content, ads, email), generates and scores leads (events, inside sales), and the field rep enters only to close. Size channels to account size: direct sales for large accounts, digital or inside sales for midsize, distributors for small, and settle in advance who gets credit for which account.

Trade-off: every new channel adds conflict and control problems, and channels can end up competing for the same customer. Ways to win intermediaries' acceptance of an online channel:

1. Offer different brands, models or SKUs online and offline.
2. Pay offline partners higher commissions to cushion the impact.
3. Take orders online but route fulfillment and payment to a local partner (Kotler cites a motorcycle maker that had web buyers pick a dealer, who shipped or held the order).

Decide how much of the line goes in each channel: the web can show the whole range; stores, catalogs and partners may carry a curated subset. Make information (ratings, reviews, stock availability) consistent across touchpoints so customers can move between them.

## Channel-management decisions

Sequence: analyze customer needs, set objectives and constraints, identify and evaluate alternatives, then select, train, motivate and evaluate members, and modify over time.

### Channel objectives

State them as: which customers, which **service output levels**, at what cost and support level. Segment by desired service and pick the best channel for each.

Inputs that shape objectives:

- **Customer goals**: price, assortment, convenience; economic, social or experiential shopping goals. The same person may trade up in one category and down in another, or research in one channel and buy in another.
- **Product characteristics**: bulky goods need short handling distances; custom or nonstandard products and high-unit-value equipment go through direct sales; products needing installation or maintenance go through the company or franchised dealers.
- **Environment**: in downturns buyers want shorter, cheaper channels; competition law limits arrangements that substantially lessen competition.
- **Competitors**: rivals' channel presence is a signal (and sometimes a reason) to enter; and when no existing channel delivers the desired experience, build your own (Kotler uses a consumer-electronics brand's own stores as the example).

### Channel alternatives

Each channel has strengths and weaknesses: sales forces handle complexity but cost most; online is cheap but weaker for complex products; distributors create sales but cut the producer off from customers; shared manufacturers' reps are economical but less intense.

Three coverage strategies by number of intermediaries:

| Strategy | Use when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Exclusive** | You need knowledgeable, dedicated resellers and a close partnership (luxury, new cars, some appliances, complex equipment) | Exclusive territories are fine; restricting where a dealer may sell can be a legal issue |
| **Selective** | You want competent, service-capable outlets without blanket coverage | Outlets compete for the same customers; select on service ability |
| **Intensive** | Frequent, convenience or impulse purchases bought in many places | Moving down from exclusive/selective for short-term volume invites price wars, erodes margins, reseller interest and brand equity |

Selection criteria for members: years in business, other lines carried, growth and profit record, financial strength, cooperativeness, service reputation; for agents, the other lines they carry and sales force size and quality; for stores, location, growth potential and clientele. To customers, **the channel is the company**: a dirty or inept outlet damages the brand.

Commitments reduce adaptability, so build in flexibility. Producers can generally drop dealers for cause, but not for refusing a doubtful legal arrangement (e.g. exclusive dealing or tying).

### Franchising

A tightly knit system whose operations are planned, directed and controlled by the franchisor. Three characteristics:

- The franchisor owns a trade or service mark and licenses it for royalties.
- The franchisee pays to join (start-up costs, fees, a share of sales).
- The franchisor provides a system for doing business (training, operating procedures, purchasing rules).

Franchisors gain motivated owner-operators, local knowledge and purchasing power; franchisees get a proven model, a known brand, easier financing and support. Tension between independence and loyalty is permanent; decide explicitly how much local freedom (menu, naming, pricing) you grant. Formats: manufacturer-sponsored retailer franchise (car dealers), manufacturer-sponsored wholesaler franchise (licensed bottlers), service-firm-sponsored retailer franchise (rental cars, fast food, hotels), and **dual distribution** (some units owned, others franchised).

### Motivating channel members

Treat intermediaries like customers: learn their needs and design a channel offering that gives them superior value (training, certification, market research, capability building, marketing support). Buyers at powerful retailers accept new items on strong evidence of consumer acceptance, a credible advertising and promotion plan, and financial incentives; bring all three.

**Channel power** is the ability to make members do what they otherwise would not. Five bases:

- **Coercive**: threaten to withdraw resources or terminate. Works but breeds resentment and countervailing power.
- **Reward**: extra benefit for specific acts. Better than coercion, but partners come to expect payment for every behavior.
- **Legal (legitimate)**: request what the contract warrants; works while you are seen as a legitimate leader.
- **Expert**: knowledge partners value; decays once they learn it, so keep developing new expertise.
- **Referent**: partners are proud to be associated with you.

Coercive and reward power are observable; legal, expert and referent are subjective. Relying only on margins, deals, allowances and threats is crude stimulus-response thinking; aim for partnership.

### Channel partnerships

- **Conventional channel**: independent producer, wholesaler and retailer each maximizing their own profit, even at the system's expense; no one controls the others.
- **Vertical marketing system (VMS)**: members act as a unified system under a **channel captain (steward)** who owns, franchises or has the power to align the others; the steward coordinates by persuading members to act in the system's interest, enlarging customer value and weeding out weak members. Three types:
  - **Corporate**: successive stages under single ownership (a paint maker owning its stores).
  - **Administered**: coordination through one member's size and power (dominant brands get displays, shelf space and promotion compliance); the advanced form is **distribution programming**, a planned, professionally managed system serving both sides.
  - **Contractual**: independent firms integrate by contract: wholesaler-sponsored voluntary chains, retailer cooperatives, franchise organizations.
- **Horizontal marketing system**: two or more unrelated firms pool resources to exploit an opportunity none can or will tackle alone (bank branches in supermarkets, co-located services, joint ventures).

Competition is increasingly between whole systems, not individual firms.

### Evaluating and modifying channels

- Measure members against standards: quota attainment, average inventory, delivery time, handling of damaged and lost goods, cooperation in promotion and training.
- Pay **functional discounts**: specified amounts for each agreed service actually performed, so you do not overpay for work not done.
- Counsel, retrain, motivate or terminate underperformers.
- To add or drop a member, do an incremental analysis: what are sales and profits with and without this intermediary?
- Channels evolve: early buyers pay for high-value-added channels; later buyers move to low-cost ones. No channel strategy lasts the whole life cycle; review when the channel underperforms, buying patterns shift, the market expands, new competitors or channel formats appear, or the product ages.

## Channel conflict

**Channel conflict** occurs when one member's actions prevent another from reaching its goal. The aim is to manage it, not to eliminate it; some conflict drives adaptation, too much is dysfunctional.

Types:

- **Horizontal**: same level (one franchisee's poor service hurts the others).
- **Vertical**: different levels (the producer sells directly to a wholesaler's biggest customer; a brand opens a web store and department stores cut its shelf space).
- **Multichannel**: two or more channels sell to the same market, especially when one gets a lower price or works on lower margins.

Causes: goal incompatibility (penetration pricing versus dealer margins), different strategies and tactics (optimistic versus pessimistic inventory views), power imbalance (retailer consolidation or dealer dependence), unclear roles and rights (territories, account ownership, credit for sales).

Resolution mechanisms, roughly from soft to hard:

- **Strategic justification**: show the channels serve distinct segments; reinforce with **branded variants** (models exclusive to a channel).
- **Dual compensation**: pay existing partners for sales through new channels (e.g. a service commission to agents on online-quoted customers).
- **Superordinate goals**: agree on a shared goal, most easily against an outside threat.
- **Employee exchange**: swap people between levels for a time.
- **Joint memberships** in trade associations.
- **Co-optation**: bring partner leaders onto advisory councils or boards and genuinely listen.
- **Diplomacy, mediation, arbitration** for chronic or acute conflict.
- **Legal recourse** as a last resort.

## Market logistics

**Market logistics** plans the infrastructure to meet demand and implements and controls the physical flows from origin to point of use, at a profit. **Supply chain management** widens it upstream to procurement and conversion, and to suppliers' own inputs. Look for redesigns that remove whole steps (digital delivery replacing physical media).

Objectives: "right goods, right place, right time, least cost" gives no guidance because no system maximizes service and minimizes cost at once. Decide on a **total-system basis**; local cost minimization backfires (cheap slow freight ties up capital and loses customers; cheap packaging raises damage; minimal inventory raises stock-outs and rush shipping). Start from what customers value (on-time delivery, emergency help, careful handling, fast replacement of defects) and from competitors' standards, aim to maximize profit not sales, and publish a concrete **service promise** with a standard per factor (e.g. share of orders delivered within N days, fill accuracy, status-response time, damage rate).

Four decisions:

1. **Order processing**: shorten the order-to-payment cycle (transmission, entry and credit check, scheduling, shipment and invoicing, payment).
2. **Warehousing**: more stocking points mean faster delivery but higher cost; centralize with fast transport, or ship from stores; warehouses can take on assembly, packing and display building.
3. **Inventory**: cost rises at an accelerating rate as fill rate approaches 100 percent. Set the **reorder point** to balance stock-out risk against overstock cost, and the order quantity to balance order-processing (setup plus running) costs against **carrying costs** (storage, capital, tax, insurance, depreciation, obsolescence; can approach 30 percent of inventory value). Anyone asking for more stock must show incremental gross profit exceeds incremental carrying cost. Options: slow movers central, fast movers near customers; **just-in-time** (near-zero stock, cash-flow benefits) at the price of fragility when any link breaks, so stress-test the network against demand and supply shocks.
4. **Transportation**: choose rail, air, truck, water or pipeline on speed, frequency, dependability, capability, availability, traceability and cost; combine modes via containerization (piggyback, fishyback, trainship, airtruck); choose private, contract or common carriers; use shelf-ready packaging and fit protection to size, weight and fragility.

## Showrooming

Shoppers examine products in a store, then buy online, often from another seller. Responses Kotler describes: price-match online rivals, link stores and websites (buy online, pick up or return in store), equip staff with tablets and customer histories, and invest in good apps, multichannel support and in-store price comparison. Shifting a sale online can be more profitable than losing it to a rival.

Current note: since this edition, marketplaces, direct-to-consumer brands, retail media networks and same-day delivery have changed channel economics; the functions and conflict mechanics above still apply.

## Worked example: B2B SaaS adding a reseller channel

A project-management SaaS sells direct (self-serve plus inside sales) and wants managed-service providers (MSPs) to resell to small firms.

| Decision | Choice and reasoning |
|---|---|
| Segment by service need | Enterprises want security reviews and SSO (direct field sales); SMBs want setup, training and one bill (MSPs); individuals want instant start (self-serve) |
| Functions shifted to MSP | Onboarding, first-line support, billing, local selling; vendor keeps product, tier-2 support, brand |
| Coverage | Selective: certified MSPs only (expert power plus certification badge) |
| Conflict risk | Multichannel: self-serve undercutting MSP price. Fix: MSP-only bundle (branded variant) and deal registration that gives account ownership to the registering partner |
| Compensation | Functional discounts: base margin plus extra for onboarding completed and 90-day retention |
| Evaluation | Quarterly: new accounts, net revenue retention, time-to-onboard, support escalations per account |

## Workflow

1. List target segments and, for each, the service outputs they want (speed, assortment, advice, installation, financing, convenience, lot size).
2. Map the channel functions and five flows for the current or proposed system; mark who performs each and what it costs.
3. Set channel objectives per segment (customers, service level, cost) and note constraints (product traits, regulation, competitors' channels, economic conditions).
4. Generate alternatives (direct, agents, distributors, retailers, marketplaces, franchise, partners) and choose coverage intensity; check brand-fit of each outlet.
5. Design the partner offering: margins as functional discounts, training, certification, co-marketing, data sharing; decide which power bases you will use.
6. Choose the system type (conventional, VMS type, horizontal partnership) and name the channel steward.
7. Anticipate conflict: define account ownership, territories, pricing parity, branded variants and dual compensation before launch.
8. Set the logistics service promise and the four logistics decisions on a total-cost basis.
9. Define member KPIs and a review cadence; run incremental with/without analyses before adding or dropping members.

## Red flags

- Channel choice made on cost or habit without stating which segment and service outputs it serves.
- A new direct or online channel launched without telling partners about account ownership, pricing or compensation.
- Moving a premium brand to mass outlets for a quarter's volume.
- Paying distributors margin for functions they do not perform.
- Partner program built only on discounts and threats.
- Territory or account-credit rules undefined across sales force, partners and web.
- "Right goods, right place, right time, least cost" as the only logistics objective; departments minimizing their own costs.
- Inventory increases requested with no comparison of incremental profit to carrying cost.
- Just-in-time with no contingency for supplier or transport disruption.
- Channel strategy unchanged since launch despite a shift in buyers or product life-cycle stage.
- No designed path for returns, refurbishment or recycling.
