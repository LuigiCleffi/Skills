# Reciprocation

People feel obliged to repay, in kind, what others give them: favors, gifts, invitations and concessions. The rule is found in every human society and is strong enough to override dislike for the giver. Use it by giving genuine, relevant value first and making concessions that are real; never by forcing unwanted 'gifts' or staging fake retreats, which is the exploitative form this chapter teaches people to recognize and neutralize.

## The rule for reciprocation

- **Definition**: we should try to repay what another person has provided us. Favors earn favors; gifts earn gifts; concessions earn concessions.
- **Why it exists**: a web of indebtedness lets one person give resources without losing them, enabling division of labor, trade, aid and cooperation. Societies train members to honor it and shame those who do not (moocher, ingrate, freeloader).
- **Future orientation**: the obligation can last decades. Cialdini's example is relief money sent by a famine-struck country to a richer one because of aid received half a century earlier.

## Three properties that make it exploitable

### 1. It is overpowering

- In Regan's classic study, a confederate who gave an unsolicited soft drink later sold about twice as many raffle tickets as one who gave nothing.
- Normally, liking predicts compliance. After a favor, liking stopped mattering: people who disliked the giver bought as many tickets as those who liked him.
- Implication: unpopular or unknown requesters (a new brand, an unfamiliar cause) can bypass the need to be liked by giving first. Cialdini's example is a religious group whose street fundraising failed until members handed out a flower or book before asking.

### 2. It enforces uninvited debts

- The rule does not require that we asked for the favor. An unrequested gift still obligates. A charity mailing that included free personalized address labels nearly doubled its response rate.
- Cultural pressure: there is an obligation to give, an obligation to receive and an obligation to repay. **The obligation to receive** is what makes the rule exploitable: it removes our choice of whom we owe.
- The giver chooses both the first favor and the form of the repayment. That asymmetry is where manipulation lives.
- A gift framed as merchandise creates little obligation; framed as a gift, it creates a lot.

### 3. It can trigger unfair exchanges

- The rule demands a similar kind of return, but not an equal size. A small favor can extract a much larger one.
- Two forces push people to overpay: the internal discomfort of being indebted, and the external fear of being seen as a taker.
- People will even avoid asking for help they need if they cannot repay it, and decline gifts that would create unwanted obligations.

## Free samples and free value

- A sample informs (legitimate) and is also a gift (obligating). Done well, both work for the buyer.
- Trial-at-home works by combining gift and use: a household leaves a bag of products on trial, then buys a share of what they have already started using.
- **Honest marketing version**: give something genuinely useful that stands on its own (a working free tier, a real audit, a useful guide), make clear what it is, and ask for the next step only if it fits the buyer's need.

## Reciprocal concessions: rejection-then-retreat

The second route. If someone retreats from a larger request to a smaller one, we feel obliged to retreat too, from 'no' to 'yes'.

- **Technique**: make a larger request likely to be refused; after refusal, move to the smaller request you wanted. The second request does not have to be small, only smaller than the first.
- **Evidence**: students asked to chaperone young offenders on a day trip mostly refused; when that ask followed a refused request for a two-year mentoring commitment, about three times as many agreed.
- **Three forces combine**:
  1. Reciprocity: the retreat looks like a concession that deserves one back.
  2. Perceptual contrast: the second request looks smaller next to the first.
  3. Structure: if the first request is accepted, the requester gets more than hoped; if refused, the retreat is primed. 'Heads I win, tails you lose.'
- **Selling down**: showing the top of the line first and then moving down raised average sale value far above starting cheap and trading up (Cialdini cites a billiard-table retailer).
- **Follow-through**: targets of rejection-then-retreat were more likely to actually do what they agreed to, and to agree to future requests. Two side effects explain it:
  - **Responsibility**: they feel they caused the requester's concession, so they own the deal.
  - **Satisfaction**: agreements reached through the other side's concessions feel better.
- **Limit**: if the first request is so extreme it seems illegitimate, the retreat is not seen as a real concession and the tactic backfires. Bad-faith opening positions destroy trust.
- **Danger to the buyer**: a 'reasonable' retreat can still be a bad decision when judged on its own. Cialdini argues a disastrous political decision was approved only because it came after two far bigger proposals; the one person who had not seen the earlier proposals immediately judged it not worth the risk.

## Honest use in marketing

- **Give first, give relevant**: the value should help the buyer solve the problem your product solves, so the gift and the offer are aligned.
- **Personal and unexpected beats generic**: (current practice, consistent with the chapter's logic) a tailored teardown of a prospect's funnel creates more goodwill than a generic PDF.
- **Let people decline the gift**: never force acceptance, never tie a 'free' item to a hidden obligation, never make cancellation of a free trial hard.
- **Make concessions real**: if you open high, open at a price or scope you would genuinely deliver and that is defensible; move to options that are truly cheaper or smaller, not to the price you always intended presented as a sacrifice.
- **Disclose**: a free consultation that is a sales meeting should say so.
- **Judge each request on its own**: after a buyer refuses, the next offer must still be good for them, not merely smaller.

## Worked example: agency lead generation

A web-performance agency wants meetings with e-commerce managers.

| Step | Honest reciprocity | Exploitative version (avoid) |
|---|---|---|
| Gift | A free 5-page speed audit of the prospect's actual store, with three fixes they can apply without the agency | A generic e-book behind a form, then daily calls 'since you downloaded it' |
| Framing | 'Yours to keep and use whether or not we ever work together' | 'We did all this work for you, the least you can do is take a call' |
| Ask | 'If you'd like help implementing fix #2, here is a 20-minute slot' | Pressure to sign immediately to 'repay' the audit |
| Concession | Offer a full retainer; if declined, offer a fixed-price single fix that genuinely costs less | Quote an inflated retainer you never expect to sell to make the 'real' price feel like a favor |
| Follow-up | Ask for a referral only if they found the audit useful | Demand referrals as compensation after a refusal |

## Defending against exploitative reciprocity

- Blanket refusal of all gifts is a poor policy: it insults genuinely generous people and cuts you off from honest exchanges.
- **Accept offers for what they fundamentally are.** A real favor deserves a future favor. A sales device deserves no favor.
- **Redefine** once you see the tactic: 'This free inspection is a sales technique, not a gift.' The rule says favors are met with favors; it does not require that tricks be met with favors.
- Apply the same redefinition to a retreat: a smaller request after a refusal is not automatically a concession.
- Ask the buyer's question: would I accept this second offer if it were the first one I heard?

## Workflow

1. Define the action you want and the value the buyer actually needs before taking it.
2. Design a first gift that is useful on its own, aligned with your offer, and as personal as the economics allow.
3. Write the framing honestly: what it is, what it costs (nothing), what you will ask later (if anything).
4. Sequence the ask: give, confirm usefulness, then make one clear, relevant request.
5. If using a high-then-lower offer structure, check that the high option is real and defensible and that the lower option is a genuine reduction.
6. Remove coercive mechanics: forced acceptance, pre-checked follow-ups, hidden auto-renewal on 'free' trials.
7. Measure downstream: show-up rate, conversion of trial to paid, satisfaction and refund rates, not only replies.

## Red flags

- A 'gift' the recipient cannot refuse or return, or an unrequested item mailed with an implied obligation to pay.
- Free trials that silently convert or are hard to cancel.
- Copy that guilt-trips: 'after everything we gave you'.
- An opening price or scope invented only to be cut.
- A negotiation opener so extreme that the counterpart sees bad faith.
- Referral requests used as a consolation ask after every refused sale.
- 'Free' consultations that are undisclosed sales pitches.
- A follow-up offer judged only as 'smaller than the first' rather than as good for the buyer.
