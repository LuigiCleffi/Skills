# Using Advantage

Advantage comes from **asymmetries** between rivals, and it holds only in particular contests, for particular products and particular buyers. Press where you are strong and side-step where you are not. Owning an advantage does not by itself make you richer; value grows only when you **deepen** it, **broaden** it, **raise demand** for what it produces, or **strengthen the barriers** that protect it.

## Advantage is particular

- Two identical rivals on a featureless field have no advantage. In real rivalry there are countless asymmetries; the leader's job is to find which are **critical**.
- No one has an advantage at everything. Exploit rivals' weaknesses, avoid leading with your own.
- **Do not wrestle the gorilla.** Rumelt's example: a startup that invented a remarkable textile technology wanted to build its own apparel brand; its investor argued that world-class development skill did not transfer to running a clothing company, and that licensing or selling the technology fit its strength. A strong runner should not switch to wrestling.
- Large powers can also be drawn into contests where the advantage lies with the side that has more patience and less sensitivity to cost.

## Competitive advantage defined

- You have a competitive advantage if you can **produce at lower cost** than competitors, **deliver more perceived value**, or a mix of both.
- Advantages have **limited extent**: costs vary by product and application, and buyers differ in location, knowledge and taste. A natural-foods grocer is advantaged only for certain products and certain affluent shoppers who value organics.
- **Sustainability** requires that competitors cannot duplicate the resources underlying the advantage, which requires an **isolating mechanism**:
  - patents and other legal rights;
  - reputation and brand;
  - commercial and social relationships;
  - network effects;
  - dramatic economies of scale;
  - tacit knowledge and skill built through experience.
- Rumelt notes that a leading smartphone franchise was protected by brand, reputation, complementary services and app-ecosystem network effects, each deliberately built.
- **An advantage on sale to all comers is a contradiction in terms.** A vendor promising that its software or training 'gives you competitive advantage' misuses the term, since every rival can buy the same thing. (Useful when writing B2B copy: claim specific outcomes, not 'competitive advantage'.)

## Advantage versus wealth: 'interesting' advantages

- Rumelt's thought experiment: a machine that produces silver at zero cost is a low-cost producer, but someone who buys it at its fair price earns only a normal return. **Having an advantage and getting wealthier are not the same.**
- A business is **'interesting'** when you can see ways to **increase the value** of its advantage through your own actions. The silver machine is not interesting: it cannot be improved, silver cannot be differentiated, one owner cannot raise world demand.
- A dominant marketplace with a large advantage can still be stagnant in value if nothing new is being done with its advantage; it becomes interesting only when someone sees unexploited ways to expand it.
- Rumelt's serial-entrepreneur example: large orchard holdings were 'interesting' because only a grower that big could earn back investment in yield research and in **stimulating category demand**; trees take years to mature, giving time to build processing scale, branding and distribution that smaller growers could not match.

## The four fronts of value creation

Wealth increases when advantage increases or when demand for the resources behind it increases. Progress on at least one front:

### 1. Deepening advantage

- Advantage = **surplus**, the gap between buyer value and cost. Deepen it by raising value, cutting cost, or both.
- Two reasons improvement stalls:
  - Believing improvement is natural or can be forced by **pressure and incentives alone**. Improvement comes from re-examining the details of how work is done (a classic study doubled bricklayers' productivity by moving materials to chest height, using movable scaffolds and fixing mortar consistency). For products, it comes from closely **studying buyers' attitudes, decisions and feelings** and anticipating problems.
  - Weak isolating mechanisms: if rivals can free-ride on your improvements, you will under-invest. Protect improvements or embed them in a business special enough that they are of little use to rivals.

### 2. Broadening the extent of advantage

- Extend into new fields by looking past products and buyers to the **special skills and resources** underneath ('build on your strengths').
- Mischief arises when firms define their strength as a vapour ('transportation', 'branded consumer products', 'management') and diversify into things they do not understand.
- Productive extensions come from deep pools of **know-how** (a chemicals firm moving from explosives into polymers and a stream of materials). Knowledge is not used up by use; it may grow.
- Extensions based on **customer beliefs** (brand, relationships, reputation) can be **diluted or damaged**; a failure in the new arena can rebound on the core.
- Brand extension needs explicit guardrails. Rumelt describes a family-entertainment studio that defined its brand by three rules (no bad language, no uncomfortable sexual situations, no gratuitous violence), which let it extend into action-adventure without harming its core.

### 3. Creating higher demand

- An advantage becomes more valuable when buyers multiply or buy more. Technically it is the **scarce resources** behind it that gain value, so higher demand raises long-term profit only if you hold scarce resources and a stable advantage.
- Engineering demand for the services of scarce resources is the **most basic business stratagem**, neglected by theorists who equate strategy with merely having an advantage.
- Rumelt's example: owners of most of a small fruit crop funded research on its health properties, expanded acreage sixfold, and created a new category (100% pure, refrigerated, sold beside fresh produce, with a distinctive brand) instead of diluting it into a juice blend. It was a large bet with the risk of a glut if demand did not come, and demand surged.
- Category demand creation pays the party that owns most of the supply or the strongest position in the category.

### 4. Strengthening isolating mechanisms

- Stronger patents, trademarks and copyrights; stretching a powerful brand over new products; reducing turnover where advantage lies in collective know-how; clarifying property rights through law or collective industry action (Rumelt recounts early oil fields where unclear ownership led to ruinous over-pumping until rules were built).
- **Be a moving target**: continual change in products and methods makes imitation costly, even when changes are not big improvements.
- Innovation streams are harder to imitate when built on **proprietary knowledge**, such as feedback from lead customers or internal operating data, rather than public science alone.

## Worked example: a workflow-automation SaaS

- Asymmetry: a library of 400 pre-built connectors for regional accounting systems that global rivals lack; strong only with mid-size firms in three countries.
- Isolating mechanisms: tacit integration know-how, partner relationships with local accountants, usage data showing which automations work.
- Gorilla to avoid: competing for global enterprises against platform giants on breadth.
- Deepen: watch customers build automations; cut setup time with templates from usage data.
- Broaden: the connector skill transfers to payroll and tax filing in the same countries; the brand stays 'accounting-grade reliable' (guardrail: no consumer apps, no unreliable beta connectors under the main brand).
- Demand: co-market 'close the books in 3 days' with accountant partners to grow automation adoption across their client bases, where the firm holds most connectors.
- Protect: ship connector updates monthly (a moving target), deepen accountant partnerships, keep the integration team stable.

## Workflow

1. List asymmetries versus each main rival; mark which are critical to buyers' decisions.
2. State the advantage precisely (cost, value, both) and its extent (where it holds and where it does not).
3. Identify isolating mechanisms and rate their durability.
4. Ask whether the advantage is 'interesting': what actions under the user's control could raise its value?
5. Develop moves on each of the four fronts; prefer fronts where the user captures most of the benefit.
6. For any extension, check whether it rests on know-how (safer) or customer beliefs (risk of dilution) and write brand guardrails.
7. Name the contests to avoid and say what to do instead (license, partner, sell, stay out).

## Red flags

- 'Our competitive advantage is our people/innovation/customer focus' with no asymmetry or mechanism.
- Sales copy promising customers 'competitive advantage' from a product anyone can buy.
- Moving from a proven strength into an unrelated business because the team is talented.
- Brand extensions with no definition of what the brand guarantees.
- Investing in category demand when competitors would capture most of the gain.
- Assuming a dominant position will keep creating value without new moves.
- Improvement programmes based on targets and incentives alone.
