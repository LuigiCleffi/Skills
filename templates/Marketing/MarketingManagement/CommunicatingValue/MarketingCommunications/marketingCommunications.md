# Managing Marketing Communications

Marketing communication is how a firm informs, persuades and reminds customers, directly or indirectly, about its products and brands: the voice of the brand and a way to build dialog, brand equity and customer loyalty. The question is not whether to communicate but what to say, how, when, to whom and how often. Build every program the same way: set a focused objective with numbers and a deadline, define the audience, craft one or two core messages, pick the media mix and schedule, make the creative earn attention and link to the brand, and measure outcomes rather than activity.

## What communication does

- Shows how and why a product is used, by whom, where and when.
- Tells customers who makes it and what the company and brand stand for.
- Motivates trial or use.
- Links the brand to people, places, events, brands, experiences, feelings and things.
- Builds brand equity (establishes the brand in memory, creates an image), drives sales and can affect shareholder value.

## The communication process

### Macromodel: nine elements

- **Parties**: sender (company) and receiver (customer).
- **Tools**: message and media.
- **Functions**: encoding, decoding, response, feedback.
- **Noise**: random and competing messages.

Rules:

- Know which audience you want and what response you want.
- Encode in words, images, sounds or movement the audience can decode.
- Use media that reach the audience; build feedback channels.
- The more the sender's field of experience overlaps the receiver's, the better the message lands.
- Selective attention, distortion and retention filter what gets through.

### Micromodel: response sequences

| Sequence | When | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Learn-feel-do (cognitive, affective, behavioral) | High involvement, meaningful differentiation | Car, house, enterprise software |
| Do-feel-learn | High involvement, little perceived differentiation | Airline ticket, laptop |
| Learn-do-feel | Low involvement, little differentiation | Salt, batteries |

Whatever the order, buyers move through **awareness → knowledge → liking → preference → conviction → purchase**. Diagnose where the audience is stuck and target that step (low price, premium or trial to push conviction into purchase).

A campaign raises the odds at every step by ensuring:

1. The right person sees the right message at the right place and time.
2. The offering is positioned on desirable, deliverable PODs and POPs.
3. The audience pays attention and comprehends the message.
4. The audience is motivated to consider buying and using it.

## The communication program

Six steps, all flowing from the marketing plan's goals, targets and value proposition:

1. Set the objectives.
2. Identify the audience.
3. Craft the message.
4. Decide on the media.
5. Develop the creative approach.
6. Measure performance.

## Step 1: Setting communication objectives

A communication objective is a specific task and achievement level, with a specific audience, in a specific period.

### Focus

- **Create awareness**: the ability to recognize or recall the brand in enough detail to buy.
  - Recognition is easier than recall. Recognition matters in-store where the product is visible; recall matters when communication happens away from the point of purchase.
  - Awareness of the need stimulates **primary demand**; awareness of the specific offering stimulates **selective demand**.
- **Build preference**: show the offering meets a currently relevant need.
  - Negatively oriented needs: problem removal, problem avoidance, incomplete satisfaction, normal depletion.
  - Positively oriented needs: sensory gratification, intellectual stimulation, social approval.
  - **Persuasive** communication builds liking, preference and conviction. **Comparative** messages work best when they engage reason and emotion together and the audience processes analytically.
  - **Reinforcement** communication reassures current buyers they chose well.
- **Incite action**: motivate purchase or purchase-related action (offers, deals). Most people are not in the market at any moment (a small share of adults plan to buy a given product in any week), so action messages reach few in-market buyers.

Choose the focus from the situation:

- Mature category, leader, low usage → increase usage frequency.
- New category, challenger with a superior product → prove superiority.

**Funnel diagnosis**: compare awareness, trial and satisfaction.

- High awareness, high trial, low satisfaction: the product, not the communication, is the problem.
- Low awareness, high satisfaction among triers: invest in awareness and trial.

### Benchmarks

Actionable objectives have a **focus**, a **quantitative benchmark** and a **temporal benchmark**; the size of the goal and the time frame depend on each other. They also become the yardstick for evaluation.

Template:

> [Create awareness / build preference / incite action] of [offering or belief] among [X% of audience] from [baseline] to [target] by [date].

Example: raise the share of IT managers at 200-2,000-employee firms who associate Brand X with 'fastest backup restore' from 12% to 35% by the end of Q3.

### Budget

- **Objective-and-task** (recommended): define objectives, list the tasks needed to reach them, cost each task, sum. Principle: the marginal profit of the last communication dollar should at least equal the marginal profit of a dollar spent elsewhere in marketing.
- **Factors** that raise or lower the budget:
  - **Life-cycle stage**: new products need more.
  - **Differentiation**: undifferentiated categories need more to build image.
  - **Market share**: high share needs less as a % of sales to maintain; building share needs more.
  - **Message complexity**: more repetitions needed.
  - **Reach**: hard-to-reach audiences cost more.
  - **Competitive communication and clutter**: need more weight.
  - **Available resources**: a ceiling, not a method.
- Flawed methods used alone:
  - **Competitive parity**: competitors are no wiser, and parity does not prevent ad wars.
  - **Affordable**: ignores communication as an investment, makes long-range planning hard, follows funds rather than opportunity.
  - **Percentage of sales**: practical but disconnected from the task.
- Communication is expensed in the year spent even though part of it builds lasting brand equity and loyalty.

## Step 2: Identifying the target audience

- The audience can be potential buyers, current users, deciders, influencers, individuals, groups, sectors or the public. It shapes what, how, when, where and to whom.
- Profile by **usage and loyalty**: new to the category or current user? Loyal to us, loyal to a competitor, or a switcher? Heavy or light user?
- The **target audience may differ from the target market**: talk to children while parents buy, or to parents while children consume; in group decisions, address each member of the decision-making unit who influences the end user.

## Step 3: Crafting the message

- Seek the **big idea**: resonates rationally and emotionally, distinguishes the brand, is broad and flexible enough to work across media, markets and time. Fresh customer insight fuels it.
- A good ad focuses on **one or two core selling propositions**.
- Write a **creative brief** (one or two pages):
  - Key message.
  - Target audience.
  - Communication objectives (to do, to know, to believe).
  - Key brand benefits.
  - Support for the brand promise.
  - Media.
- Explore many messages: in-house team, agency, or crowdsourcing. Crowdsourcing can backfire if the brand gives away judgment (a consumer-chosen product name was so disliked it had to be withdrawn).
- Appeals should tie to the positioning and establish POPs or PODs: performance-related (quality, economy, value) or extrinsic (contemporary, popular, traditional).

## Step 4: Deciding on the media

### The nine modes of communication

| Mode | What it is | Strengths and notes |
|---|---|---|
| Advertising | Paid, nonpersonal presentation by an identified sponsor in print, broadcast, network and display media | Builds long-term image or triggers quick sales; heavy advertising itself signals quality |
| Online and social media | Websites, email, search and display ads, blogs, forums, social posts, video channels | Reaches people in active search or casual browsing |
| Mobile | Messages on phones and tablets: text, apps, mobile web, social | Timely (when and where the person is) and pervasive |
| Direct marketing | Mail, phone, email, messaging or in-person contact to solicit response from specific people | Data analytics make it more personal and relevant |
| Events and experiences | Sponsored sports, arts, entertainment, festivals, tours, museums, street activity, causes | Engaging and implicit (indirect soft sell) |
| Word of mouth | Person-to-person communication, offline or observable online | Can be encouraged or 'seeded' with messages worth sharing |
| Publicity and public relations | Programs for employees, consumers, firms, government and media to promote or protect image | High credibility; underused; good for correcting misconceptions |
| Personal selling | Seller persuades buyer, usually face to face, in retail or direct-to-customer | Depends on the seller's skill |
| Packaging | Pack, styling, store décor, stationery | Powerful where decisions are made at the point of purchase |

- Firms in the same industry use very different mixes (personal selling vs heavy advertising). Tools substitute for one another, so coordinate them.
- Customers meet the brand through many touchpoints (communities, trade shows, sponsorship, factory visits, PR, cause marketing). Evaluate each activity by how effectively and efficiently it builds awareness and creates, maintains or strengthens associations; use sponsorships to add associations that other activities do not (a 'safety' brand sponsoring sport to seem active and contemporary).
- **Paid, owned, earned**:
  - **Paid**: TV, print and display ads, paid search, sponsorships.
  - **Owned**: brochures, packaging, website, blog, social accounts.
  - **Earned**: voluntary word of mouth, press, buzz, viral spread; social media are central.

### The media plan

Choose the most cost-effective vehicles within each medium. Producing a national TV ad can cost as much as running it once, so placement choices matter.

Evaluate vehicles on:

- **Cost per thousand** people reached.
- **Audience quality**: the right people (young parents vs teenagers for baby lotion).
- **Attention probability**.
- **Editorial quality**: prestige and believability of the surrounding content.
- **Placement policies and extras**: regional or occupational editions, lead times.

Big-event advertising can be cost-efficient per viewer and is amplified when designed to drive online sharing and search; release it early to maximize social and PR reach.

### Reach, frequency and impact

- **Reach (R)**: different people or households exposed at least once in a period.
- **Frequency (F)**: average number of exposures per person in the period.
- **Impact (I)**: qualitative value of an exposure in that medium (a food ad in a food magazine beats one in a business magazine).
- **Total exposures (E) = R x F = gross rating points (GRP)**. 80% reach x frequency 3 = 240 GRP. GRP alone does not show the reach/frequency split.
- **Weighted exposures (WE) = R x F x I.**
- Trade-off: with a fixed budget, higher cost per thousand or higher frequency means fewer people reached. Example: $1,000,000 at $5 CPM buys 200 million exposures; at frequency 10 that reaches 20 million people; at $10 CPM, only 10 million.
- **Reach matters most** for launches, extensions of known brands, infrequently bought brands, or an undefined target.
- **Frequency matters most** with strong competitors, a complex story, high resistance or a frequent purchase cycle. The higher the forgetting rate, the more repetition is warranted.

### Timing and allocation

- **Macroscheduling** (seasons, business cycle): follow, oppose, or ignore the seasonal sales pattern.
- **Microscheduling** (within a short period): concentrated, continuous or intermittent.
- Make communication more continuous the higher the **buyer turnover**, **purchase frequency** and **forgetting rate**.

Patterns for a launch:

| Pattern | What | Fits |
|---|---|---|
| Continuity | Even exposure through the period | Expanding markets, frequently bought items, tightly defined buyers |
| Concentration | All spend in one period | One selling season or holiday |
| Flighting | Bursts separated by gaps | Limited funds, infrequent purchase, seasonal items |
| Pulsing | Low continuous level plus periodic heavy waves | Learning the message thoroughly at lower cost |

Geography: **national buys**, **spot buys** (a few markets, areas of dominant influence), **local buys**.

## Step 5: Developing the creative approach

How it is said is often more decisive than what is said. An ineffective ad may have the wrong message or the right message badly expressed.

### Message appeal

- **Informational appeals** elaborate attributes or benefits: problem-solution, demonstration, comparison, testimonials. They assume rational processing.
  - **One- vs two-sided**: two-sided messages (admitting a shortcoming) work better when negative associations must be overcome, with better-educated audiences and with people initially opposed.
  - **Order**: in a one-sided message, put the strongest argument first where attention is fleeting; with a captive audience a climactic order may work. For an opposed audience, start with the other side's argument and finish with your strongest.
- **Transformational appeals** elaborate a non-product benefit or image: the kind of person who uses the brand, or the experience it creates; they aim to stir motivating emotion.
  - **Negative appeals** (fear, guilt, shame) for starting or stopping behaviors. Fear works best when moderate, from a credible source, and when the product credibly and efficiently relieves the fear it raises. Use them responsibly and never exaggerate the threat.
  - Messages persuade most when they **moderately disagree** with what the audience believes; stating what they already believe merely reinforces, and too large a gap is rejected.
  - **Positive appeals**: humor, love, pride, joy. 'Borrowed interest' devices (babies, puppies, music, sex appeal) win attention in low-involvement settings but can hurt comprehension, wear out and overshadow the product.
- Execution choices by medium: print (headline, copy, illustration, color); radio (words, voice, vocal qualities matched to the product); TV or in person (all of these plus body language); online (layout, fonts, graphics, visual and verbal information).

### Message source

Credible sources win attention and recall. Credibility has three components:

- **Expertise**: specialized knowledge backing the claim.
- **Trustworthiness**: perceived objectivity and honesty; friends beat strangers and salespeople; unpaid beats paid.
- **Likability**: candor, humor, naturalness.

The best source scores high on all three (doctors for health claims, a founder who embodies integrity). Ordinary customers and employees can add realism and overcome skepticism.

**Congruity principle**: when a liked source praises a disliked brand, attitudes move toward each other: the brand gains a little, the source loses a little. A source who endorses many disliked brands ends up disliked. A good image can lift a brand, at some cost to the source.

**Celebrity endorsements** (from the chapter's insight box):

- Work when the celebrity is credible or personifies a key attribute.
- Need high recognition, high positive affect and high fit; familiarity plus likability is what the industry scores.
- Celebrities can play strategic roles: co-designing, testing, acting as 'ambassadors', or uncredited voice-overs chosen for voice talent.
- Risks: fee hold-outs, withdrawal, flops, loss of popularity, scandal.
- Mitigations: background checks; several endorsers; creating your own **brand character** (a fictional spokesperson the brand controls).
- Disclose paid endorsements clearly; current advertising rules require it.

### ADPLAN: checking creative execution

A framework for reviewing a storyboard or execution; each element can also be measured.

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| **Attention** | Does it capture and hold the target's interest (initial capture and sustained, for example before the 'skip' button)? |
| **Distinction** | Do its themes, content or devices set it apart from category advertising? |
| **Positioning** | Does it put the brand in the right category, with a strong benefit and a reason to believe? |
| **Linkage** | Will people remember the brand and message, not just the ad? |
| **Amplification** | Are the thoughts it triggers mostly positive (leading to favorable attitudes and purchase)? |
| **Net equity** | Does it fit the brand's heritage and established associations without undermining them? |

ADPLAN complements, not replaces, a proper creative brief, a clear purpose (awareness vs persuasion), suitable media and the budget.

## Step 6: Measuring communication effectiveness

Managers want outcomes and revenue, but communicators often report inputs (clippings, number of ads) and expenses. Intermediate outputs (reach, frequency, recall, recognition, persuasion shifts, CPM) are better; outcomes are best.

- **Supply side**: media coverage (seconds of on-screen brand visibility, column inches). Quantifiable but ignores content and meaning; sponsorship exposure rarely equals the editorial value PR people claim.
- **Demand side**: effect on the audience. Ask whether they recognize or recall the message, how often they saw it, what points they recall, how they felt, and what their attitudes were before and are now. Add behavioral measures: purchases, liking, sharing and talking about it.
- **Posttests**: compare results with benchmarks (target awareness 50%, achieved 30%: underspending, weak ads, or another factor).
- **Share of expenditure → share of voice → share of mind and heart → share of market**: use it to judge over- or under-spending relative to competitors.
- **Pretests**: useful diagnostics, but ads can be built to test well and perform poorly; never the sole criterion.
- **Sales impact**:
  - **Historical**: statistical models relating past sales to past spend.
  - **Experimental**: test vs control markets or audiences.
  - Brand tracking studies over time.

## Worked example: communication plan for a regional physiotherapy chain

| Element | Decision |
|---|---|
| Situation | Strong satisfaction among patients (NPS high), low unaided awareness (9%) in two new cities |
| Response sequence | Learn-feel-do (high involvement, meaningful differences) |
| Objective | Raise aided awareness among adults 30-65 with musculoskeletal pain in City A from 15% to 40%, and booked first visits from 120 to 300 per month, within 6 months |
| Audience | Sufferers (decider), GPs and sports coaches (influencers), employers' HR (for corporate plans) |
| Big idea | 'Back to what you love': return to a specific activity, not 'treatment' |
| Message | Informational core (same-week appointments, outcome tracking) with transformational execution (patients back on the bike, in the garden); two-sided line acknowledging 'physio takes work, we make it worth it' |
| Source | Real patients and named physiotherapists (expertise, trustworthiness); no paid celebrity |
| Mix | Search ads and local SEO (paid and owned), GP referral packs (direct), community running-club sponsorship (events), PR on outcome data (earned), clinic signage and booking page (packaging equivalent) |
| Schedule | Pulsing: always-on search, plus two heavy waves timed with January resolutions and spring sports season |
| Budget | Objective-and-task: cost each task; check marginal return against spending on extra clinic hours |
| Creative check | ADPLAN review of the video spot; fix weak linkage by showing the clinic name in the first 3 seconds |
| Measurement | Pre/post awareness survey, booked visits by source, cost per first visit, share of voice in local search |

## Workflow

1. Pull the target market, value proposition and positioning from the marketing plan.
2. Diagnose the audience's current state (awareness, trial, satisfaction; where in the response hierarchy they are) and pick the response sequence.
3. Write the objective with focus, quantitative and temporal benchmarks.
4. Set the budget by objective-and-task; sanity-check it against life cycle, differentiation, share, complexity, reach, clutter and resources.
5. Define the audience by usage and loyalty and list every member of the decision-making unit.
6. Find the big idea; write the creative brief with one or two core selling propositions.
7. Choose the mix across the nine modes and paid, owned and earned media; justify each by the association or step it moves.
8. Build the media plan: vehicles by CPM, audience quality, attention and editorial fit; set reach versus frequency; choose a schedule pattern and geographic buys.
9. Choose appeal type, sidedness, argument order and source; check source credibility, fit and congruity; review creative with ADPLAN.
10. Define demand-side and behavioral measures before launch; pretest for diagnosis; posttest against benchmarks; track share of voice and sales impact.

## Red flags

- Objectives like 'raise awareness' with no number, audience or date.
- Budget equal to last year plus inflation, or matching a competitor, with no task list.
- More than two core messages in one ad.
- Reporting impressions, clippings and posts as success with no change in awareness, attitude or behavior measured.
- Spending on awareness when the funnel shows triers are dissatisfied (fix the product first).
- A celebrity or influencer with no fit to the product, or an unchecked background, or an undisclosed paid relationship.
- Memorable ads nobody can attribute to the brand (weak linkage).
- Borrowed-interest devices that overshadow the product.
- Fear appeals that are extreme, not credibly relieved by the product, or exaggerate the risk.
- One-sided claims to a skeptical, informed audience that already knows the weakness.
- A media plan that spreads too thin (high reach, frequency of one) for a complex message.
- Creative that contradicts the brand's heritage or positioning (negative net equity).
- Crowdsourced or user-generated content launched without brand judgment.
