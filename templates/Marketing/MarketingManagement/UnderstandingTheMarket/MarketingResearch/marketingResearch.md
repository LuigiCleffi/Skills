# Conducting Marketing Research

Research exists to produce insight that changes a decision, not to collect data. Start from a business question that is neither too broad nor too narrow, spend on research only up to the value of the better decision it will buy, and combine methods so the weaknesses of each are covered. Then size the opportunity carefully (potential is not forecast, forecast is not quota) and close the loop with a small set of metrics, models and a dashboard that show what marketing actually delivers.

## Scope of marketing research

- **Definition**: the function that links consumers, customers and the public to the marketer through information used to identify opportunities and problems; generate, refine and evaluate marketing actions; monitor performance; and improve understanding of marketing. It specifies the information needed, designs the method, runs collection, analyzes results and communicates findings and implications.
- **Insight** is diagnostic: it explains how and why something happens in the market and what it means for the marketer. A researcher who predicts a film will flop from general cultural mood, without reading the story, delivers opinion, not insight. A pack redesign launched without testing consumer reaction can cost a fifth of sales before the old pack returns.
- Good research shows scientific method, creativity, multiple methods, sound models, cost-benefit thinking, healthy skepticism and ethical conduct.
- Common failures: research used too little or wrongly, a vague problem definition, unrealistic expectations of what research can say.

### Who does research and where to get it cheaply

- Companies typically spend around 1 to 2 percent of sales on research, much of it with outside firms; large firms also have in-house insight teams involved across the brand life cycle.
- Outside firms: **syndicated-service** (sell standard consumer and trade data), **custom** (specific projects), **specialty-line** (for example, field interviewing).
- Low-cost sources: company records, libraries, universities, chambers of commerce, government statistics, the web, inexpensive online survey tools.
- **Competitor and store visits**: look for what others are doing right.
- **Employees**: frontline staff and small teams sent to observe customers can identify problems worth solving.

## The research process

1. Define the problem. 2. Develop the research plan. 3. Collect the information. 4. Analyze the information. 5. Make the decision.

### 1. Define the problem

- Too broad ("find out everything about our customers") wastes money; too narrow ("will enough people pay exactly X so we break even in a year") presupposes the answer.
- Challenge the assumptions behind the question: why that price, why break even, how important is being first, how long would a lead last?
- Write one **business question** (the decision), break it into sub-questions, then translate it into **research objectives** (what you need to learn).
- Choose the type of research:
  - **Exploratory**: identify the problem and possible solutions.
  - **Descriptive**: quantify (how many, how much, who).
  - **Causal**: test cause and effect.

### 2. Develop the research plan

**Value the research first.** If the decision without research is worth 50,000 and research is expected to improve it to 90,000, spend at most 40,000. If research cannot change the decision, do not buy it.

Then decide five things:

**Data sources**

- **Secondary data** (collected for another purpose): start with the company's own data, then low-cost external sources.
- **Primary data** (collected now for this purpose): needed when secondary data do not exist, are outdated or unreliable. Most projects need some.

**Research approaches**

| Approach | What it is | Strengths | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Observational | Watching people shop and use products, diaries, photos and video, in-store tracking | Shows what people do, not what they say | Privacy and consent |
| Ethnographic | Anthropological immersion in how people live and work ("a day in the life"; what happens just before and after using the product) | Surfaces unarticulated needs; works in B2B by studying end users | Time and cost; needs skilled interpretation |
| Focus groups | 6 to 10 (trending smaller) selected people, a professional moderator, a structured agenda | Exploratory depth on motives and language | Small, unrepresentative samples; artificial setting; do not generalize |
| Surveys | Questionnaires on knowledge, beliefs, preferences, satisfaction; own instrument, omnibus surveys, panels, intercepts, post-service surveys; review and social listening | Quantifies at scale | Survey burnout; keep short, offer incentives; stated answers can differ from behavior |
| Behavioral | Purchase, scanner, database, ticketing and online behavior data | Actual choices are more reliable than stated ones | Explains what, rarely why |
| Experimental | Matched groups, different treatments, control of extraneous factors, significance tests | Establishes cause and effect | Needs careful design; real-world confounds |

**Research instruments**

- **Questionnaires**: the most common instrument. Wording, form and order change answers, so pretest and debug. Closed-end questions are easy to tabulate; open-end questions reveal how people think.

| Closed-end type | Use |
|---|---|
| Dichotomous | Yes or no |
| Multiple choice | Three or more answers |
| Likert scale | Agreement with a statement (strongly disagree to strongly agree) |
| Semantic differential | Position between two bipolar words |
| Importance scale | How important an attribute is |
| Rating scale | Poor to excellent |
| Intention-to-buy scale | Definitely buy to definitely not buy |

| Open-end type | Use |
|---|---|
| Completely unstructured | Free opinion |
| Word association | First word that comes to mind |
| Sentence completion | Finish an incomplete sentence |
| Story completion | Finish an incomplete story |
| Picture interpretation | Fill in a speech bubble for a character |

- **Qualitative measures** (indirect, unstructured; good first steps because people are less guarded; small samples and interpretation vary between researchers):
  - Word association: map possible brand associations.
  - Projective techniques: bubble exercises, comparing brands to people, animals, cars or countries.
  - Visualization: collages of images that depict perceptions.
  - Brand personification: if the brand were a person, who would it be?
  - **Laddering**: a chain of "why" questions from attribute to functional benefit to emotional benefit to core value (well built → reliable → people can reach me → I can help those I care about).
- **Measurement devices**: skin-conductance sensors, eye tracking, facial coding, and **neuromarketing** (EEG, fMRI). Brain and physiological responses sometimes predict behavior better than stated preferences, but they are costly, obtrusive and not a sole basis for decisions.

Mix qualitative and quantitative methods so their strengths offset each other (for example, a video diary plus an online survey in a home-use test).

### 3. Collect the information

The most expensive and error-prone phase (unreachable respondents, refusals, biased or dishonest answers).

**Sampling plan**

- **Sampling unit** (whom): define the target population and a **sampling frame** in which everyone has an equal or known chance of selection.
- **Sample size** (how many): larger is more reliable, but well-drawn samples of under 1 percent of a population often suffice.
- **Sampling procedure** (how chosen): **probability sampling** allows confidence intervals ("95 chances in 100 that the true value lies between X and Y"). Convenience and intercept samples cannot support that claim.

**Contact methods**

| Method | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| Online (embedded surveys, panels, virtual focus groups, communities, social polls, clickstream, split tests of prices, headlines and features, text chats) | Fast, cheap, flexible; communities can co-develop products and halve development time | Self-selection; you must still ask the right people the right questions |
| In person (arranged or intercept) | Most versatile; can probe and observe | Most expensive; interviewer bias; intercepts are non-probability samples |
| Mail and email | Reaches people who avoid interviews; no interviewer bias | Low and slow response; questions must be simple |
| Telephone | Quick; can clarify and follow up | Must be short; growing resistance to calls |

**Data mining** (cluster analysis, predictive modeling, cognitive modeling) on customer data helps to: identify prospects; decide which customers get which offer (and automate follow-up sequences); deepen loyalty by remembering preferences; reactivate lapsed buyers; and avoid serious mistakes (penalizing a major depositor, two staff pitching different prices to the same customer, giving a premium customer standard service).

### 4. Analyze and 5. Decide

- Tabulate, compute averages and dispersion, apply statistical techniques and decision models, test hypotheses and run sensitivity analysis on key assumptions.
- Translate findings into the decision economics. Kotler's airline example: at one price half of first-class passengers would buy in-flight connectivity, at a lower price 60 percent would, so the higher price earns more per flight; annualize and compare with the per-plane investment to get payback, then add image and goodwill effects.
- The manager decides; research raises confidence or prompts more study. Separate **data** from **insight**: information is not knowledge until it is interpreted against the problem.

## Measuring market demand

Forecasts drive finance, capacity, purchasing and hiring, so define terms precisely.

| Concept | Definition |
|---|---|
| Market demand | Total volume bought by a defined customer group, in a defined area and period, in a defined environment, under a defined marketing program |
| Company demand | The company's estimated share of market demand at alternative levels of its marketing effort (depends on how its offering and spend compare with competitors'; modeled with sales-response functions) |
| Market forecast | Market demand at the expected level of industry marketing spend |
| Company sales forecast | Expected company sales under a chosen plan and assumed environment |
| Sales quota | A goal for a line, division or rep, usually set a little above the forecast to stretch effort |
| Sales budget | A conservative estimate for purchasing, production and cash planning, usually a little below the forecast |
| Total market potential | The limit market demand approaches as industry marketing spend becomes very high, in a given environment (higher in boom than recession). Estimate: potential buyers x average quantity per buyer x price |
| Company sales potential | The limit company demand approaches as its effort rises relative to competitors'; below market potential because rivals keep hard-core loyal buyers |

### Forecasting

Typical sequence: macroeconomic forecast (inflation, unemployment, rates, spending, investment, GDP) → industry forecast → company forecast (assumed share). Firms build their own or buy from research, forecasting and futurist firms.

All forecasts rest on **what people say, what people do, or what people have done**:

| Method | Basis | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Industry sales and market share | Have done | Compare growth with the industry (growing 5 percent in a market growing 10 percent means losing ground); retail audits; B2B firms often lack competitor data |
| Survey of buyers' intentions (including **conjoint analysis** of how buyers value features, service and price) | Say | Best when buyers are few, cheap to reach, and have clear intentions they will disclose and act on; good for industrial goods, durables, planned purchases, new products |
| Composite of sales-force opinions | Say | Grassroots detail by product, territory, customer and rep; adjust for optimism, pessimism and sandbagging to get lower quotas; give reps plan information and past accuracy |
| Expert opinion (dealers, distributors, suppliers, consultants, trade associations, forecasting firms; group discussion, pooled estimates, **Delphi** rounds) | Say | Useful where data are thin |
| Past-sales analysis (**time series**: trend, cycle, seasonal, erratic; exponential smoothing; statistical demand analysis; econometrics; machine learning) | Have done | Assumes the future resembles the past unless causal factors are modeled |
| Market test | Do | For new products, new channels or territories, or when buyers do not plan and experts are unreliable |

## Measuring marketing productivity

Inputs are easy to count; outputs (awareness, image, loyalty, new-product prospects) take time and are entangled with other changes. Use two complementary approaches and a dashboard.

### Marketing metrics

- Choose metrics for the problem at hand. Examples: long-term brand strength (awareness, consideration, trial) plus program metrics (impressions, traffic, conversion); online efficiency (cost per acquisition, cost per click, CPM, revenue by search and display); website, social and email engagement.
- Ambler's split: **short-term results** (sales, shareholder value) and **changes in brand equity** (awareness, attitudes, behavior, share, relative price premium, complaints, distribution and availability, customer count, perceived quality, loyalty and retention).
- Add internal metrics: innovation (share of sales from recent products) and employee measures, because staff are your first customers.

### Marketing-mix modeling

- Regression and other multivariate models on scanner, shipment, price, media and promotion data estimate the effect of each activity and guide reallocation (wasted spend, optimal and minimum levels).
- Limits: weak on how elements work together; focuses on incremental lift rather than baseline and long-term effects; poorly integrates satisfaction, awareness and brand equity; usually ignores competitors, the trade and the sales force (often the largest spend).

### Marketing dashboards

- A concise set of interconnected performance drivers viewed in common across the organization, with two scorecards: **performance** and **early warning**.
- Four measurement pathways:
  - **Customer metrics**: how prospects become customers (awareness → preference → trial → repeat) and how experience builds value.
  - **Unit metrics**: units by line and geography, marketing cost per unit, where margin is made by product and channel.
  - **Cash-flow metrics**: short-term return and net present value of programs and campaigns.
  - **Brand metrics**: long-term perceptual and financial health of the brand.
- Start broad, then reduce to a few key drivers. Use clear visualization; a marketing controller can own data quality. Guard against losing the big picture, misusing personal data, and pressuring staff with numbers.

## Six ways to draw ideas from customers

1. Observe how customers use your product (and competitors').
2. Ask about their problems with your product.
3. Ask about their dream product, even if it sounds impossible.
4. Actively solicit feedback through panels, forums and owner groups.
5. Build a brand community of enthusiasts.
6. Give customers toolkits to change or improve the product themselves.

## Template: research brief

```markdown
# Research brief: <topic>

## Decision
The business question, the decision it informs, the decision owner and deadline.

## Value of information
What we would do without research; what research could change; maximum justified spend.

## Sub-questions and research objectives
1. ...

## Type
Exploratory / descriptive / causal (and why).

## Plan
- Secondary data to check first (internal, external).
- Primary approach(es): observation, ethnography, focus groups, survey, behavioral, experiment.
- Instruments: questionnaire outline with question types; qualitative techniques; devices.
- Sampling: unit and frame, size, procedure (probability or not).
- Contact method(s).

## Analysis
Planned tabulations, models, hypotheses, sensitivity checks.

## Output
How findings will be presented, and the decision rule ("launch if at least X percent would pay Y").

## Ethics and privacy
Consent, data handling, incentives, no deception.
```

## Worked example: should a project-management SaaS add a paid AI assistant?

- **Business question**: will an AI assistant add enough incremental revenue and retention to justify its cost against other roadmap options? Sub-questions: which plans should include it, at what price, for which teams?
- **Too broad**: "what do teams want from AI?" **Too narrow**: "will 10 percent of users pay 8 per seat?"
- **Value of information**: building it costs about 400,000 a year; a wrong price could forfeit an estimated 150,000 of margin; cap research at about 40,000.
- **Plan**: product analytics on how people use the existing summary feature (behavioral, secondary); eight contextual interviews with team leads watching them prepare status reports (ethnographic, with laddering to reach the benefit 'look on top of things in front of my boss'); then a conjoint survey of 600 admins from a probability sample of the customer base to value assistant features against price; finally a pricing experiment showing two prices to matched cohorts of new trials.
- **Analysis**: conjoint shows willingness to pay is concentrated in teams of 20 or more; the experiment shows a higher price loses few conversions in that segment.
- **Demand**: market potential = eligible paying teams x expected seats x price; company forecast assumes take-up from the experiment; set the sales quota slightly above and the budget slightly below.
- **Dashboard**: adoption rate, assistant-weekly-active share, add-on revenue, churn difference between adopters and non-adopters (performance); support tickets about wrong answers and cost per query (early warning).

## Workflow

1. Write the business question and decision; test it for being too broad or too narrow and challenge its assumptions.
2. Estimate the value of information and cap the research budget.
3. Check internal and secondary data before buying primary research.
4. Pick approaches and instruments to match the research type; combine qualitative and quantitative methods.
5. Draft the questionnaire or guide with the right question types; pretest it.
6. Specify sampling unit and frame, size and procedure; choose contact methods; plan for non-response.
7. Analyze with summary statistics, models and sensitivity checks; turn results into decision economics and a recommendation.
8. For sizing, separate market potential, market forecast, company forecast, quota and budget; triangulate at least two forecasting methods.
9. Define metrics across the four pathways and build a dashboard with performance and early-warning scorecards; review and prune it.

## Red flags

- A research request that cannot name the decision it will change.
- Spending more on research than the decision is worth, or nothing on a decision worth millions.
- Generalizing from a handful of focus groups or a convenience sample.
- Leading or ambiguous questions; long surveys that cause burnout; no pretest.
- Trusting stated intentions over observed behavior without checking.
- Claiming causality from correlations or campaign before-and-after data without a control.
- Treating market potential, forecast and quota as the same number; forecasts with no stated assumptions about marketing spend and environment.
- Sales-force forecasts taken at face value.
- Dashboards with dozens of vanity metrics and no early-warning indicators or link to profit and brand health.
- Marketing-mix models that leave out sales force and trade spend.
- Customer data used without consent, or tracking and facial recognition deployed without transparency.
