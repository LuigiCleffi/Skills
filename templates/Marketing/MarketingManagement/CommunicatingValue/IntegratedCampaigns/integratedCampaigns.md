# Designing an Integrated Marketing Campaign in the Digital Age

Integrated marketing communications (IMC) is the coordinated use of communication tools that work in concert and reinforce one another to achieve the company's strategic goals. Without explicit integration, a campaign becomes a pile of unrelated or conflicting messages made by separate teams in separate channels. Take a 360-degree view of how the customer meets the brand, give each channel the job it does best, make sure every channel shares the core meaning while adding different associations, and judge the mix on coverage, contribution, commonality, complementarity, conformability and cost.

## Managing IMC

### Four levels of integration

- **Horizontal**: coordinate communication with every other marketing action: packaging, pricing, promotions, distribution.
- **Vertical**: align communication objectives with the higher-level marketing and corporate goals.
- **Internal**: bring in information from product development, research, sales and customer service.
- **External**: coordinate with agencies (advertising, social, PR), event organizers and co-sponsors.

Agencies increasingly sell one-stop communication services; integration is still the client's job. IMC gives someone clear responsibility for unifying the brand image and messages across thousands of company activities.

### Six criteria for an integrated program

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| **Coverage** | What share of the target does each option reach, and how much do options overlap (same or different people)? |
| **Contribution** | What can this option achieve on its own (awareness, image, response, sales) without exposure to any other? |
| **Commonality** | Do options reinforce the same core associations and meaning? Consistency makes associations easy to recall and new ones easy to attach. |
| **Complementarity** | Do options add *different* associations each is best suited to build (a cause sponsorship for trust, TV for performance claims)? |
| **Conformability** | Does each option work both for people who have seen the other communications and for those who have not? |
| **Cost** | Weighed against all the above, is this the most effective and efficient mix? |

Use it as a scorecard:

| Channel | Coverage of target | Overlap with other channels | Standalone contribution | Core associations reinforced | Unique association added | Works for first-time viewers? | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | | | | |

## Advertising

Any paid presentation and promotion by an identified sponsor using paid media time or space.

### Television

- Strengths:
  - Vividly demonstrates attributes and explains benefits.
  - Dramatizes user and usage imagery, personality and intangibles.
  - Reaches captive audiences during live events.
  - A distinctive, persistent character can lift recognition dramatically (Kotler cites an insurer whose mascot took name recognition from low teens to over 90%).
- Limits: fleeting exposure; creative elements can overshadow the brand; clutter; high cost per viewer compared with online video (but online video on small screens has less impact).
- Streaming and ad-free platforms shift viewing control to the consumer; brands must find new ways to engage across programming and devices.
- Best used in combination with other media.

### Print

- Read at the reader's pace: good for detailed information and for user and usage imagery; weak for demonstrations; fairly passive.
- Newspapers: timely, pervasive, strong for local and retail ads; poor reproduction and short shelf life; declining, moving online.
- Magazines: better for building user and usage imagery.
- What matters, in order: **picture, headline, copy**. The picture draws attention, the headline reinforces it and leads into copy, the copy engages, the brand name is prominent. Even outstanding ads are noticed by under half the audience and read in full by under 10%, so print ads must be clear, consistent and well branded.

### Radio and audio

- Pervasive (most people listen daily, often in cars and out of home).
- Flexibility: highly targeted stations, cheap production and placement, short lead times; strong in the morning; balances broad and local coverage; local personalities.
- Podcasts extend reach and on-demand listening.
- Limits: no visuals, passive processing; overcome with music, sound and imaginative copy.

### Online advertising

- Advantages:
  - Trackable (unique visitors, clicks, time spent, actions, next destination).
  - Easy to test and optimize messages and creative.
  - Contextual placement on related sites; keyword targeting at the moment of search.
  - Many formats, from text to video to interactive experiences.
- Disadvantages: easy to screen out; click fraud from bots inflates results; less control (hacking, vandalism, unsafe adjacency).
- **Native advertising**: paid messages matching the look and feel of the surrounding editorial, designed to be non-disruptive. Three common formats: content recommendations, in-feed ads on social networks, promoted search listings. Also used in print, TV and radio. Label it clearly as sponsored: disguising ads as editorial misleads readers and breaks advertising rules.

### Place advertising

- **Billboards**: the '15-second sell'; must be grasped instantly; strong creative (3-D or interactive elements) can generate buzz far beyond the site.
- **Public spaces**: cinemas, transit, street furniture, elevators, stadiums, gas pumps, even fruit labels; naming rights for venues. Used to create memorable impressions as traditional media weaken.
- **Product placement**: paid or supplied products in films, TV and video; strongest when woven into the story; can be amplified with tie-in ads and promotions off screen. Disclose paid placements where rules require.
- **Point of purchase**: many brand decisions are made in store: cart and shelf ads, floor graphics, demonstrations, sampling, instant coupons, in-store radio and mobile messages in the store.

## Online communication

Customers spend more than half their media time online and set the rules of engagement: what information they want, what they will pay, and which intermediaries filter for them.

### Company website and microsites

- The site should express purpose, history, products and vision; attractive at first view and interesting enough for repeat visits.
- Visitors judge **ease of use** (understandable, navigable content) and **physical attractiveness** (layout, fonts, color). Delight with the site raises downstream behavior (test drives, purchases).
- Track page views, unique visitors and visit length; build user-centric privacy controls, automate where possible and minimize intrusions.
- **Microsites**: supplementary pages placed where customers already are (advice for used-car buyers with an insurance offer on a car site).

### Driving traffic: SEO and SEM

- **SEO**: improve the chance your link ranks high in organic (unpaid) results for relevant searches; cheaper than SEM.
- **SEM (paid search)**: pay search engines to appear with results for chosen keywords; usually pay per click; cost depends on position and keyword popularity; rising competition makes keyword choice, bidding and monitoring critical.
- Guidelines:
  - Broad terms build the brand; specific model or service terms generate and convert leads.
  - Feature the search terms on the right pages so engines can find them.
  - Bid on each keyword by its probable return.

## Social media

Social media let consumers share text, images, audio and video with each other and with companies. They give the brand a public voice and presence, reinforce other communication cheaply, push the firm to stay current, and can build communities that become long-term assets.

Realities:

- Social media are rarely the sole source of a brand's communication.
- Brands vary in how 'social' they are: people engage most with media, charities and fashion, least with packaged goods. Most people use brand social channels for information, deals or entertainment; few want two-way conversations. **Only some consumers want to engage with some brands, some of the time.**
- 'Always-on' audiences expect near-instant responses; build real-time capability to address problems and seize opportunities.
- Take the good with the bad: popular contests can crash, criticism happens whether or not you participate. A strong, constructive presence lets you offer credible counterpoints, and a loyal community often defends the brand.

### The four platforms

- **Online communities and forums**: consumer-run or company-sponsored. Success comes from activities that bond members. They provide two-way, hard-to-get insight (often better than focus groups) and can crowdsource ideas that become products.
- **Blogs**: outlets for word of mouth, from personal to mass audience. Companies run their own and monitor others. Bloggers must disclose relationships with marketers they endorse; some consumers blog or post video to retaliate for bad service.
- **Social networks**: important in B2C and B2B. Users are there to connect, not to shop, so attention and persuasion are hard; ads may appear beside inappropriate content.
  - Most content is never shared; only a small fraction cascades beyond the first recipient. People share for intrinsic reasons (fun, learning) and more often extrinsic ones (social and self-image).
  - Advice for shareable video (from a viral-video researcher cited in the chapter):
    - Show the brand in short, unobtrusive pulses.
    - Open with joy or surprise.
    - Build an emotional roller coaster.
    - Surprise but do not shock (discomfort kills sharing).
  - Viral content often follows the rules of entertainment more than selling (surf videos, parenting advice, money-saving tips, record labels, TV channels), but its success depends on consumers' willingness to pass it on.
- **Customer reviews**: among the most trusted brand information sources, second only to friends and family.
  - Ratings skew positive because later raters conform to earlier positive ratings.
  - Negative reviews weigh more than positive ones.
  - A few 'influentials' carry outsized weight with particular consumers.

### Influencer marketing

Paying a popular online figure to promote the offering within their own feed: a mix of publicity and paid endorsement.

- Demand has driven fees up sharply.
- Fraud is widespread: bought followers, views, retweets and endorsements inflate apparent reach and fees.
- Vet influencers with audit tools that detect bot activity and estimate real followers and engagement.
- Require clear disclosure of the paid relationship; never buy fake followers, reviews or engagement for your own brand.

## Mobile communication

Four distinctive traits of a mobile device:

1. Tied to one user.
2. Virtually always on.
3. Allows immediate consumption (it is a channel with payment built in).
4. Highly interactive (location, photos, video).

- Go beyond 'mini-billboards': apps that add convenience, social value, incentives and entertainment.
- Loyalty programs that track visits and purchases.
- Location-based offers to customers who **opted in** (texts with offers when near a store).
- Mobile coupons at or near the point of purchase, and digital signs that send offers to phones.
- Cross-screen identity for relevance, but only with privacy safeguards.
- Small screens and fleeting attention make informing and persuading hard, but phone users are more engaged; some commerce apps drop ads entirely to minimize clicks to purchase.

## Events and experiences

Becoming part of a personally relevant moment in customers' lives broadens and deepens the relationship.

### Why sponsor events

- **Identify with a target market or lifestyle** (sampling at the audience's sports).
- **Increase salience** of the name through sustained exposure.
- **Create or reinforce key brand associations** (events carry their own associations).
- **Enhance corporate image**: likable, prestigious, patriotic or community-minded.
- **Create experiences and evoke feelings** that link indirectly to the brand.
- **Express commitment to the community or social issues** (cause-related events).
- **Entertain key clients or reward employees** (hospitality, morale, incentives).
- **Permit merchandising or promotional tie-ins** (contests, in-store merchandising, direct response).

Risks: outcomes can be unpredictable and outside the sponsor's control; some consumers resent commercialization of events.

### Choosing and running sponsorships

- Choose events that meet the brand's marketing objectives and communication strategy.
- The event needs sufficient awareness, the desired image and the ability to create the desired effects.
- The audience must match the target market and view the sponsorship favorably.
- The ideal event is unique, not crowded with sponsors, lends itself to ancillary activity, and reflects or enhances the brand.
- Design the sponsorship program, then measure its effects (see measurement in marketing communications).

### Experiential marketing

Connects the offering to unique, interesting experiences that show how it fits in the customer's life, rather than selling features. Forms:

- Company tours and corporate museums.
- Off-site brand worlds and flagship experiences.
- Factory pickup and delivery experiences (fly to the factory, tour, drive the new car).
- Pop-ups and stunts that stimulate consumer and media involvement.

## Word of mouth

People talk about many brands every day; some brands were built mainly by word of mouth.

- **Viral marketing**: encouraging consumers to pass on audio, video or written company content online. Online video is cheap and allows creative freedom (a blender maker's deadpan videos blending current gadgets drew millions of views).
- Outrageousness cuts both ways: add safety framing ('don't try this at home') and practical content alongside.
- Products need not be edgy to be discussed. Brands seen as interesting are discussed more **online**; **face-to-face** talk favors brands that are salient, visible and come to mind easily.
- People mostly spread positive word of mouth about their own experiences and pass on negative stories they heard from others.
- Word of mouth can be managed and facilitated: liberal sampling, product placement, engaging affinity groups whose values fit the product, attention-getting installations, and PR rather than ads ('someone else talking'). A sharp launch video that hits a real pain point can build a business.
- Paid and owned media should be designed to earn media (commentary, posts, discussion). Never fabricate buzz, reviews or 'customers'.

## Publicity and public relations

| | Publicity | Public relations |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Attention to the company or offering | Manage overall reputation and build relationships with the community and stakeholders |
| Means | Editorial space: news stories, articles, editorials | Programs to promote or protect image with all relevant publics |

### Publicity

- Advantages: no media cost; third-party source makes it more credible than ads.
- Downside: no direct control; it can be irrelevant or even harmful. Unpredictability is the price of low cost and credibility.
- Objectives: build awareness, build credibility (editorial context), boost sales force and dealer enthusiasm before launch, hold down promotion cost.
- Uses:
  - **Launching new products.**
  - **Repositioning mature products** or places.
  - **Building interest in a category** (trade associations reviving commodities).
  - **Defending products** in public trouble (crisis management).
  - **Building a corporate image** that reflects on products (executive keynotes).
- Plan it jointly with advertising and other communications. Kotler uses a pet-food brand that revived its old jingle with a celebrity duet and a charity donation per download to win massive coverage.

### Public relations

The best PR departments monitor public attitudes, build goodwill, and counsel management to adopt positive programs and drop questionable practices **before** they create bad publicity.

Three functions:

- **Press coverage**: present news about the organization in the best light.
- **Corporate communications**: internal and external understanding of the organization.
- **Lobbying**: dealing with legislators and officials on legislation and regulation.

PR toolkit:

- **Publications**: annual reports, brochures, articles, newsletters, magazines, audiovisual material.
- **Events**: news conferences, seminars, outings, trade shows, exhibits, contests, anniversaries.
- **News**: find or create favorable stories; get releases used and conferences attended.
- **Speeches**: executives answering media and speaking at associations and sales meetings.
- **Public service activities**: money and time for good causes.
- **Identity media**: logos, stationery, brochures, signs, forms, cards, buildings, uniforms, dress codes.

PR is central to limiting damage in a crisis and rebuilding afterward.

## Packaging as communication

The package is often the first encounter, shapes evaluation, and drives the final choice. The **label** identifies the offering, describes key attributes, highlights benefits, instructs on use, storage and disposal, adds aesthetic appeal and leverages the brand.

Three principles:

- **Visibility**: cut through information clutter and arrest attention (a distinctive bottle shape made a soap brand famous).
- **Differentiation**: let time-pressed shoppers, and loyal ones, spot the brand at a glance.
- **Transparency**: clearly communicate the value proposition and give a reason to buy at the shelf (color cues such as green for sustainable products, used truthfully).

Packaging vs advertising: advertising builds a memorable impression for future purchase; packaging has an almost instant effect at the point of purchase, so it needs direct visual impact, especially for familiar low-cost products bought with little thought.

## Measuring social media ROI

Many firms spend heavily on social media but few quantify its business impact; counting likes and posts does not reliably track business results. Four categories of benefit (from a digital analyst cited in the chapter):

- **Short-term financial**: more revenue (reviews on product pages lifting sales) or lower costs (community members answering support questions).
- **Short-term digital**: better search visibility and traffic from reviews, videos, communities.
- **Long-term brand lift**: preference and purchase intent among engaged users.
- **Long-term risk avoidance**: monitoring that catches problems before they become crises.

Tools range from free alerts to paid monitoring and visualization platforms that track mentions, competitors, influencers and sentiment. The simplest way to tie social activity to results is a trackable contest, promotion or offer redeemed in store or online.

## Worked example: IMC plan for a DTC outdoor-gear brand launching a recycled jacket

| Channel | Job (complementarity) | Core message (commonality) | Coverage note | Measure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Online video (YouTube, CTV) | Demonstrate waterproofing and warmth | 'Made from 40 bottles. Built for 40 winters.' | Broad reach of outdoor enthusiasts | View-through, brand lift |
| Paid search + SEO | Capture people searching 'recycled waterproof jacket' | Same line on landing page | High-intent, small | Cost per order |
| Community forum and reviews | Credibility from real users | Verified-buyer reviews, honest about fit | Existing customers | Review volume, rating, return rate |
| Influencers (vetted, disclosed) | Lifestyle imagery in the target's feeds | Real field tests | Overlaps video; check for fake followers | Tracked codes, engagement quality |
| Trail-race sponsorship and repair pop-up | Associate with durability and repair culture | 'Built for 40 winters' plus free repair | Local, deep | Sign-ups, sentiment |
| PR | Third-party proof of the recycling claim | Lifecycle data verified by an independent lab | Media and opinion leaders | Coverage tone, share of voice |
| Packaging and hangtag | Transparency at purchase | Bottle count, care and repair instructions | Every buyer | In-store conversion |

Conformability check: the video and hangtag each explain the bottle line for someone who saw nothing else; the forum and influencer posts deepen it for those who did.

## Workflow

1. Start from the communication objectives, audience and positioning set in the communication plan.
2. Map every touchpoint where the target meets the brand (360-degree view), including packaging, store, service and employees.
3. Assign each channel a job: the association or response it is best suited to build (complementarity).
4. Write the core message and brand cues every channel must carry (commonality); check each execution works stand-alone (conformability).
5. Choose advertising media by their strengths: TV for demonstration and imagery, print for detail, radio for flexible local reach, online for targeting and testing, place media for context and point of purchase.
6. Build owned media and traffic: website usability and attractiveness, microsites, SEO for organic terms, SEM bids by expected return.
7. Plan social media realistically: which platforms (communities, blogs, networks, reviews), what content worth sharing, response capacity in real time, influencer vetting and disclosure.
8. Add mobile, events, experiences, word-of-mouth seeding and PR where they add coverage or associations other channels cannot.
9. Score the mix on coverage, contribution, commonality, complementarity, conformability and cost; cut channels that add cost without unique contribution.
10. Integrate horizontally (price, promotion, distribution), vertically (strategy), internally (sales, service, R&D data) and externally (agencies, partners); name one owner for brand consistency.
11. Define measurement per channel and for the whole: financial, digital, brand lift and risk avoidance; use trackable offers where possible.

## Red flags

- Each channel run by a different agency with its own message and no shared brief.
- The TV, social, packaging and sales deck describe the brand differently.
- Every channel repeats the same message with no channel adding a distinct association.
- Ads that only make sense if you have seen the previous ones.
- Native ads or sponsored posts not labeled as advertising; undisclosed influencer payments or product placements.
- Influencers chosen by follower count alone, with no bot or audience audit.
- Bought followers, fake reviews or staged 'customer' content.
- A social strategy assuming customers want to chat with a packaged-goods brand every day.
- No team or process to respond in hours when something goes wrong online.
- Sponsorships chosen because an executive likes the sport, with no audience fit or measurement.
- PR used only to push releases, not to counsel management on practices that will cause bad press.
- Paid search bidding on every term with no return-based bid strategy; SEO ignored.
- Location-based mobile messages sent to people who never opted in.
- Social media success reported as likes and follower counts with no revenue, cost, brand or risk measure.
