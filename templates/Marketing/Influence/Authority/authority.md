# Authority

People are trained to defer to legitimate authority, and that deference often runs automatically, triggered by symbols (titles, uniforms, expensive trappings) rather than by actual expertise. Credible authority requires two things: real, relevant expertise and trustworthiness. Show genuine credentials that bear on the claim, admit honest limits, and never borrow the look of expertise (actors as doctors, invented titles, fake badges) to sell what the evidence does not support.

## The strength of obedience

- In Milgram's experiments, ordinary people asked by a lab-coated researcher kept administering what they believed were severe, dangerous shocks to a protesting victim. About two thirds went to the maximum. Experts had predicted almost no one would.
- Sex, ignorance of danger and sadism did not explain it. Participants hated what they were doing; they obeyed because the authority insisted. When the authority told them to stop, they stopped; when the victim (not the authority) demanded continuation, no one continued; when two authorities disagreed, everyone stopped.
- Obedience is taught from childhood through family, school, religion, law and the military, because a structured system of authority makes complex societies possible.
- **Usually it is a good shortcut**: authorities typically know more and control rewards. Because it usually works, people stop thinking when an authority speaks, and that is where errors and exploitation enter.
- Cialdini's hospital example: a poorly abbreviated ear-drop prescription was administered in the wrong place because nobody questioned the doctor's order. In another study, nearly all nurses prepared an unauthorized drug at double the labeled maximum because a stranger on the phone claimed to be a doctor.

## Connotation, not content

When people are in click, whirr mode they respond to the symbols of authority as readily as to the substance. An actor known for playing a doctor sold a decaffeinated coffee on health grounds for years. Three symbol types matter most:

### Titles

- The hardest symbol to earn and the easiest to fake. A professor who mentions his title finds conversation partners suddenly deferential and agreeable.
- **Status inflates perceived size**: the same man introduced with higher academic rank was judged progressively taller. Importance distorts perception generally (valued coins, high-stakes cards seen as bigger).
- The outward signs of authority can be counterfeited with flimsy materials.

### Clothes

- A requester in a security-guard uniform got far more compliance with odd street requests, even after he had walked away. Observers drastically underestimated the uniform's effect.
- A jaywalker in a business suit drew several times more followers into traffic than the same man in work clothes.
- Con artists combine uniforms and conservative suits in impersonation schemes.

### Trappings

- Fine clothes, jewelry and luxury cars carry status. Drivers waited far longer before honking at a luxury car than at an economy car, and predicted the opposite of what they did.

### Authority's surprise factor

- Across studies, people consistently underestimate how much authority and its symbols affect them. That surprise is part of why it works.

## Credibility: expertise plus trustworthiness

- **Expertise**: real knowledge and credentials **relevant to the claim**. A business suit says nothing about crossing streets; an actor's fame says nothing about health.
- **Trustworthiness**: we are swayed more by experts who seem impartial than by those who gain from persuading us.
- **Arguing against one's own interest**: admitting a minor drawback ('a bit more expensive', 'we are number two') makes the main claims more believable. Cialdini describes a waiter who steered large parties to slightly cheaper dishes, then sold expensive wine and dessert as a now-trusted adviser. The tactic is honest only when the admission and the subsequent recommendation are both genuine.

## Honest use in marketing

| Element | Honest application | Counterfeit (never) |
|---|---|---|
| Expert endorsers | Real practitioners with relevant credentials, named and verifiable; disclose compensation | Actors in lab coats; 'doctors recommend' with no survey behind it; credentials unrelated to the claim |
| Titles and credentials | Accurate team bios, certifications actually held, licenses with numbers | Invented titles, purchased or self-awarded 'awards', implied affiliation with institutions |
| Trust badges | Real security certifications, audit reports, payment-provider marks used per their terms | Decorative 'secure' or 'certified' icons with nothing behind them |
| Media logos | 'As featured in' only for genuine editorial coverage | Logos for paid placements or press releases presented as coverage |
| Evidence | Cite studies accurately: who, sample size, what was measured | 'Clinically proven' on a single in-house test; cherry-picked results |
| Trappings | Professional design, quality packaging and service that match real quality | Premium look disguising a weak product |
| Two-sided messages | Admit real limitations and say who the product is not for | Staged 'confessions' of trivial flaws to buy credibility for false claims |
| Thought leadership | Publish original, useful expertise (data, frameworks, how-to) | Ghost-written content attributed to people who did not write or review it |

Additional rules:

- **Match the authority to the claim**: a cybersecurity claim needs a security expert or auditor, not a famous founder.
- **Expertise is shown, not only stated**: demos, worked examples, transparent methodology and specific answers build authority faster than titles.
- **Regulated categories** (health, finance, legal) have specific rules on expert claims and endorsements; check them.
- **Do not exploit deference to override customer judgment** on high-stakes decisions; give them what they need to check.

## Worked example: B2B security software

A vendor of cloud-security monitoring sells to mid-market CTOs.

- **Relevant expertise**: the landing page leads with the head of research (named, former incident responder, links to talks and CVEs disclosed), not a celebrity investor.
- **Verifiable credentials**: SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 badges link to the auditor's letter or trust portal.
- **Third-party authority**: an analyst report is quoted with date and exact wording; peer-review ratings link to the review site.
- **Trustworthiness**: a 'when we are not the right fit' section says the product does not cover on-premise networks and recommends a category alternative.
- **Content authority**: a quarterly threat report with transparent methodology and raw numbers.
- **Never**: a stock-photo 'security expert', badges for certifications in progress presented as complete, or 'used by Fortune 500 companies' based on a single free-tier sign-up.

## Defending against counterfeit authority

- **Remove the surprise**: remember that authority symbols move people far more than they expect.
- **Question 1: 'Is this authority truly an expert?'** Check the credentials and whether they are relevant to this claim.
- **Question 2: 'How truthful can we expect this expert to be here?'** Ask what they gain from your compliance. Be alert to a small admission against interest used to prepare a bigger ask.
- Authorities are usually right; the goal is not blanket resistance but noticing when a symbol is standing in for substance.

## Workflow

1. List the claims the campaign must make credible (efficacy, safety, ROI, security, quality).
2. For each claim, identify the most relevant genuine authority: in-house experts, customers who are experts, independent certifiers, analysts, research.
3. Gather verifiable evidence: credentials, certifications, study details, coverage links.
4. Present expertise concretely (bio, track record, methodology) and early, before the main argument (current practice consistent with the chapter: establish credibility before persuading).
5. Establish trustworthiness: disclose relationships, include honest limitations and fit guidance.
6. Align visual trappings with the real quality level; do not let design overpromise.
7. Check regulatory rules for expert claims and endorsements in the category and market.
8. Audit for counterfeits: actors, irrelevant credentials, decorative badges, misattributed content.

## Red flags

- An endorser whose fame or title is unrelated to the claim, presented as if it were relevant.
- Actors or stock photos portraying doctors, engineers or 'experts'.
- 'Experts agree', 'clinically proven' or 'doctor recommended' without a cited source.
- Badges, seals or awards that are not actually held, or are purchased.
- 'As seen in' logos for paid or self-published placements.
- Credibility built with a trivial admission and then spent on an inflated recommendation.
- Premium visual trappings masking a commodity or inferior product.
- Content bylined to executives who did not write or review it.
- Sales scripts that discourage buyers from checking claims ('trust me, I have done this for 20 years').
