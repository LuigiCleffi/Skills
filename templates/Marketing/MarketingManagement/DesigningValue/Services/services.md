# Designing and Managing Services

A service is an act one party performs for another that is essentially intangible and transfers no ownership. Because services are intangible, inseparable from their providers, variable and perishable, they need their own marketing: make the intangible visible, standardize and train for consistency, balance demand with capacity, and manage the gap between what customers expect and what they perceive. When products become hard to differentiate, service is often where the advantage lies.

## Where service sits in the offering

| Category | Example | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Pure tangible good | Salt, soap | Service barely matters. |
| Tangible good with accompanying services | Car, laptop, phone with support | The more technical the product, the more support quality matters. |
| Hybrid | Restaurant meal | Goods and service count equally. |
| Major service with minor goods | Air travel | Service is primary; goods support it. |
| Pure service | Therapy, massage, babysitting | Everything is the service encounter. |

## Search, experience and credence benefits

- **Search**: can be evaluated before purchase (price, color, location).
- **Experience**: can be evaluated only after purchase (a meal, a haircut).
- **Credence**: hard to evaluate even after consumption (an audit, surgery, legal advice).

Services are high in experience and credence qualities, so buying them feels riskier. Consequences:

- Buyers rely more on word of mouth than on advertising.
- They judge quality from price, provider and physical cues.
- They are very loyal to providers who satisfy them, and inertia makes it hard to win customers away from a competitor.
- In a social-media world, one visible service failure can destroy that loyalty fast (Kotler uses a disabled cruise ship to show how a single failure hit a whole category's bookings).

## The four characteristics of services

### Intangibility

Services cannot be seen, tasted, felt, heard or smelled before purchase. Buyers infer quality from place, people, equipment, communication material, symbols and price. The provider's task is to **manage the evidence**: 'tangibilize the intangible'.

- Facilities, signage, reception, employee appearance, collateral and the delivery vehicle all carry the brand.
- Brand elements (logos, characters, slogans, metaphors) make the key benefit concrete.
- Signature touches symbolize care (a small gift at check-in, a branded help desk).
- Firms selling trust (finance, insurance) use solid addresses, buildings and sponsorships to signal stability.

### Inseparability

Services are usually produced and consumed at the same time; the provider is part of the service and the client is often present. Provider-client interaction is central. When clients strongly prefer a specific provider, price can ration that provider's time.

Ways around the limits:

- Work with larger groups (one-to-many sessions, cohorts, webinars).
- Work faster with a more structured session.
- Train more providers and build confidence in the network.
- **Yield management**: vary price so demand matches capacity at each time.

### Variability

Quality depends on who provides the service, when, where and to whom. Buyers know this and research before choosing. Three steps toward quality control:

1. **Hire and train well.** Trained staff show six traits: competence, courtesy, credibility, reliability, responsiveness, communication skill.
2. **Standardize the process.** A **service blueprint** maps the process, each customer contact point and the evidence the customer sees. Its layers: customer actions; the line of interaction; frontstage (visible) employee actions; the line of visibility; backstage actions; the line of internal interaction; support processes and systems. Use it to find pain points, design new services, build a zero-defects culture and plan service recovery.
3. **Monitor satisfaction** with suggestion and complaint systems, surveys, third-party comparison shopping, regional programs and customer databases for personalization.

Service guarantees reduce perceived risk from variability.

### Perishability

Services cannot be stored; unused capacity is lost and peaks require extra capacity. Some providers charge for no-shows because the value existed only at that time.

Demand-side strategies:

- **Differential pricing** to shift demand off-peak (matinees, weekend rates).
- **Cultivate nonpeak demand** (breakfast, mini-break weekends).
- **Complementary services** for people waiting (a bar while waiting for a table, ATMs).
- **Reservation systems** to manage demand levels.
- **Dynamic or yield pricing** that adjusts prices with expected demand (Kotler uses a zoo that moved visits to weekdays and raised admission revenue).

Supply-side strategies:

- **Part-time staff** for peaks.
- **Peak-time efficiency routines** (only essential tasks; paraprofessionals assist).
- **More customer participation** (customers fill in forms, self-bag).
- **Shared services** (equipment shared across providers).
- **Facilities for future expansion.**
- New delivery formats that add capacity (drive-through windows, pickup lockers).

## The new service realities

- **Technology**: changes delivery (digital banking, accelerated by the pandemic) and boosts staff productivity. Do not push efficiency so hard that perceived quality drops; blend automation with a human route (live chat, escalation). Personalization and real-time adjustment need privacy and security safeguards, and customers must be reassured about them.
- **Customer empowerment**: customers want unbundled services and fewer providers to deal with; social media and review sites amplify bad experiences more than good ones. Respond fast on every channel, monitor public posts and reach out, but above all prevent the dissatisfaction from recurring.
- **Customer coproduction**: customers help deliver the service, affecting their own and others' experience and staff productivity. A large share of service problems are caused by customers, and self-service raises it. Customers still hold the firm responsible. Remedies:
  - Redesign processes and redefine customer roles to simplify encounters.
  - Give employees and customers the right technology (detect issues before customers do).
  - Create 'high-performance customers': clear roles, motivation, ability.
  - Encourage **customer citizenship**: customers helping each other and modeling good behavior.
- **Employee satisfaction**: positive employee attitudes drive customer loyalty. Contact staff thrive when they want to pamper customers, read their needs accurately, build personal relationships and solve problems. Market a career, not a job; train, support and reward; reinforce customer-centered attitudes internally; audit job satisfaction regularly. Empower reps to break policy to make things right.

## Service excellence: three kinds of marketing

- **External marketing**: preparing, pricing, distributing and promoting the service to customers.
- **Internal marketing**: training and motivating employees to serve customers well. The marketing department's biggest contribution may be getting everyone else to practice marketing.
- **Interactive marketing**: employees' skill in serving the client, both 'high tech' and 'high touch'. Teamwork and delegated authority to the front line improve flexibility and problem solving.

## Best practices of top service companies

- **Customer-centricity**: a clear target and distinctive strategy for it; seeing the business from the customer's side; proactively solving needs customers have not voiced, not just delivering what they request. Examples: rigorous multi-interview hiring, staff who track guest preferences, small dedicated teams per client.
- **Commitment to service quality**: leaders review service performance as closely as financials; explicit standards (conformance to quality, service, cleanliness, value); senior owners such as a chief customer or experience officer. Set standards appropriately high: 98% accuracy sounds good but means thousands of failures at scale. Audit your own and competitors' service regularly (comparison and mystery shopping, surveys, complaint forms, audit teams) and use **importance-performance analysis** to rate each service element on customer importance and company performance and decide where to act.
- **Catering to high-value customers**: profit tiers get different treatment (perks, VIP service for the most loyal; leaner service for low-profit tiers). Two cautions: customers who get lesser treatment will bad-mouth a firm that claims superior service; and perks can be so generous they destroy profit (Kotler cites casinos that gave a skilled high roller terms that let him beat the house).
- **Managing complaints**: many customers leave after a bad experience without saying why; a complaint is a gift if handled well. Encourage complaints, empower staff to fix problems on the spot, and value frontline 'extra-role' behavior. Customers judge complaint handling on three things: the **outcome**, the **procedure** that produced it, and the **interpersonal treatment** along the way.

### Call centers (from the chapter's insight box)

- Running call centers purely as cost centers (rewarding short calls) raises churn; reward first-call resolution instead.
- Staffing matters: cutting a few reps can turn zero long holds into many.
- Route complex calls to the best-trained reps; match customers to suitable agents with data.
- Hire for traits that predict retention and service skill, not just prior experience; give reps flexibility over shifts.

## Differentiating through service

When the physical product is hard to differentiate, add valued services and keep improving them. Some firms turn the product into a service contract (pay per hour of engine use, with the seller carrying downtime risk). Main service differentiators:

- **Ease of ordering**: streamline evaluation through purchase; electronic ordering for B2B; replenishment and subscription mechanics (one-text or one-button reorder).
- **Speed and timing of delivery**: speed, accuracy and care; quick-response systems linking suppliers, plants and outlets; guaranteed delivery windows.
- **Installation**: making the product operational where it will be used; a real selling point for novices and complex equipment.
- **Customer training**: teaching the customer's people to use the product properly.
- **Customer consulting**: data, information systems and advice; often an essential and profitable line.
- **Maintenance and repair**: monitoring programs, e-support databases, products that send self-diagnostics; a smooth repair process matters even (especially) for luxury brands.
- **Returns**: costly and inconvenient for everyone, but a lenient policy can raise later purchases and referrals and bring customers into stores. Split them:
  - **Controllable returns** (seller or customer errors, packaging, logistics): eliminate root causes.
  - **Uncontrollable returns** (customers must try the product to judge fit): build efficient handling and resale processes. Knowledgeable advice before purchase reduces both.

## Innovation with services

- New service categories keep appearing around a simple, focused promise (a salon that only does blow-dries; walk-in retail clinics for simple cases with transparent prices; fractional or prepaid access to private jets).
- Late entrants can win with a different revenue model (a travel search engine paid per click rather than per booking).
- Innovation inside existing services pays: letting buyers pick their own seats, adding social sharing or reviews raised conversion substantially in Kotler's ticketing example.
- Regular innovators can reinvent a category by removing costly conventions and adding new value (Kotler uses a circus that dropped animals and added theatrical themes).

## Managing service quality

### Managing expectations: the five-gap model

Customers form expectations from past experience, word of mouth, personal needs and the firm's communications, then compare perceived with expected service.

| Gap | Between | Example cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Customer expectations and management's perception of them | Managers assume patients want better food; they want responsive nurses | Research customers directly; listen to front-line staff |
| 2 | Management perception and service-quality specifications | 'Be fast' with no number | Turn needs into measurable standards |
| 3 | Specifications and actual delivery | Poor training, inability or unwillingness, conflicting standards (listen vs be quick) | Train, staff, resolve conflicting goals, give tools |
| 4 | Delivery and external communications | Brochure shows a room nicer than the real one | Promise only what operations deliver; brief staff on campaigns |
| 5 | Perceived and expected service | Frequent doctor visits read as 'something is wrong' | Explain what the customer is seeing; manage cues |

- Successful firms add benefits that surprise and delight, exceeding expectations.
- For continuous services (subscriptions, memberships, utilities, insurance) customers keep asking 'am I using this enough for what I pay?'. Activity that raises current or expected usage protects the relationship; low usage predicts cancellation.
- Long relationships have a dark side: clients may come to feel the provider has lost objectivity, gone stale, or is taking them for granted.

### The five determinants of service quality (SERVQUAL)

In descending order of importance:

1. **Reliability**: performing the promised service dependably and accurately; right first time, on time, error-free records, staff who can answer questions.
2. **Responsiveness**: willingness to help and prompt service; telling customers when things will happen.
3. **Assurance**: knowledge and courtesy of staff and their ability to convey trust and confidence.
4. **Empathy**: caring, individualized attention; the customer's best interests at heart; convenient hours.
5. **Tangibles**: facilities, equipment, staff appearance, communication materials.

These underpin the 21-item SERVQUAL scale. The **zone of tolerance** runs from the minimum service customers will accept to the level they believe can and should be delivered; performance inside it is satisfactory.

Dynamic view: perceptions at any time depend on prior expectations of what *will* happen and what *should* happen, plus the last encounter. Raising expectations of what the firm *will* deliver can improve perceived quality; lowering expectations of what it *should* deliver can also improve it.

### Self-service technologies

- Customers value convenience; self-service (kiosks, apps, tabletop ordering, online booking, self-install) cuts cost and can raise spend.
- Reengineer the front office: decide what people do best, what machines do best, and how they work together.
- The hardest part is first use. Customers adopt when they understand their role, see a clear benefit, and feel able to use it.
- Self-service is not for everyone; trapping people in automated menus with no human route provokes anger.

## Product-service bundles

- Equipment makers compete on product-support services (ordering, delivery, installation, training, consulting, maintenance, repair), and online service now counts too. Good product plus poor local service is a serious disadvantage globally.
- Customer-service departments range from passive call-forwarding to teams that welcome requests and complaints and even contact customers proactively after the sale.
- Typical evolution: manufacturer runs its own parts and service (stays close to problems, earns premium margins, sometimes pricing equipment low and parts high) → authorized dealers take over (closer, faster) → independents undercut on price or speed.
- Long warranties, near-disposable equipment and in-house service teams squeeze service-contract revenue, so manufacturers must make money on the equipment itself.

## Worked example: an IT managed-services firm

A firm selling managed IT support to 20-200 person companies.

- **Intangibility**: publish a live status page, monthly health reports with screenshots and named technicians; branded onboarding kit.
- **Inseparability**: move routine training to monthly group webinars; keep named senior engineers for top-tier clients.
- **Variability**: blueprint the ticket journey (customer submits, line of interaction at triage, backstage diagnosis, line of internal interaction with vendors); write standards ('P1 acknowledged in 15 minutes, engineer on call in 30').
- **Perishability**: off-peak discounts for scheduled upgrades; part-time contractors for month-end peaks; self-service password resets.
- **Gap check**: Gap 4 found: the sales deck promised '24/7 on-site', operations deliver 24/7 remote and next-day on-site. Fix the deck, not just the operations.
- **Guarantee**: service credits when the P1 response standard is missed; claim by replying to the incident email.

## Workflow

1. Classify the offering on the good-service continuum and mark which benefits are search, experience or credence.
2. For each of the four characteristics, list the specific problem it creates for this business and pick a strategy (evidence to manage, ways to scale providers, standards and training, demand and supply levers).
3. Draw a service blueprint of the main journey; mark pain points, failure points and the evidence the customer sees at each step.
4. Run the five-gap check: research expectations, write measurable standards, verify delivery, audit every external promise against operations, and explain cues customers might misread.
5. Score the service on the five SERVQUAL dimensions against importance and competitors; prioritize reliability first.
6. Choose service differentiators (ordering, delivery, installation, training, consulting, maintenance, repair, returns) that matter to the target and that competitors do poorly.
7. Design internal marketing: hiring profile, training, empowerment rules, rewards tied to resolution and satisfaction, regular employee-satisfaction audits.
8. Design complaint handling and recovery: easy channels, on-the-spot authority, fair outcome, transparent procedure, respectful treatment; feed root causes back into the blueprint.
9. Decide what moves to self-service; define the customer's role, show the benefit, support first use, and keep a human route.
10. Plan tiered treatment for high-value customers without overpromising to others or giving away the margin.

## Red flags

- Marketing copy promises more than operations deliver (Gap 4), for example '24/7', 'instant', 'dedicated'.
- 'Fast', 'friendly', 'premium' service with no measurable standard (Gap 2).
- No visible evidence of quality for a credence-heavy service: no credentials, case results, facilities or named people.
- Call center or support team rewarded on handle time rather than resolution.
- Chatbot or phone tree with no way to reach a human.
- Complaints handled with scripts, selective replies or 'bribes' instead of fixing the cause.
- Peak demand met only by queues; off-peak capacity wasted with no pricing or promotion lever.
- Dynamic pricing that customers cannot understand or that feels exploitative at moments of need.
- A 'superior service' claim while low-tier customers get noticeably worse treatment.
- High churn in a subscription with no tracking of usage relative to price.
- Staff morale ignored while customer satisfaction is targeted.
- A strict returns policy with no split between controllable and uncontrollable returns.
