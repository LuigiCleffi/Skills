# Liking

People prefer to say yes to those they know and like, and a stranger can borrow that preference through attractiveness, similarity, compliments, familiarity, cooperation and association with good things. Many of these work without awareness. Build real liking (genuine shared ground, honest appreciation, true partnership, relevant associations) and use friends' recommendations only when the friend genuinely chose to recommend; never manufacture rapport or turn friendships into sales pressure.

## Friendship as a sales channel

- **The home-party model**: the request comes not from the salesperson but from a friend hosting the event who profits from each sale. The social bond can matter more than product preference. Guests often feel obliged to attend and to buy.
- Reciprocity (party games and prizes), commitment (guests describe products they already own) and social proof (each purchase signals others like it) are also present, but liking for the host is the main engine.
- **Referral naming**: just mentioning a friend's name ('your friend suggested I call') makes rejection feel like rejecting the friend. Sales trainers call this the 'endless chain': each satisfied customer supplies names of friends.
- **Friends-and-family pricing** that only pays off if the friend switches turns a relationship into leverage.
- **The salesperson formula**: a fair price plus someone the customer likes. The rest of the chapter explains what makes someone likable.

## Factors that cause liking

### Physical attractiveness

- **Halo effect**: one positive trait colors overall judgment. Attractive people are automatically assumed more talented, kind, honest and intelligent.
- Effects appear in elections, hiring, sentencing, damages awards, helping and persuasion, and people deny being influenced.
- Grooming and presentation matter in sales and brand imagery. Honest use: present people well; do not pretend attractiveness is evidence of product quality.

### Similarity

- We like people similar to us in opinions, personality, background, lifestyle and dress. People granted favors more readily to similarly dressed requesters and signed similarly dressed petitioners' petitions without reading them.
- Salespeople are trained to find (or claim) shared hobbies, hometowns or experiences, and to 'mirror and match' posture and language. Insurance buyers bought more from agents like themselves in age, religion, politics and habits.
- Small, even trivial, similarities work. Because a veneer of similarity is easy to fake, it is easy to abuse.

### Compliments

- We tend to believe praise and like those who give it, even when it is plainly self-serving. In one study, pure praise increased liking for the flatterer even when it was inaccurate.
- A car salesman famous for mailing every past customer a monthly card saying only that he liked them illustrates how far simple positive regard goes.
- Limits: transparent manipulation can backfire, and false praise is deception.

### Contact and familiarity

- We like what is familiar. People prefer their mirror image; voters favor familiar names; repeated exposure, even unnoticed, increases liking and persuasion.
- **But familiarity under unpleasant conditions (frustration, conflict, competition) decreases liking.** Cialdini's analysis of school desegregation shows contact alone, inside a competitive classroom, raised hostility.

### Cooperation

- Rival groups at a summer camp grew hostile through competition and were not reconciled by pleasant joint activities; they were reconciled by shared problems that required working together. Cooperative 'jigsaw' classrooms, where every student holds one piece needed by all, built friendships across groups.
- Compliance professionals point to, amplify, or manufacture cooperation: the car salesperson who 'fights the manager' for your deal, the good cop who is 'on your side'. The good cop/bad cop routine also uses contrast and reciprocity.
- Honest use: when you genuinely share a goal with the customer (their success is your success), make that partnership concrete.

### Conditioning and association

- **Negative association**: people dislike bearers of bad news even when blameless (the weather presenter blamed for storms; ancient messengers punished for defeats). People instinctively connect themselves to good news and distance themselves from bad.
- **Positive association**: advertisers pair products with attractive models, celebrities, cultural trends and big events ('official sponsor'). Viewers rate a product better with an attractive model present, and deny the influence. The link does not need to be logical.
- **The luncheon technique**: people grew fonder of statements and people they encountered while eating. Food and pleasant settings transfer good feeling to whatever is paired with them; this is why requests at fundraising dinners come during or after the meal.
- **Basking in reflected glory**: fans say 'we won' and 'they lost', wear team colors after wins and hide after losses. The urge is strongest when self-esteem has just been damaged. Brands can let customers share in real success (community wins, collective milestones).

## Honest use in marketing

| Factor | Honest application | Manipulative counterfeit (never) |
|---|---|---|
| Friends and referrals | Double-sided referral rewards, opt-in sharing, friend chooses whom to invite | Scraping contacts, implying a friend recommended when they did not, guilt-based friend pricing |
| Similarity | Spokespeople, case studies and reps who genuinely share the customer's world; segment-specific creative | Invented shared hometowns or hobbies; scripted mirroring to fake rapport |
| Compliments | Specific, true appreciation of the customer's work or choices | Generic flattery as a closing technique |
| Familiarity | Consistent, useful, non-intrusive presence; recognizable brand assets | Frequency so high it becomes annoying (familiarity under irritation breeds dislike) |
| Cooperation | Shared goals made concrete (customer success plans, co-created roadmaps, community challenges) | Fake 'I am on your side against my manager' theatrics |
| Association | Endorsers and sponsorships relevant to the product, with disclosed relationships | Irrelevant celebrity halo used to imply quality; undisclosed paid endorsements |
| Bad news | Deliver it with candor and a fix; own it rather than hide it; do not route it through the brand's most-liked faces only | Burying bad news or blaming customers |

## Worked example: consumer coffee brand

A direct-to-consumer coffee subscription wants growth from referrals and creators.

- **Referral program**: subscribers get a share link; the friend gets a free bag, the referrer gets one when the friend's second order ships. No contact-list import, no auto-messages sent in the subscriber's name.
- **Similarity**: creative features real home baristas segmented by context (new parents making 6 a.m. coffee, remote workers, campers), each speaking in their own words.
- **Association**: partner with a regional climbing gym the brand actually supplies, rather than paying an unrelated celebrity; disclose the partnership.
- **Cooperation**: a 'roast vote' where subscribers choose next season's origin; the brand reports results honestly.
- **Bad news**: a price increase is announced by the founder with the actual cost reasons and a way to lock the old price for three months.

## Defending against exploitative liking

- There are too many routes to liking (many unconscious) to block each one. Watch for the effect instead: **ask whether you like this requester more, or faster, than you would expect under the circumstances**.
- If yes, do not turn hostile (some people are simply likable). **Separate the requester from the request**: you will live with the product or contract, not the salesperson. Judge the deal on its merits.
- For marketers: if a campaign only works because the audience likes the messenger, check that the offer itself is good.

## Workflow

1. Identify who the audience trusts and resembles: peers, community members, specific professions, local figures.
2. Choose messengers (reps, creators, ambassadors, customers) with genuine similarity and relevance; disclose all paid relationships.
3. Build referral mechanics that keep the referrer in control and reward both sides.
4. Script rapport around real common ground discovered by listening, not invented.
5. Frame the relationship as cooperation toward the customer's goal and back it with concrete commitments.
6. Plan exposure frequency for familiarity without irritation; cap frequency and refresh creative.
7. Pair the brand with associations that are relevant and true (sponsorships, causes, settings).
8. Plan how bad news is delivered: candid, early, with a remedy.
9. Review for fake rapport, undisclosed endorsements and friendship pressure.

## Red flags

- Referral flows that message a user's contacts without explicit, per-message consent.
- 'Your friend X recommended us' when X only clicked a share prompt or did not recommend at all.
- Sales scripts that instruct reps to claim shared hobbies or hometowns.
- Celebrity or influencer deals with no product relevance, or without disclosure.
- Ad frequency so high that users complain or block.
- Fake 'I am fighting my manager for you' negotiation theater.
- A campaign that wins on likability while the offer is mediocre.
- Bad news hidden, delayed or blamed on customers.
