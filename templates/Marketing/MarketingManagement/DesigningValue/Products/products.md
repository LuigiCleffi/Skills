# Designing and Managing Products

A great brand sits on a great product: customers judge an offering on product, service and brand together, and the product is usually where the core benefit lives. Differentiate the product on the attributes target customers value, design it as a whole experience rather than a list of specs, manage it as part of a line and portfolio where every item has a job, and use packaging, labels and guarantees to sell it and reduce the buyer's risk.

## Product differentiation

Every product can be differentiated, even near-commodities (branded chicken, aspirin, steel). Products range from low-variation goods to highly differentiable ones (cars, buildings, furniture). The attributes to work with:

| Attribute | What it means | Decision rule |
|---|---|---|
| Core functionality | Delivers the core benefit | Non-negotiable; a product that fails its core value proposition fails, however good the rest (Kotler cites a phone leader that kept optimizing old standards while the category redefined itself). |
| Features | Additions that supplement the basic function | Survey recent buyers; compute customer value vs company cost per feature; ask how many want it, how long to ship, how easily copied. Prioritize, and teach customers how to use and benefit from each one. Think in bundles (trim levels, plans). |
| Performance quality | Level at which primary characteristics operate | Set the level appropriate to target and competition, not the maximum possible. Manage it over time: continuous improvement pays, stagnation is punished. |
| Conformance quality | Degree to which all units are identical and meet the promised spec | Buyers assume it; low conformance disappoints some buyers. Test finished units. |
| Durability | Expected operating life under normal or stressful conditions | Premium for durability must not be excessive, and must not be wasted on products that obsolesce quickly. |
| Reliability | Probability of no malfunction in a period | Buyers usually pay a premium for it. |
| Form | Size, shape, physical structure | Even a commodity can vary dosage, size, shape, color, coating, action time. |
| Style | Look and feel to the buyer | Creates distinctiveness that is hard to copy; does not guarantee performance. |
| Customization | Delivering what an individual wants and does not want | Mass customization (configure, personalize, print, build-to-order) is a strategic differentiator where demand exists. |

- Feature fatigue: more features raise perceived capability at purchase but lower satisfaction in use. Fewer, well-explained features often win.
- Platform plus modules: offer a basic platform and add-on modules to meet different requirements and lower cost.

## Product design

Design is the totality of features that affect how consumers perceive a product's look, feel and function. It offers functional and aesthetic benefits and appeals to both reason and emotion.

- In crowded markets, aesthetics may be the main way to stand out; design matters most for long-lived durables.
- Design can change perception of the experience itself (an interior shaped to feel roomier) without the customer noticing why.
- Design is a way of thinking that runs through the whole marketing program, not a phase. For the company, a well-designed product is easy to make and distribute; for the customer, it is pleasant to look at and easy to open, install, use, repair and dispose of.
- Approaches vary:
  - **Design thinking**: data-driven, three phases: observation (ethnography), ideation (creative brainstorming), implementation (collaborative teams bringing the idea to reality).
  - **Designer-led**: a few trusted designers, little consumer consultation, few long-lived products.
  - **Universal design and incremental improvement**: small frustration-relieving changes that make everyday objects pleasant for users of all ages.
- Design-award criteria are a usable checklist: benefit to the user, benefit to the business, benefit to society, ecological responsibility, appropriate aesthetics, usability testing.
- Organizationally, strong design companies give design a cross-divisional center with a line to the CEO and explicit design principles (for example: simple and intuitive, efficient and long lasting, adaptive and engaging).

## Product portfolios

A product portfolio (product mix) is everything a company offers, across categories and lines. Four dimensions:

- **Width**: number of product lines.
- **Length**: total number of items; average line length = total items / lines.
- **Depth**: number of variants of each product (scents x forms x additives).
- **Consistency**: how closely lines relate in end use, production, channels or other ways.

These give four growth moves: add lines (widen), lengthen lines, add variants (deepen), or increase consistency. They also frame which lines to build, maintain, harvest or divest.

Multibrand companies often concentrate resources on core brands. Every product in a line, and every brand in a portfolio, must have a defined role. Upgrading lower-tier brands with parts from higher tiers saves cost but risks diluting the upper tiers' cachet.

## Product line analysis

A product line is a group of related products sold by the same company.

- Know each item's sales and profits, to decide which to build, maintain, harvest or divest; understand each line's market profile and image.
- Items serve different layers of need with different margins (traffic builders at near-zero margin, higher-margin specialties). Maximize the profitability of the whole line, not each item.
- **Product map**: plot your items and competitors' items on the two attributes buyers use (for example weight x finish quality). It shows which of your items face direct competition, which have none, where gaps exist (an empty cell with real demand and a feasible cost is a candidate item), and which segments (overlaid on the map) your line serves well or poorly.
- **Modernization**: overhaul piecemeal (lets you see how customers and dealers react, easier on cash, but competitors see the change coming) or all at once. In fast markets modernization is continuous; time improvements to avoid hurting current sales (too early) or giving competitors time (too late), and use them to migrate customers to higher-value items.

## Product line length

Length follows objectives:

- **Up-selling**: an entry item that brings customers into the brand young, hoping they trade up.
- **Cross-selling**: related products sold together.
- **Hedging** against economic cycles: offerings in discount, middle and premium tiers.
- Market share and growth goals favor longer lines; profitability goals favor shorter, carefully chosen lines.

Lines tend to lengthen (excess capacity, sales force and distributor pressure for a full line) while costs rise (design, engineering, inventory, changeovers, order processing, transport, launch promotion). The usual pattern is growth followed by massive pruning. Review the line periodically with sales and cost analysis to cut deadwood that depresses profits.

### Line stretching (beyond the current range)

- **Down-market stretch**: reasons are growth in the lower tier, a stagnating middle, or tying up low-end competitors before they move up (a common counterattack). Branding options: parent brand on everything; a sub-brand ('Basic' versions); a different name (protects parent equity but equity must be built from scratch, at cost). Risks: dilution of the core brand's image and cannibalization. It works better when customers can compartmentalize the offerings and see real functional differences.
- **Up-market stretch**: reasons are growth, higher margins, or being a full-line maker. Consumers may not give a brand 'permission' to stretch upward, which is why some firms create a new name for the premium line; others use the core brand with a sub-brand or modifiers ('Pro', 'Ultra', 'Extra Strength'). Risk: lacking the resources, know-how and people to build a superior product for demanding buyers.
- **Two-way stretch**: a middle-market company extends in both directions to blanket the market and sandwich competitors; carries both sets of risks but works when each tier is differentiated by benefit, ingredients and price.

### Line filling (more items within the current range)

Motives: incremental profit, dealer complaints about gaps, consumer desire for variety, becoming the full-line leader, plugging holes against competitors. Line filling can build a brand-migration path inside the line. It is overdone when it causes cannibalization, confuses customers (items too similar; buyers default to the cheapest), or serves an internal need (a slot in the org chart) rather than a real market need.

## When less is more

Managers assume variety helps because larger assortments:

- Match more preferences to options.
- Keep options open and avoid feelings of restricted choice.
- Let buyers feel they explored the whole category.

But large assortments can hurt through:

- **Information overload**: more to process.
- **Choice overload**: several satisfactory options force painful trade-offs.
- **High expectations**: buyers expect the 'perfect' option, and walk away when it does not appear.

Rule: experts (who know attributes and already have preferences) benefit from big assortments; novices are helped by fewer, clearer options. Fewer options can raise conversion, lower returns and increase satisfaction. Design lines around two factors: consumer goals and consumer expertise.

## Packaging

Packaging is all activities of designing and producing the container. Up to three layers: primary (the bottle), secondary (the box), shipping (the case).

- The package is often the buyer's first encounter with the product: a 'five-second commercial'. Distinctive packages become part of brand equity.
- Why packaging grows in importance: self-service (shoppers pass hundreds of products a minute and decide in-store), consumer affluence (willingness to pay for convenience, appearance, dependability, prestige), company and brand image (instant recognition, a shelf 'billboard effect'), and innovation opportunity (convenience formats that earn a premium).
- Objectives: identify the brand; convey descriptive and persuasive information; facilitate transport, protection and storage; aid consumption. Align functional (structural) and aesthetic elements (size, shape, material, color, text, graphics), and harmonize them with price, advertising and the rest of the program.
- Color defines brands and carries meaning that varies by culture and segment. Common Western readings: red energy and boldness, orange friendliness, yellow warmth and joy, green health and freshness, blue trust and competence, purple nobility and wisdom, pink softness, brown honesty, black classic strength, white purity. Validate locally.
- Redesigns keep a brand current but can destroy recognition: Kotler uses a juice brand whose 'modernized' pack dropped its iconic cues, was mistaken for a store brand, lost sales and was reverted within months. Preserve the key brand elements.
- **Test** every package: engineering (survives normal conditions), visual (legible, harmonious colors), dealer (attractive, easy to handle), consumer (buyers respond favorably).
- **Environment**: reduce and green packaging, but check it still meets customer expectations; a compostable bag that customers found unbearably noisy was withdrawn. Cultural expectations (wrapping as luxury) also constrain.

## Labeling

- Functions: **identify** the product or brand; **grade** it; **describe** it (who made it, where, when, contents, how to use safely); **promote** it through graphics.
- Labels need periodic freshening; iconic labels should evolve gradually.
- Labels and packages are regulated: deceptive labels are unfair competition, there are mandatory labeling requirements, nutrition facts, and restrictions on terms such as 'light', 'natural', 'low-fat', 'high-fiber'. Definitions vary by country, so check each market's rules before reusing claims.

## Guarantees and warranties

Guarantees and warranties are explicit or implicit promises that the product will perform as specified, or the seller will fix it or refund money within a period. Expressed or implied, they are legally enforceable.

- **Guarantee**: compensation if the product fails to work as promised or expected. Benefits to the customer: credibility, reduced functional and monetary risk, peace of mind. Benefits to the company: sharper focus on the customer experience, accountability, faster development of performance standards, guidelines for recovering from failures.
  - General ('satisfied or your money back, for any reason') vs specific attribute guarantee (performance, reliability, durability).
  - Fixed period vs variable (life of the product or of the customer).
- **Warranty** differs on two dimensions: it usually covers repair or replacement, not refund; and extended warranties cost extra (bought at purchase or later), while guarantees are free.
- Extended warranties and service contracts can be very profitable for sellers even though they rarely pay off for buyers; sell them honestly.
- Guarantees reduce perceived risk and signal quality and company stability. They help most when the company or product is not well known, or when quality is genuinely superior (a long powertrain warranty reassured buyers about an unproven carmaker).
- Three traits of an effective guarantee or warranty: **relevant** (covers a feature customers care about and that plausibly fails), **easily understood** (promise and claim steps stated plainly), **easy to invoke** (few exclusions and limitations).

## Worked example: a B2B SaaS line review

A project-management SaaS has Free, Starter, Team, Business and Enterprise plans plus 14 add-ons.

| Item | Role | Margin | Problem found | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Free | Entry, up-selling | Negative | Converts well | Maintain |
| Starter | Line filling | Low | 80% of buyers pick it over Team; Team differs by two features nobody uses | Merge into Team (choice overload, cannibalization) |
| Team | Core | High | Feature fatigue: 40 toggles in settings | Hide advanced features; add onboarding that teaches the top 3 |
| Business | Up-sell | High | Healthy | Maintain |
| Enterprise | Up-market stretch | Highest | Lacks SSO audit and SLA that big buyers expect | Fix parity gaps before promoting |
| 14 add-ons | Depth | Mixed | 9 add-ons under 1% attach | Prune to 5 bundles |

Guarantee: a specific attribute guarantee on uptime (relevant: buyers fear outages), one-sentence claim process (easy to invoke), service credits without fine print (easily understood).

## Workflow

1. Confirm the target segment and positioning first; product decisions serve them.
2. Check core functionality: does the product reliably deliver its core benefit? If not, stop and fix that before anything else.
3. Score the product and key competitors on the nine differentiation attributes; pick one or two where you can be clearly better and customers care.
4. Run new features through the feature decision sheet; cut or hide low-value ones and plan how customers will learn the ones you keep.
5. Treat design as cross-functional: observe users, ideate, prototype, test usability; check ease of opening, setup, use, repair and disposal.
6. Map the line: list items with sales, margin and role; build a product map against competitors on the two attributes buyers use; mark gaps, overlaps and segments.
7. Decide length: stretch down, up or both only with a clear motive, a branding choice (parent, sub-brand, new name) and a check for cannibalization and dilution; fill only for a real market need.
8. Size assortments to buyer expertise: fewer, clearer choices for novices; filters and depth for experts.
9. Brief packaging and labels against the four objectives, test them four ways, check claims against each market's regulation, and protect iconic cues in redesigns.
10. Design the guarantee or warranty to be relevant, easily understood and easy to invoke; make sure operations can honor it.

## Red flags

- The roadmap adds features because a competitor has them, with no customer value vs cost estimate.
- Settings screens or spec sheets keep growing while satisfaction and activation fall (feature fatigue).
- 'Premium quality' everywhere, without a target-appropriate performance level.
- Tiers or SKUs that differ trivially; most buyers pick the cheapest; nobody can say each item's role.
- A down-market version under the parent brand with no plan to protect the flagship.
- A premium line under a brand customers see as mass-market, and no new name or proof.
- A product added to fill an internal slot, not a market need.
- A pricing page or catalog with so many options that novices bounce.
- A pack redesign that removes the logo, colors or shapes customers recognize; no consumer or dealer test.
- Pack claims ('natural', 'light', 'clinically proven') not checked against local rules or evidence.
- A guarantee full of exclusions, hard to claim, or covering something customers do not worry about.
- Selling extended warranties with fear-based copy that misstates the risk.
