# Proximate Objectives

One of a leader's most powerful tools is a **proximate objective**: a target close enough at hand to be feasible, which the organization can reasonably be expected to hit, even overwhelm. Good proximate objectives resolve ambiguity, focus energy and create a strong position from which the next objectives become possible.

## Audacious can still be proximate

- Rumelt argues that the famous moon-landing commitment was not a leap into the unknown but a carefully chosen proximate objective:
  - It followed a **diagnosis** (the contest was for world opinion; the rival was leveraging space spectaculars with fewer resources).
  - It was **feasible**: engineers knew how to build rockets, much technology existed, and the steps were laid out.
  - It **played to relative advantage**: the rival led in near-term feats that needed existing heavy rockets, but a landing needed rockets far larger than either side had, which favoured the side with a larger resource base.
- Contrast with objectives nobody knows how to achieve, pursued as if feasible ('eliminate drug use' under current frameworks). Huge effort aimed at an infeasible objective can even make the problem worse.
- Marketing test: 'become the category leader in three years' is not proximate unless you can lay out the steps and show why the race favours you.

## Resolving ambiguity

- Teams cannot work against open-ended ambiguity: any design can be attacked with someone's story of what might go wrong.
- Rumelt recalls an engineering programme stuck because nobody knew what a landing surface would be like; a planner wrote a working specification modelled on the smoother parts of earth. It was not the truth, but a strategically chosen assumption engineers could design to, with the insight that if reality were much worse, the whole programme would be in trouble anyway.
- **A key duty of any leader is to absorb much of the complexity and ambiguity and pass on a simpler, solvable problem.** Taking responsibility is more than accepting blame; it is setting proximate objectives.
- Marketing applications of a 'working specification':
  - An explicit ICP and buying committee, even if imperfect, so content, ads and sales plays can be built.
  - A message hypothesis to test rather than endless debate about positioning.
  - A defined launch scope ('the launch is a success if 200 qualified trials from existing customers in 6 weeks') instead of 'make a splash'.

## Taking a strong position and creating options

- **The more dynamic and uncertain the situation, the more proximate the objective must be.** Foresight gets worse as change accelerates, so looking further ahead is illogical.
- In uncertain settings the logic is **take a strong position and create options**, like chess masters who make moves that improve their position (more mobility for their pieces, less for the opponent's, stable patterns of strength) and later cash in accumulated advantage.
- Rumelt's example: a regional business school's plan was a list of initiatives (research visibility, alumni giving, global studies, sustainability). Asked for one feasible objective that would make the biggest difference, the faculty converged on 'get students into better jobs', made it task-like (ten target employers who should hire their graduates but do not, faculty teams to study and meet their recruiting needs), and replaced unrelated initiatives with a new programme that also served the target employers.
- Push objectives from goals toward **tasks**: concrete, assignable, checkable.

## Hierarchies of objectives

- High-level proximate objectives become goals for lower units, which set their own proximate objectives, in a **cascade of problem solving** at finer levels of detail.
- They cascade **in time** too: first steps succeed, then more nuanced objectives follow (for example, after an acquisition, first take the acquired brands into a few new countries, then refine).
- Any entry into a new market involves this cascade of adjusting and elaborating objectives.

## The ladder of competences

- What is proximate for one organization is out of reach for another because of differences in skills and accumulated resources.
- Rumelt's helicopter-pilot illustration: you cannot concentrate on a crisis, night flying, formation or landing on a pitching deck until basic control is automatic. **Concentrating on a priority assumes many other things are already handled routinely.**
- Skills at coordination are rungs on a ladder; higher rungs are reachable only after lower ones.
  - A startup still struggling to coordinate engineering, marketing and distribution should not make 'open European offices' its objective.
  - A newly international firm should master operating across languages and cultures before trying to arbitrage knowledge globally as veterans do.

## Template: proximate objective test

| Test | Question | Pass? |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic | Does it follow from the diagnosis and pivot point? | |
| Feasible | Do we know the steps? Do we have, or can we get, the skills and resources? | |
| Relative advantage | Does the race favour us rather than rivals? | |
| Ambiguity absorbed | Does it hand the team a defined, solvable problem? | |
| Task-like | Can it be assigned, scheduled and checked? | |
| Strong position | Does success create options for the next move? | |
| Ladder | Are the lower-rung competences already routine? | |

## Worked example: a B2B data-security startup

Draft OKR: 'Become the trusted leader in cloud data security; 3x pipeline; launch in Europe; grow brand awareness.'

Rewritten:

- Diagnosis: deals stall in security review because buyers cannot see proof that the product works in their stack.
- Proximate objective for the quarter: 'Publish verified integration guides and reference architectures for the three cloud data warehouses that appear in 70% of our pipeline, and get 10 named customers to act as references for each.'
- Cascade: product marketing owns the guides; solutions engineering builds the reference architectures; customer marketing recruits references; sales enablement updates the security-review playbook.
- Deferred: Europe (the ladder rung of a repeatable US sales motion is not yet reached); broad awareness campaigns.
- Option created: once references exist, an analyst-relations push and a partner listing on each warehouse's marketplace become feasible.

## Workflow

1. Start from the kernel's diagnosis and guiding policy; list current goals and initiatives.
2. Ask: if we could have only one feasible objective, which would make the biggest difference?
3. Check feasibility and relative advantage; drop or reshape objectives nobody knows how to achieve.
4. Where ambiguity blocks progress, write a working specification (assumed ICP, message hypothesis, scope) and state why it is a reasonable bet.
5. Make the objective task-like: owner, scope, date, checkable result.
6. Cascade it into team-level proximate objectives; sequence follow-on objectives in time.
7. Check the competence ladder; defer objectives that require rungs not yet reached.
8. In fast-changing markets, prefer objectives that build a strong position and create options over long-range targets.

## Red flags

- Objectives that restate the aspiration ('be the leader', 'world-class brand').
- Long-range targets in a market changing quarterly.
- Teams debating unresolved uncertainty with no working assumption to build against.
- OKRs that are metrics without a defined task or mechanism.
- A young company pursuing international expansion, a platform play and a new segment at once.
- A launch with no definition of success.
