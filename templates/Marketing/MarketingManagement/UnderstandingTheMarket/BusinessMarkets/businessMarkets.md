# Analyzing Business Markets

Business buying is done by groups of professionals solving an organizational problem and a personal one at the same time. Identify the buying situation, map every role in the buying center and what each one values, and make your value visible at each of the eight buying stages. Fight commoditization by moving from product to solution, adding services, building a credible corporate brand, and proving value in money terms (total cost of ownership, economic value) rather than conceding on price.

## What business markets are

**Organizational buying** is how formal organizations establish the need for purchased goods and services and identify, evaluate and choose among brands and suppliers. The **business market** is every organization that buys goods and services used to produce other products or services that are sold, rented or supplied to others. More money and more items change hands in business markets than in consumer markets, because every step in a supply chain buys from the step before and buys supporting goods and services.

**Commoditization** (buyers seeing all suppliers as offering identical benefits) is the main enemy: it erodes margins and loyalty. The defense is relevant differentiation that customers believe is worth the extra cost.

Big B2B marketing challenges (per the Institute for the Study of Business Markets): integrating sales and marketing, managing innovation, gathering and using customer insight, proving marketing's contribution, engaging customers and customers' customers, balancing central and local marketing, and building marketing talent.

### How business markets differ from consumer markets

| Characteristic | Implication for the marketer |
|---|---|
| Fewer, larger buyers | A handful of contracts can make or break the year; key-account focus |
| Close supplier-customer relationships | Expect customization, supplier scorecards, value-added and cost-saving demands; reciprocity (buying from your customers) is common |
| Professional purchasing | Trained buyers, formal policies, RFQs, proposals, contracts; supply technical data and evidence |
| Multiple buying influences | Committees with technical experts and executives; sell with trained reps and teams |
| Derived demand | Demand comes from end-consumer demand; monitor end users and the economy (production, investment, consumer spending, interest rates); you mostly fight for share, not total demand |
| Inelastic demand | Total demand barely moves with price, especially short term and for components that are a small share of total cost |
| Fluctuating demand | A small change in consumer demand causes a larger swing in demand for plant and equipment (accelerator effect); plan for volatility |
| Geographically concentrated buyers | Lower selling costs; watch regional shifts of industries |
| Direct purchasing | Complex or expensive items are bought from manufacturers, not intermediaries |

## Types of buying decisions

| Situation | What happens | In-supplier strategy | Out-supplier strategy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Straight rebuy** | Routine reorder from an approved list | Keep quality and service high; propose automatic reordering | Offer something new or exploit dissatisfaction; win a small order and grow share |
| **Modified rebuy** | Buyer wants to change specs, price, delivery or terms | Renegotiate proactively before a competitor does | Opportunity to break in |
| **New buy** | First purchase; risk drives more participants, more information and more time | Use the best salespeople; brand and reputation build trust; reach as many participants as possible | Same |

- The new buy is the biggest opportunity and challenge. It passes through awareness, interest, evaluation, trial and adoption: mass and digital media matter most for awareness, salespeople for interest, technical sources for evaluation.
- In a new buy the buyer decides specs, price limits, delivery terms, service terms, payment terms, quantities, acceptable suppliers and the chosen supplier, with different people influencing each decision.
- New buys become rebuys over time; once in, keep adding value to secure rebuys.

## The buying center

The buying center (decision-making unit) is everyone who participates in the purchase decision and shares its goals and risks. Typically five or six people, sometimes dozens, some outside the organization (consultants, advisors, officials, channel members).

| Role | Who and what they do |
|---|---|
| Initiator | Requests the purchase |
| User | Uses the product; often initiates and defines requirements |
| Influencer | Shapes specifications and supplies evaluation information (often technical staff) |
| Decider | Decides requirements or suppliers |
| Approver | Authorizes the deciders' or buyers' proposed actions |
| Buyer | Has formal authority to select suppliers and negotiate terms |
| Gatekeeper | Controls information and access to the others (purchasing, assistants, IT security) |

One person can hold several roles (a purchasing manager may be buyer, influencer and gatekeeper), and several people can hold one role. Purchasing agents dominate rebuys; other staff matter more in new buys; engineers often pick components and purchasing picks suppliers.

### Dynamics and motives

- Purchasing has moved from clerical to strategic: fewer, better suppliers and global strategic sourcing. Match the caliber of your sales team to theirs.
- Members have different criteria: engineers want performance, production wants ease of use and supply reliability, finance wants economics, purchasing wants operating and replacement cost, unions or safety officers want safety.
- Buyers have personal styles: keep-it-simple, own-expert, want-the-best, want-everything-done; analytical buyers and old-school hagglers.
- **Organizations do not buy; people do.** Each buyer solves the organization's economic and strategic problem and a personal one (achievement, reward, avoiding blame). B2B decisions are both rational and emotional. Address hidden anxieties (will it work, will it integrate, will I look bad) as well as specifications.

### Selling to the buying center

- Decide which companies to target, then whom within each buying center: who participates, which decisions they influence and how much, and what criteria they use.
- Small sellers focus on the key influencers; large sellers sell in depth to many participants. Use communication programs to reach hidden influencers.
- Revisit assumptions about who buys; a vendor that shifted from selling to the CIO to selling to business-unit leaders grew new-customer sales.
- Use ethnographic research; a supposed price-driven commodity market may turn out to want early collaboration.
- Look at your **customers' customers**: solving their problems can improve your product or business model.

## The eight-stage business buying process

Rebuys compress or skip stages (search and proposals in particular).

| Stage | What the buyer does | What the marketer should do |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Problem recognition | Internal triggers (new product, breakdown, poor supplier) or external ones (trade show, ad, email, content, sales call) | Trigger recognition with direct marketing, content and insight about costs of the status quo |
| 2. General need description | Defines characteristics and quantity, with engineers and users | Help define the need; show how you meet or exceed it |
| 3. Product specification | Technical specs; **product value analysis** looks for components that can be redesigned, standardized or made cheaper without hurting performance | Get involved early to shape specs; use value analysis yourself to position and cut excess cost |
| 4. Supplier search | Directories, referrals, trade media, shows, online search; **buying alliances** and group purchasing organizations; e-marketplaces (catalog sites, vertical hubs, auctions, spot exchanges, private exchanges) | Be findable (directories, marketplaces, search); be on approved lists; understand that online buying lowers costs but can erode loyalty |
| 5. Proposal solicitation | Invites written proposals, then presentations from a shortlist | Write proposals in customer terms (value and benefits); presentations that inspire confidence; team selling |
| 6. Supplier selection | Ranks attributes (price, reputation, reliability, agility, technical service, flexibility) in a supplier-evaluation model; incentive to buy = perceived benefits minus perceived costs; trend toward fewer or single sources (balanced against disruption risk) | Frame the offer so all benefits and savings are recognized; influence how the buyer thinks about the economics of buying, owning, using and disposing |
| 7. Contract negotiation | Final specs, quantity, delivery, returns, warranties; leasing; **blanket contracts** (stockless purchase plans) for MRO; long-term supply contracts; extranets; **vendor-managed inventory** | Offer leasing, blanket contracts and VMI to lock in the relationship and lower the buyer's costs |
| 8. Performance review | Asks end users, scores suppliers on weighted criteria, or computes cost of poor performance; continues, modifies or ends | Monitor the same criteria, report results proactively, fix issues before review |

## Developing effective business marketing programs

### From products to solutions

- **Systems buying**: buyers prefer a total, turnkey solution from one seller. **Systems selling** responds; tech vendors become one-stop shops.
- **Systems contracting**: one supplier provides all maintenance, repair and operating (MRO) supplies and manages the customer's inventory; the customer gets lower procurement cost and price protection, the seller steady demand and lower costs.
- For large projects, get in early to shape specifications, and take the broadest view of the customer's need. Kotler contrasts a bid to build a factory with a rival bid that also trained the workforce and helped sell the output; the broader bid won despite costing more.

### Enhancing services

Add high-quality services to products to raise value and tie customers closer: long-term maintenance contracts priced per usage hour, subscriptions that make upgrades automatic, support services. Customers pay a premium for predictability and peace of mind.

### Building B2B brands

- Brands reduce perceived risk and make purchases easier to justify internally (the old line that nobody gets fired for buying the market leader).
- The **corporate brand** often matters most because it covers many products. Unify brand architecture and identity across units and countries; show real projects and business-specific messages.
- A brand known only to technical users may need to reach executives who do not yet see the category as strategic.

### Overcoming price pressure

Do not default to discounting. Options:

- **Framing** the offer around benefits and savings.
- **Total cost of ownership**: show lower life-cycle cost than competitors.
- Cite the value of services the buyer already receives, plus know-how and faster time to market.
- Improve productivity to lower your own costs.
- Offer a lower price with restrictions (limited quantities, no refunds, no adjustments, no services) for pure price buyers.
- **Guaranteed cost reduction**: promise to find savings in the customer's operations at least equal to the price gap.
- **Gain-sharing collaboration**: guarantee savings in exchange for more share of wallet; make up shortfalls and share upside. Requires a baseline database, an agreed measurement method and a dispute mechanism.
- **Economic value analysis (EVA)**: monetize functional benefits (performance, reliability, warranty) so value becomes visible.

### Customer-perceived value

- **Total customer benefit** = economic, functional and psychological benefits from four sources: **product, services, people, image**.
- **Total customer cost** = monetary cost plus time, energy and psychological costs of acquiring, using, maintaining, owning and disposing.
- The buyer chooses the supplier with the highest perceived value (benefit minus cost) relative to alternatives.
- Three levers: raise total benefit, cut non-monetary costs, cut monetary cost.
- **Pricing between cost and value**: if the customer values the offer at 20,000 and it costs 14,000 to make, price must fall between them. At 19,000 the customer gets 1,000 of surplus and the seller keeps 5,000. Win only if the customer surplus beats the competitor's.

### Managing communication

- B2B buying increasingly starts with search: invest in SEO and search marketing, a useful website, webinars, podcasts, white papers, blogs, video and social, and repurpose content around buyer keywords.
- B2C-style brand campaigns can reposition a B2B firm (for example, showing that services, not hardware, are now the core business).
- For very large purchases, buyers want trusted, independent sources and personal contact.

## Managing business-to-business relationships

### Relationship types

Four forces shape the relationship: **availability of alternatives, importance of supply, complexity of supply, supply market dynamism**. Relationships range from basic buying and selling (routine exchange, moderate cooperation) to collaborative partnerships built on trust and commitment. The closest relationships arise when supply is important and procurement is hard (complex requirements, few alternatives). Not every customer needs or merits a close relationship; roles shift over time.

### Trust, credibility and reputation

- **Trust** is a firm's willingness to rely on a partner; it rests on perceived competence, integrity, honesty and benevolence. Firms look more trustworthy when they give full and honest information, align employee incentives with customer needs, co-create value, and offer valid comparisons with competitors.
- Online trading adds worries about quality, delivery and payment; use credit checks, verification, escrow and trust services.
- **Corporate credibility** has three parts:
  - **Expertise**: can they make and deliver it?
  - **Trustworthiness**: are they honest, dependable and sensitive to our needs?
  - **Likability**: are they attractive, prestigious, dynamic and pleasant to work with?

### Risks and opportunism

- Relationships balance **safeguarding** (predictability) against **adapting** (flexibility).
- **Specific investments** (customer-specific training, equipment, systems) raise efficiency but lock both sides in: the buyer faces switching costs and holdup; the supplier risks dedicated assets and intellectual property.
- **Opportunism** is cheating or undersupplying against an explicit or implicit contract, including passive refusal to adapt or negligence. It forces costly monitoring. Contracts fall short when opportunism is hard to detect, assets are specific and contingencies hard to foresee; then joint ventures make sense.
- A long future time horizon and strong solidarity norms shift specific investments from expropriation to bonding.

## Institutional and government markets

- **Institutional market**: schools, hospitals, care homes, prisons and others providing goods and services to people in their care. Low budgets and captive clienteles; the goal is neither profit nor pure cost minimization (poor quality hurts reputation). Suppliers often set up dedicated divisions with tailored products, packaging and pricing, and can co-develop products that cut cost per unit while keeping quality.
- **Government market**: usually open bidding with award to the lowest qualified bidder (with allowance for quality and on-time record), or negotiated contracts for complex, high-risk projects with little competition. Expect heavy paperwork, public scrutiny, delays and procurement staff turnover; different agencies have different needs and timelines. Win by justifying cost and bottom-line impact, showing past performance with other public bodies, following procurement guidelines exactly, and registering in the official supplier databases and online purchasing catalogs. Reforms favor off-the-shelf items, online communication and debriefing losing bidders.

## Worked example: a fleet-telematics SaaS selling to a regional logistics company

- **Situation**: new buy (the company tracks vans with spreadsheets and a basic GPS vendor), so expect a long cycle and many participants.
- **Buying center**: operations director (initiator and decider), dispatchers and drivers (users), IT security lead (influencer and gatekeeper), CFO (approver), procurement manager (buyer), plus the external insurance broker (influencer, because telematics can lower premiums).
- **Criteria**: operations wants on-time delivery and fewer wasted miles; drivers fear surveillance; IT wants integration and data security; CFO wants payback under 12 months; procurement wants price and contract flexibility; the operations director personally wants a rollout that does not fail in peak season.
- **Process plays**: stage 1, publish a fuel-waste benchmark report and run search ads on 'van fuel costs'; stage 3, offer to help write the specification (route optimization, open API, data residency); stage 5, a proposal in the customer's terms; stage 6, an economic value analysis showing fuel savings, overtime reduction and an insurance discount, with total cost of ownership over three years against the incumbent; stage 7, a pilot on 20 vans with a guaranteed savings clause; stage 8, a quarterly business review with the agreed KPIs.
- **Price pressure**: procurement asks for 25 percent off. Instead of discounting, offer a gain-sharing model (lower base fee plus a share of verified fuel savings), or a cheaper tier without route optimization and with self-serve support only.
- **Credibility**: case studies from two similar regional fleets (expertise), a security certification and transparent data policy (trustworthiness), a driver-friendly app and coaching program that addresses the surveillance worry (likability).

## Workflow

1. Classify the market (business, institutional, government) and note derived-demand drivers and demand volatility.
2. Identify the buying situation (straight rebuy, modified rebuy, new buy) and whether you are the in- or out-supplier.
3. Map the buying center with the template; list each member's criteria and personal stakes, including hidden and external influencers.
4. Locate the account in the eight-stage process and plan the marketing and sales action for the current and next stages.
5. Build the value case: total customer benefit (product, services, people, image) versus total customer cost; quantify with TCO or economic value analysis; set price between cost and customer value with a surplus that beats the alternative.
6. Decide whether to sell a product, a solution (systems selling or contracting) or a product plus service contract.
7. Plan communication: search and content for early stages, proposals and proof for evaluation, brand for risk reduction.
8. Choose the relationship depth the account merits; build trust and credibility; agree safeguards for specific investments.
9. For public-sector bids, follow the procurement rules exactly and lead with cost justification and past performance.

## Red flags

- B2B campaigns aimed at one contact with no map of the other buying-center roles.
- Messaging that lists features but never translates them into money, risk or time saved.
- Responding to every price objection with a discount instead of TCO, value analysis or a restricted offer.
- Engaging only after the specification is written by a competitor.
- Proposals written in the vendor's language rather than the customer's outcomes.
- No plan for gatekeepers, security reviews or procurement policies.
- Ignoring the personal risk the champion takes by recommending a new supplier.
- Treating every account as a strategic partnership, or no account as one.
- Promises in proposals that delivery cannot keep; opportunistic undersupply after the contract is signed.
- Forecasts that ignore derived demand and the economic cycle of the customer's end market.
- Government bids with incomplete paperwork or no cost justification.
