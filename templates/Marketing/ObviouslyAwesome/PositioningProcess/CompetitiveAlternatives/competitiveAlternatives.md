# Step 4. List Your True Competitive Alternatives

Positioning rests on how customers perceive the product compared with other ways of solving their problem, and only the customer's view counts. Ask what your best-fit customers would do if your product did not exist, list those alternatives (often not products at all), rank them by how common they are, and group them into two to five clusters. These clusters become the baseline every differentiator and value claim is measured against.

## Why alternatives, not features or problems

- Product people naturally start with the product and its features. But features and their value are only unique, interesting and valuable when perceived against alternatives.
- The foundation is the problem *and* how customers see your offering against other ways of solving it.
- Asking customers about their problem is not enough: they are often poor at describing it with useful nuance, and problem descriptions do not reveal the categories they put solutions in. Dunford's database users described a "fast query" need, yet when asked what they would use instead, they named analytics and data-warehouse tools, not other databases.
- Many weak positions stem from not knowing the true alternatives in customers' minds.

## The question

Dunford's core question: "What would our best customers do if we didn't exist?"

- Sometimes the answer is a direct competitor that looks like you.
- Often it is "use pen and paper", "use a spreadsheet", "hire an intern", "ask the agency to do it".
- For very novel products: "do nothing", because customers do not yet see they have a problem.

## Rules for the list

1. **Stay anchored on the best-customer list** from [Step 1](../BestCustomers/bestCustomers.md). Name only what those customers would see as alternatives; ignore edge cases who know an obscure vendor or have unusual reasons.
2. **Rank from most to least common.** Focus effort on the common alternatives and stop worrying about rare ones.
3. **Drop rivals without mindshare.** You know the landscape better than customers do. If a competitor has few customers, prospects would not realistically pick them if you vanished. Ask what the majority of best customers would really do.
4. **Group into clusters.** Alternatives usually cluster naturally ("do it manually" = intern + spreadsheet; "small-business accounting suite" = several named products). Expect two to five groups.

Sources: best-customer interviews ("what did you use before?", "what else did you consider?"), sales call notes, win/loss reasons, onboarding questionnaires, review-site "switched from" fields.

## Worked example

HVAC dispatch SaaS, best customers (15 to 60 techs, heavy emergency work):

| Rank | Group | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Whiteboard plus phone and text | Dispatcher, whiteboard, group SMS | 9 of 14 best customers; cheap, flexible; breaks at night and with many techs |
| 2 | General field-service suite | Large all-in-one FSM platforms | 4 of 14; strong for planned maintenance and invoicing; slow, many clicks for urgent calls |
| 3 | Answering service plus calendar | Outsourced after-hours line, shared calendar | 3 of 14; covers nights but calls are not routed to the nearest qualified tech |

Rival startup X, which the team tracks closely, appears in zero best-customer answers and is left off.

## Workflow

1. Take the best-customer list and gather their answers to "what would you do if we did not exist?" and "what did you use before?".
2. List every alternative named, including non-products and "do nothing".
3. Remove alternatives that only edge cases mention and vendors with negligible mindshare.
4. Rank by frequency among best customers.
5. Cluster into two to five groups and name each group in customer language.
6. For each group note what customers like and dislike about it; these notes seed [Step 5](../UniqueAttributes/uniqueAttributes.md).
7. Use the groups, not a startup leaderboard, as the comparison set in battlecards, comparison pages and claims.

## Red flags

- The competitor slide lists only funded startups or analyst-quadrant vendors.
- "Do nothing", spreadsheets or manual work are absent, while many deals are lost to "no decision".
- Claims like "easier", "faster" or "cheaper" with no named baseline.
- A list of fifteen alternatives with no ranking.
- Alternatives drawn from the whole customer base or from prospects who never bought.
- Problem statements gathered, but no question about what customers would use instead.
