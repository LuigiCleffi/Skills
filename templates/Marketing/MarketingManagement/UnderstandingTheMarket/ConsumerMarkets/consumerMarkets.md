# Analyzing Consumer Markets

Before choosing tactics, understand how the target consumer thinks, feels and acts across the whole journey, from noticing a need to using and disposing of the product. Map which cultural, social and personal factors shape the need, what psychological processes filter your messages, and what happens at each of the five buying stages, then design the offering and communication to win at the stage where you are losing. Treat perception as the reality you must manage, and use what you learn about decision shortcuts to help people choose well, never to trick them.

## The model of consumer behavior

Marketing tactics (the seven Ts) and the market context (economic, technological, legal, political, sociocultural, physical) pass through two filters:

1. **Consumer characteristics**: cultural, social, personal.
2. **Consumer psychology**: motivation, perception, emotions, memory.

These drive the **buying decision process** (problem recognition, information search, evaluation, purchase, postpurchase) and finally the **purchase decisions**: product, brand, store or channel, quantity, timing and payment method. Ask the who, what, why, where, when and how questions: who buys, decides and influences; what needs and wants are served; why this brand; where and when they buy (online, offline, seasonality); how they perceive and feel about the offering; what social, lifestyle, demographic and economic factors weigh in.

## Consumer characteristics

### Cultural factors (broadest and deepest influence)

- **Culture**: shared behaviors, beliefs, values and symbols passed on largely without thinking. Cultures differ, for example, in individualism versus collectivism. Individualist shoppers tend to buy for themselves, follow trends, shop across channels for the best deal and return what disappoints; collectivist shoppers tend to follow the crowd, value long-term reputation, buy for the family, stay with trusted retailers and prefer stores.
- **Subcultures**: nationality, religion, ethnicity, region. When large and affluent enough, they merit dedicated programs.
- **Social class**: relatively homogeneous, enduring, ordered divisions with shared values and brand preferences; purchases can signal class. Mobility between classes varies by country.

### Social factors

- **Reference groups**: any group that directly or indirectly affects beliefs and behavior. Membership groups (family, friends, coworkers, interest and religious groups), **aspirational groups** people hope to join, and **dissociative groups** they reject.
- **Opinion leaders (influencers)**: give informal advice on a category; often confident, socially active, heavy users. Identify their demographics, psychographics and media, and address them directly.
- **Roles and status**: people choose products that express their role and actual or desired status, so brands carry self-defining and status-symbol potential.
- **Family**: the most important buying unit. Family of orientation (parents, siblings) shapes values, ambition and self-worth; family of procreation (partner, children) drives everyday buying. Big-ticket purchases are mostly joint decisions; traditional gender purchasing roles are shifting; children exert direct influence (requests) and indirect influence (parents know their preferences).

### Personal factors

- **Age, life-cycle stage and household composition**, plus psychological life stages.
- **Life events and transitions** (marriage, birth, illness, relocation, divorce, first job, career change, retirement, bereavement) create new needs across many categories. A new parent suddenly needs not just baby goods but insurance, financial services, housing and a bigger car. Reach people early in a transition, ethically, especially when they are vulnerable.
- **Occupation**: identify occupational groups with above-average interest and tailor products to them.
- **Economic circumstances**: level, stability and pattern of income, savings and liquidity, debt and borrowing power, attitude to spending. In a downturn, redesign, reposition or reprice, or emphasize value lines.
- **Personality and self-concept**: consumers pick brands whose personality matches their actual self, ideal self, or how they think others see them; the effect is stronger for publicly consumed products. High self-monitors pick brands that fit the situation. Different situations evoke different selves.
- **Values and lifestyle**: core values (deeper than attitudes) guide long-term choice; lifestyle is the pattern of activities, interests and opinions. A key split is **money-constrained** (serve with lower-cost offers) versus **time-constrained** (serve with multitasking, time-saving offers). Time-pressed buyers may still want some involvement: a meal kit that needs one pan and 15 minutes can beat the microwave meal (the "convenience involvement" segment).

## Consumer psychology

### Needs, wants, demands and motivation

- **Needs** are basic requirements, biological (hunger, thirst) or psychological (recognition, esteem, belonging).
- **Maslow's hierarchy**: physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualization. People satisfy the most pressing level first.
- **Wants** are needs directed at specific objects, shaped by society. **Demands** are wants backed by ability and willingness to pay; measure both how many want it and how many can buy.
- Marketers do not create needs; they connect offerings to needs that already exist.
- Needs can be stated, real, unstated, delight or secret. Probe what "powerful" or "peaceful" really means; responding only to the stated need shortchanges the customer. Help customers learn what they want (subscription razors showing people they could pay less; meal kits overcoming lack of cooking confidence).
- A need becomes a **motivation** when aroused enough to drive action; motivation has **direction** (which goal) and **intensity** (how hard).
- **Motivation research** uses in-depth interviews and projective techniques (word association, sentence completion, picture interpretation, role play) to surface motives people do not state. Kotler cites a cake-mix brand whose sales rose once buyers were asked to add a fresh egg, giving them a sense of real participation. Deep-motive insights still need ongoing product renewal to last.

### Perception

Perception is how we select, organize and interpret inputs; in marketing, **perception matters more than reality** because it drives behavior.

- **Selective attention**: people screen out most messages. They are more likely to notice stimuli related to a current need, stimuli they expect, and stimuli with a large deviation from normal (100 off rather than 5 off). Unexpected offers get through. There is no evidence that subliminal messages can systematically control behavior.
- **Selective distortion**: people interpret information to fit their beliefs. Branded and blind taste tests of the identical product give different verdicts; visual cues can override taste, even for experts. Strong brands benefit because ambiguous information is read positively.
- **Selective retention**: people remember the good points of products they like and forget the good points of competitors.

### Emotions

- Responses are not all rational. Brands and ads can evoke pride, excitement, confidence, amusement, wonder or disgust. Emotional appeals work best when rooted in a real functional or rational benefit.
- Emotional brand stories are more likely to be shared. Nostalgia and retro marketing can create a strong connection.
- The consumer's emotional state changes how other tactics work; fear, for example, can strengthen or weaken appeals based on popularity or limited availability.

### Memory

- **Short-term** (temporary, limited) versus **long-term** (durable, potentially unlimited) memory. Long-term memory is **episodic** (personal events with their context and emotions), **semantic** (facts and concepts) or **procedural** (automatic skills).
- **Associative network model**: memory is nodes linked by associations of varying strength; activating one node spreads to strongly linked ones. A **brand** is a node with linked associations (thoughts, feelings, images, experiences, beliefs, attitudes, competitors, ambassadors, country of origin). Map them by strength, favorability and uniqueness.
- **Encoding**: the more attention to meaning and the more links to existing knowledge, the stronger the memory.
- **Retrieval**: stored information decays slowly, but may be **available yet not accessible** without retrieval cues (packaging and in-store displays that echo the advertising). Top-of-mind brands get talked about. **Interference** from crowded categories makes consumers confuse brands, so distinctive cues matter.

## The buying decision process

Five stages, often iterative and non-linear (the **consumer decision journey**); routine purchases skip stages. Use the full model for new or high-involvement purchases with functional, psychological or monetary risk, and plan touch points for every stage.

### 1. Problem recognition

Triggered by internal stimuli (a basic need reaching a threshold) or external ones (a friend's purchase, an ad). Common drivers: **natural depletion**, **dissatisfaction** with the current solution, **lifestyle or goal changes**, and **social influence**. Research which circumstances trigger the need and build campaigns around those moments, especially for discretionary purchases.

### 2. Information search

- Many consumers search little (one store, one or two brands). Two levels: **heightened attention** (more receptive) and **active search** (reading, asking, browsing, visiting stores).
- Sources: **personal** (family, friends), **commercial** (ads, websites, salespeople, packaging), **public** (media, social media, rating organizations) and **experiential** (handling, trying). Commercial sources deliver the most information; personal, experiential and independent public sources are the most persuasive. Commercial sources inform; personal sources legitimize and evaluate. Online reviewers now act as independent authorities.
- Internal information (own experience) versus external information (sought for high-involvement buys).
- **Successive sets**: total set → awareness set → consideration set → choice set → choice. Your job is to get into each one. Learn which other brands are in the choice set and which sources put you there.
- **Market partitioning**: identify the hierarchy of attributes buyers decide on first (brand-dominant, nation-dominant, type-dominant, price-dominant). The hierarchy reveals segments and tells you who you really compete with. How options are displayed (by brand or by flavor; ranked by predicted fit online; recommendation engines) shapes that hierarchy.

### 3. Evaluation of alternatives

- **Beliefs** (convictions, right or wrong) and **attitudes** (enduring evaluations, feelings and tendencies) are hard to change; generally fit the product to existing attitudes rather than fight them. Attitudes formed through trial predict purchase better than attitudes formed from ads.
- Consumers seek benefits and see products as **bundles of attributes**; they attend to the attributes that deliver the benefits they want. Segment by attribute and benefit importance.
- **Choice overload**: more options can hurt when no option is clearly superior. Reduce overload by adding a clearly superior option, cutting the information load, asking guiding questions, or easing time pressure.
- **Expectancy-value model** (compensatory): perceived value = sum of (importance weight x belief score) across attributes. Four ways to raise your score: (1) redesign the product (real repositioning), (2) change beliefs about your brand (psychological repositioning, better communication of real strengths), (3) change beliefs about competitors (competitive depositioning, truthfully), (4) shift the importance weights toward attributes where you excel.

### 4. Purchase decision

- Up to five sub-decisions: brand, channel, quantity, timing, payment method.
- **Non-compensatory rules and heuristics**: consumers often evaluate attributes in isolation or use shortcuts, especially under time pressure, low knowledge, many similar options or a need to justify the choice.
- **Choice architecture**: how choices are presented nudges behavior (portion-controlled packs that are profitable and steer healthier eating). Use nudges that serve the customer's own interest.
- **Elaboration likelihood model**: the **central route** (careful evaluation of key information) needs motivation, ability and opportunity; otherwise people take the **peripheral route**, relying on cues such as a credible source, a liked endorser, attractive packaging, familiarity from repetition or sponsorship, or a promotion.
- **Low-involvement products** (cheap, frequent, undifferentiated): repeat buying is often habit, not loyalty. Stress quality and brand familiarity, keep distribution tight so buyers never have to switch. To raise involvement modestly: link the product to an engaging issue, to a personal situation, to emotions tied to values or ego defense, or add an important feature.
- **Intervening factors** between intention and purchase: the **attitudes of others** (stronger when their attitude is intense and they are close to the buyer), including **infomediaries** (expert reviewers, rating services, customer reviews), and **unanticipated situational factors** (job loss, a more urgent purchase, a bad sales encounter). Intentions are not reliable predictors on their own.
- **Perceived risk** makes people modify, postpone or avoid purchase:

| Risk | Fear | Ways to reduce it |
|---|---|---|
| Functional | It will not perform | Demos, trials, specs, independent tests, guarantees |
| Physical | It may harm me or others | Safety certifications, clear usage guidance |
| Financial | Not worth the price | Price comparisons, money-back guarantee, financing |
| Social | Embarrassment in front of others | Genuine reviews and testimonials from people like the buyer |
| Psychological | It will affect my well-being or self-image | Reassurance, brand values, support |
| Opportunity | Failure costs time and money to replace | Easy returns, onboarding, migration help |

Risk rises with money at stake, attribute uncertainty and low self-confidence; consumers cope by avoiding decisions, asking friends, and preferring national brands and warranties.

### 5. Postpurchase behavior

- **Satisfaction** depends on the gap between expectations and perceived performance: below is disappointment, equal is satisfaction, above is delight. Overpromising creates dissatisfaction, so make claims you can beat.
- Postpurchase **dissonance** is common; reinforce the choice (welcome messages, satisfied-owner stories, clear instructions, useful updates, easy access to service).
- Satisfied customers repurchase and advocate; dissatisfied ones return, complain publicly or online, or privately exit and warn friends. Provide fast channels for redress.
- **Use and disposal**: the **consumption rate** drives repurchase. Speed replacement by tying it to a date or event, offering subscriptions, adding wear indicators, and showing where actual usage falls below recommended usage. Understand disposal for sustainability and new opportunities.

## Behavioral decision theory

Consumers are not always deliberate or rational.

- **Heuristics and biases**
  - **Availability**: events that come easily to mind seem more likely.
  - **Representativeness**: judging likelihood by resemblance to a stereotype, leading to base-rate neglect.
  - **Conjunction fallacy**: believing two events together are more likely than one alone.
- **Framing**: the same option looks cheap or expensive depending on the comparison (a mid-price option next to a premium one versus next to a budget one). Comparative claims, per-day pricing and "24 months" instead of "two years" are framing effects.
- **Mental accounting**: people put money in separate mental accounts.
  - Segregate gains (list multiple benefits separately).
  - Integrate losses (add-ons feel smaller attached to a big purchase).
  - Integrate small losses with larger gains (payroll withholding feels painless).
  - Segregate small gains from large losses (a rebate as a silver lining on a big-ticket buy).
  - Losing a pre-bought ticket feels worse than losing the same cash, because the concert account is already spent.

Honest use: frame truthfully, choose defaults that serve the customer, and never exploit biases to sell something the customer would regret on reflection. Do not invent comparison prices, hide total costs, or fake popularity or limited availability.

## Worked example: a direct-to-consumer mattress brand

- **Characteristics**: couples aged 28 to 40, first home or first child (life event); time-constrained; joint decision for a big-ticket item.
- **Problem recognition**: triggered by moving house, back pain or a new baby. Campaign moments: home-moving season, with content on sleep and parenthood.
- **Search**: heavy use of reviews and comparison sites (public sources) and friends (personal); stores used for touch and feel (experiential). The brand is often in the awareness set but drops out before the consideration set because buyers cannot try it.
- **Evaluation**: expectancy-value weights from a survey: comfort 0.4, support for back pain 0.3, price 0.2, delivery and returns 0.1. The brand scores lower than it should on comfort because buyers cannot try it, so the problem is a belief, not the product. Actions: 100-night trial and partner showroom tryouts (address belief through experience), and publish an independent firmness test (change beliefs with evidence).
- **Purchase**: perceived financial and opportunity risk is high. Offer free returns and pickup, interest-free installments, and clear total price with no hidden delivery fees.
- **Postpurchase**: send a sleep-adjustment guide in week one to reduce dissonance (new mattresses feel different for a while), ask for an honest review at day 30, and remind about the 10-year replacement cycle with a trade-in offer.

## Workflow

1. Define the target consumer and the purchase situation (new, repeat, high or low involvement).
2. List the cultural, social and personal factors that shape the need; identify buying roles and any life-event triggers.
3. Identify the real need behind the stated one (use interviews or projective techniques where motives are hidden); separate needs, wants and demand.
4. Map the five stages with the template; note sources, sets, risks and emotions at each stage.
5. Build the attribute list and an expectancy-value table for your brand and the main choice-set rivals; choose among the four levers (redesign, change own beliefs, change competitor beliefs truthfully, shift weights).
6. Decide the persuasion route: central (evidence, specifics) for motivated, able buyers; credible peripheral cues for low-involvement buyers.
7. Design risk relievers and postpurchase reinforcement; set satisfaction, repurchase and consumption-rate metrics.
8. Review copy and choice architecture for framing; keep it truthful and in the customer's interest.

## Red flags

- Personas made of demographics only, with no needs, triggers, sources or buying roles.
- Messaging that answers the stated need while the real need goes unaddressed.
- A brand known by many but considered by few, and nobody has checked which stage loses them.
- Campaigns trying to reverse entrenched attitudes when adapting the product would be cheaper.
- Comparison pages that overload buyers with options and specs and no clear recommendation.
- Big-ticket offers with no answer to functional, financial or social risk.
- Overpromising in ads, followed by complaints and returns (expectation gap).
- Low-involvement brands spending on rational argument while being out of stock at the shelf.
- No postpurchase communication, no review or redress channel, no replacement reminders.
- Framing that misleads: fake reference prices, hidden fees, invented scarcity or popularity, defaults that work against the buyer.
- Marketing to people at vulnerable life moments in ways that exploit rather than help.
