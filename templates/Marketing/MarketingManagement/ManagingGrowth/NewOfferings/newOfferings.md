# Developing New Market Offerings

Treat new-offering development as a disciplined funnel: many cheap ideas, fewer prototypes, a handful of validated business models, and one or two staged launches. Each stage ends in a gate that tests desirability (customers want it), feasibility (we can build it) and viability (it creates value for the company and collaborators). Start from an unmet customer need rather than a technology, launch first to the customers most likely to adopt, then expand. Most failures come down to one cause: the offering does not create superior value for target customers in a way that also pays off for the company and its partners.

## Why innovate and why launches fail

- Innovation is broader than products: a new technology, brand-building approach, pricing mechanism, incentive scheme, communication channel, distribution method or business model. It matters most where technology and market uncertainty are high, competition is fierce, investment is heavy and life cycles are short.
- Firms that do not innovate become exposed to shifting needs, shorter life cycles, new competitors and technologies they never noticed. Highly innovative firms make risk-taking normal, routinize the innovation process, work in teams and let people experiment and fail. Kotler uses a materials company with self-chosen projects, 'dabble time' and a 'Real, Win, Worth' screen (Is it real? Can we win? Is it worth it?) to illustrate.
- Spectrum: from minor improvements and line extensions (the large majority of new products; fewer than 1 in 10 is truly new to the world) to new-to-the-world products that create markets. Incremental innovation is cheaper and broadens brand meaning; radical innovation is riskier but can build image, durable advantage and big returns.
- Failure rates are very high. Common causes: ignored or misread research, overestimated market size, high development cost, poor performance, wrong price, weak communication, insufficient distribution support, strong competitor response, weak internal support, inadequate return.

## Organizing for innovation

| Structure | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Managers of current offerings | Know customers, competitors, processes | Focused on today's products; may lack skills or motivation for new ones |
| New-product department | Authority, top-management access, owns screening through commercialization | Can become a silo |
| Innovation centers | Local insight for regional products; hubs with partners, start-ups, universities | Coordination with the core business |
| Venture teams ('intrapreneurs', skunkworks) | Freed from other duties, separate budget, longer horizon | Integration back into the company |
| Communities of practice | Cross-department knowledge sharing | Needs sponsorship to implement ideas |
| Cross-functional teams | Mix of expertise; marketers keep engineers from building a 'better mousetrap' nobody wants | Requires clear decision rights |

## The stage-gate framework

```
Idea generation -> [idea validation] -> Concept development -> [concept validation]
-> Business-model design -> [business-model validation] -> Offering implementation
-> [offering validation / market test] -> Commercial deployment
```

1. **Idea generation**: find an unmet need and a broad idea for meeting it better than alternatives.
2. **Concept development**: build a prototype with the core functionality; validate technical feasibility and fit with the need.
3. **Business-model design**: define target market, value created and key offering attributes; validate that it meets the need in a way that benefits company and collaborators.
4. **Offering implementation**: secure resources and build a market-ready version; test it.
5. **Commercial deployment**: communicate and make it available, usually first in selected markets, while continuing to test and optimize.

Three aims run through every stage: **desirability** dominates idea generation; desirability and **feasibility** share concept development; business-model design must confirm desirability, feasibility and **viability** before full investment.

Funnel economics: early stages are cheap per idea and screen out many; investment per project rises at each gate, and the last two stages consume most resources. The process is often iterative, not linear: failing a later gate can send you back to rethink the concept or even the idea. Modern stage-gate systems are flexible and scalable, with portfolio management, accountability and outside input. Kotler cites a steelmaker that generates 50 to 100 ideas for each one implemented.

### Illustration in brief

Kotler walks a food company through the stages with a milk-additive powder: the idea is refined by asking who uses it, what primary benefit it provides and on what occasion; each answer changes the competitive set (a breakfast drink competes with cereal and breakfast bars, a snack drink with soft drinks). The chosen concept becomes a precise statement (form, benefits, flavors, pack, price), the business model adds target size, competitors, collaborator and company value with share and ROI targets plus a detailed launch mix, a test city validates it, and deployment starts with the most likely adopters and the single most popular variant before expanding.

## Idea generation

- **Top-down (market-driven)**: start with an important unmet need you can meet better than alternatives (e.g. wearables too bulky, so a discreet smart ring; thermostats that need constant programming, so a learning thermostat). This is the preferred route.
- **Bottom-up (invention-driven)**: start with a technology or accidental discovery and look for a need it meets (drugs that failed for one condition succeeding for another). Technology is never enough on its own; the first entrant with the technology often loses to the one that packages it to meet a need.

### Idea validation and the two errors

Assess **desirability** (does it address an important unmet need?) and **viability** (will it benefit the company?). Two errors:

- **Failing to reject a bad idea**, the familiar cause of failed launches.
- **Rejecting a good idea**, common too: internal resistance kills ideas with lines like 'we tried it before', 'not the right time', 'not how we do things', 'we've done fine without it', 'let's discuss next meeting'.

A frequent trap: assuming customers can easily be educated to value something that solves no pain they feel. A nice-to-have adopts far slower than forecast and leaves room for competitors (Kotler's example is an early digital video recorder).

### Exploratory research tools

- **Observe customers** in natural settings, physical and online: how they evaluate, buy, use, what they browse and share.
- **Interview customers** about unmet needs, but remember they often cannot articulate breakthroughs; current-need focus tends to yield incremental ideas.
- **Interview employees**: suggestion systems, internal incubators, monthly hack days.
- **Interview experts** (open innovation): scientists, engineers, patent attorneys, labs, consultants, channel members, agencies, even competitors.
- **Analyze competitors**: what customers like and dislike, reverse-engineering, and finding the right points of parity and difference.
- **Crowdsourcing**: contests and co-creation with customers or solver networks; prize challenges can solve technical problems cheaply.

Combine methods; each has blind spots.

## Concept development

A **prototype** is a working model that fleshes out the idea and exposes problems before the real offering is built. It need not function: a diagram, drawing, mockup or partial model is enough early on; fidelity rises as development advances.

- **Alpha testing**: inside the firm, including extreme field conditions and lab measurement.
- **Beta testing**: with customers, in labs or at home; insider programs that give interested users early versions. In B2B, watch how customers actually use the product to uncover safety, service and training needs and to quantify the value added (an input to pricing).

**Concept validation** answers two questions: Can a functional prototype, and later a full version, be built? Does it fulfill the need better than the alternatives? Tools: experiments that vary one aspect at a time (**A/B testing**) and **conjoint analysis** (respondents evaluate combinations of attributes to reveal the value of each).

## Business-model design

The jump in investment from here dwarfs earlier costs, so test commercial viability now. Three components:

- **Target market**: customers, competitors, collaborators, the company, and the context.
- **Value proposition**: the value for customers and collaborators, and how the company captures some for itself.
- **Market offering**: product, service, brand, price, incentives, communication, distribution.

Guiding questions: Does it create value for target customers? For collaborators? For the company?

### Business-model validation

- **Desirability**: customers find the benefits worth the money, time and effort (a clear cola failed despite heavy promotion because the concept itself did not appeal).
- **Feasibility**: current technology and company expertise can deliver the functionality.
- **Viability**: revenue and cost structure produce profit (a famous dot-com pet retailer had awareness but lost money on most sales).

The three interlock: an undesirable offering will not be viable; an infeasible one will not meet needs.

**Demand forecasting** confirms the need matters to a segment big enough. Three information bases: what people **say** (buyer-intention surveys, sales-force composites, expert opinion), what people **do** (test markets), what people **have done** (past purchase records, time-series and statistical demand analysis).

## Offering implementation

**Core resources**: facilities (equipment, call centers, IT), supply channels, distribution channels, skilled people, capital. Either **build or acquire** them (own them) or **collaborate** with partners who have them (use without owning).

**Developing the market offering**: turn the prototype into the final product and service, build the brand, set retail and wholesale prices, design promotions, and plan communication and distribution.

How much prototyping and **market testing** to do rises with novelty, complexity and the cost of changing the offering after launch (retooling a car plant versus updating an app). Choose test markets deliberately: representative, contained, with reasonable media costs, or markets whose demanding consumers predict where the world is heading. Alternatives to classic test markets: launch fast and fix flaws in the first months when speed matters more than perfection; launch in a region big enough that rivals cannot disrupt the read, and use scanner or usage data within days.

**Minimum viable offering**: a streamlined version with only the features essential to meet the need, to test market performance before building the full version.

## Commercial deployment

### Selective market deployment

Launch first to a **primary target**: customers most likely to buy, who will help refine the offering and generate early revenue, and whom you can reach efficiently. Benefits: a natural test environment that reveals customer, competitor and collaborator reactions; agility to adjust; fewer resources; revenue to fund expansion. Some products catch on immediately, others take years; plan the runway accordingly and expect incumbents to fight back (legal, technical and competitive moves).

### Market expansion

Ramp production, promote to all target customers and ensure availability across the whole market. Expansion moves to customers who are harder to reach and slower to see the value, so it costs more effort per customer than the initial launch. Broader markets usually need variants for different needs (enter with one offering for likely adopters, add variations as you widen), which in turn needs more resources.

## Adoption of innovations

**Diffusion of innovation**: how knowledge of an offering spreads and how fast it is bought or used.

**Rogers' model** (adopter categories by time of adoption):

| Category | Share | Profile | How to market |
|---|---|---|---|
| Innovators | ~2.5% | Venturesome tinkerers | Early access, lower price in exchange for testing and feedback |
| Early adopters | ~13.5% | Opinion leaders seeking advantage | Personalized solutions, strong support; less price sensitive |
| Early majority | ~34% | Deliberate pragmatists; adopt once benefits are proven | Proof, references, mainstream availability |
| Late majority | ~34% | Skeptical, risk-averse, price-sensitive | Lower price, low risk, simplicity |
| Laggards | ~16% | Tradition-bound | Adopt only when the status quo is untenable |

Some experts also target **revenue leaders** (highest lifetime value) to reach profit faster. Limits: it classifies but cannot predict a given person's category, and people can be innovators in one domain and laggards in another.

**Moore's model** (technology products): technology enthusiasts, visionaries, pragmatists, conservatives, skeptics. Adoption is **discontinuous**: success in one group does not roll over to the next because each group values different things. The biggest gap, the **chasm**, lies between early adopters (enthusiasts and visionaries, who want breakthroughs) and the mainstream (pragmatists, who want productivity gains within existing models, plus conservatives and skeptics). Crossing it is the central challenge for tech pioneers.

## Worked example: AI meeting-notes add-on for a B2B collaboration platform

| Gate | Evidence required | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | Interviews with 20 team leads; observed that action items get lost after calls (top-down need) | Pass: frequent, painful, current workarounds are manual |
| Concept | Clickable prototype tested with 8 customers; A/B test of 'summary first' vs 'action items first' layout | Action-items-first preferred; transcription accuracy feasible for English only |
| Business model | Target: mid-size teams already on the platform; value: 2 hours saved per manager per week; price as a paid tier; partner value for integrators; 18-month payback | Pass with condition: inference cost per meeting under target |
| Implementation | Minimum viable offering (English, one calendar integration); beta with 40 customer workspaces | Fix privacy controls (opt-in recording, retention settings) before launch |
| Deployment | Primary target: existing customers in tech and agencies (visionary teams); then expand to pragmatists with admin controls, compliance documentation and more languages | Chasm plan: references from one vertical before broad launch |

## New-offering gate checklist

```markdown
| Gate | Question | Evidence | Decision (go / pivot / kill) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Idea | Important unmet need? Better than alternatives? Benefits the company? | | |
| Concept | Can we build it? Does the prototype meet the need better than alternatives? | | |
| Business model | Target market, value for customers / collaborators / company, offering attributes; desirable, feasible, viable; demand forecast basis | | |
| Offering | Resources secured (build, buy, partner); market test or MVO results | | |
| Deployment | Primary target defined; expansion triggers; variants planned | | |
```

## Workflow

1. Frame the opportunity as an unmet customer need; note whether the idea is top-down or bottom-up (if bottom-up, find the need before proceeding).
2. Generate many ideas cheaply using observation, interviews (customers, employees, experts), competitor analysis and crowdsourcing.
3. Screen ideas for desirability and viability; actively guard against both screening errors and against 'customers will learn to want it' assumptions.
4. Write a precise concept (who, benefit, occasion, form, price) and build the lowest-fidelity prototype that answers the open question; run alpha, beta, A/B or conjoint tests.
5. Design the business model: target market, value proposition for customers, collaborators and company, and the full offering; forecast demand from what people say, do or have done.
6. Decide build, buy or partner for missing resources; choose market testing depth from novelty, complexity and cost of post-launch change; consider a minimum viable offering.
7. Pick a primary target (most likely adopters, reachable, revenue-generating) and define expansion triggers and variants.
8. Map adopter categories and, for technology, the chasm; plan different messages and proof for each group.
9. Keep testing and optimizing after launch; record gate decisions to improve the process.

## Red flags

- A launch plan that begins with the technology and never names the customer pain.
- No kill criteria at any gate; every idea that enters the funnel ships.
- Market size estimated from total population rather than people with the need.
- Prototype fidelity far higher than the question requires, or no prototype before full build.
- Business case shows customer value but not collaborator value (retailers, partners) or a path to profit.
- National launch of a novel, complex product with no test or primary target.
- Treating innovators' enthusiasm as proof the mainstream will follow.
- Beta tests run without the customer-facing team observing real use.
- Good ideas killed by 'we tried that before' with no evidence.
- An MVP so stripped that it no longer meets the core need, so the test proves nothing.
