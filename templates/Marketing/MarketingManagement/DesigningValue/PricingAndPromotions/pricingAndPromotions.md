# Managing Pricing and Sales Promotions

Price is the one element of the marketing mix that produces revenue; the others produce costs. It also communicates the value positioning of the brand. Set price deliberately: start from a clear objective, understand how customers perceive price, let costs set the floor, competitors orient and customer value set the ceiling, build a price structure rather than a single number, and use promotions sparingly so they do not teach customers that the list price is soft.

## How pricing goes wrong

- Pure cost-plus: 'calculate costs and add the industry margin'.
- Not revising prices often enough to capture market changes.
- Setting price independently of the rest of the marketing program instead of as part of positioning.
- Not varying price enough across product items, segments, channels and purchase occasions.
- Unclear ownership. Large firms set objectives centrally and let line managers propose; in B2B, pricing improves when authority is shared across sales, marketing and finance and balanced between the field and central management.

Context: buyers compare prices instantly online and in-store on phones and can pool demand; sellers can monitor demand, adjust prices in real time, and tailor promotions by segment, individual or location.

## Consumer psychology and pricing

Customers are not price takers. They interpret price using prior purchases, advertising, sales calls, word of mouth and point-of-purchase information, and decide on the **perceived** price, not the stated one. They have a lower threshold (below which quality seems suspect) and an upper threshold (above which it is not worth it).

### Reference prices

Few people recall exact prices, but they compare observed prices with internal or external references:

- Fair price, typical price, last price paid.
- Upper bound (reservation price) and lower bound (lower threshold).
- Competitor price, expected future price, usual discounted price.

Ways sellers shape reference prices: placing a product among expensive competitors, separating departments by price tier, stating a manufacturer's suggested price, showing an original higher price, pointing to a competitor's higher price. Use only true references: invented 'was' prices and fake list prices are deceptive and illegal in many markets.

- Unpleasant surprises (perceived price higher than expected) affect purchase more than pleasant ones.
- Expectations matter: if buyers expect cheaper supply later, they pay less now.
- Framing: breaking a price into smaller units ('$50 a month' vs '$600 a year') can make it feel smaller. Show the total clearly too.

### Image pricing

- Many buyers use price as a quality signal, especially for ego-sensitive products (perfume, cars, designer clothes, gifts).
- Price signals quality most when real quality information is missing; when quality information is available, price matters less as a cue.
- Exclusivity and scarcity justify premiums; for some luxury buyers a higher price raises demand because fewer others can afford it. Deliberately limiting supply can protect the brand. Status meaning varies by market (a basic brand at home can be premium abroad).

### Pricing cues

- **Odd endings**: buyers read left to right, so $299 feels like 'the $200s'. Useful where there is a mental price break.
- 9-endings signal bargain; avoid them for a premium image. Prices ending in 0 or 5 are easy to process and remember.
- **Sale signs** lift demand, but only if not overused: category sales peak when some, not all, items carry them.
- Cues work best when buyers know little about prices, buy rarely, are new to the category, or face varying designs, seasonal prices or sizes. They wear out with use.
- **Limited availability** ('three days only') spurs active shoppers. Only state it when it is true.

## Setting the price: six steps

Firms set prices for new products, new channels or regions, and bids. Most markets have three to five price tiers; brand strategy should signal each price-quality tier.

### 1. Define the pricing objective

| Objective | What | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Short-term profit | Price that maximizes current profit, cash flow or ROI | Requires known demand and cost curves; risks sacrificing the long run, ignoring competitors and law |
| Market penetration | Low price to maximize share, lower unit costs, higher long-run profit | Market highly price sensitive and grows at low prices; costs fall with experience; low price discourages competitors |
| Market skimming | High price to 'skim' buyers with the highest willingness to pay, then lower over time | Enough buyers with high current demand; high price does not attract competitors; high price signals superiority |
| Quality leadership | Premium price to fund R&D, production and service | Strong brand, loyal base, real quality edge |

Nonprofits may aim for partial or full cost recovery, maximum attendance, or income-based prices. Whatever the objective, firms that use price strategically outperform those that let costs or the market decide.

### 2. Determine demand

- Demand curves usually slope down; for prestige goods they can slope up for a while.
- **Price elasticity**: how much quantity changes with price. Inelastic: demand barely moves (a price rise can raise revenue). Elastic: demand moves a lot (a cut can raise revenue if costs do not climb disproportionately).
- Elasticity depends on the size and direction of the change; there is often a **price indifference band** where small changes do nothing.
- Short-run and long-run elasticities differ (buyers may stay then leave, or leave then return), so the full effect of a change takes time to see.
- Elasticity is low when:
  1. The product is distinctive with few substitutes.
  2. Buyers do not notice the higher price.
  3. Buyers are slow to change habits.
  4. Buyers think the higher price is justified (cost, scarcity, taxes).
  5. The spend is small relative to income or to the end product's total cost.
  6. Someone else pays part or all of it.
- Research averages: elasticity around -2.6 across studies; higher for durables and early life-cycle products; short-run promotional elasticities exceed regular price elasticities.

### 3. Estimate costs

Demand sets the ceiling; costs set the floor (cost of producing, distributing and selling plus a fair return for effort and risk).

- **Fixed costs** do not vary with output; **variable costs** do; **total cost** = fixed + variable; **average cost** = total / units.
- Scale: average cost falls as a plant fills, rises past capacity, and plant size has an optimum before diseconomies of scale set in.
- Use **activity-based costing** to see the real cost of serving different retailers and customers.
- **Experience curve**: average cost falls as accumulated production rises (for example a fixed percentage drop each time volume doubles). A low-cost leader can cut price to push out higher-cost rivals, gain volume and fall further down the curve. Risks: a cheap image; assumes weak followers; a competitor may leapfrog with lower-cost technology and strand the leader. Experience effects apply to marketing costs too.

### 4. Analyze competitors' costs, prices and offers

- Value the differences: add the worth to the customer of features you have and the competitor lacks to the competitor's price; subtract the worth of features they have and you lack. Then decide to price above, at or below.
- **Value players** (low price plus good quality, efficient operations, a few segments, one extra benefit) have changed what customers expect.
- Launch your own low-cost operation only if it makes the existing business more competitive and gains advantages it would not have alone, and design it to make money in its own right, not merely as defense (fighter brands without synergies have often failed).

### 5. Select a pricing method

Costs set the floor, competitors and substitutes give the orienting point, customers' valuation of unique features sets the ceiling.

| Method | How | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Markup** | Unit cost / (1 - desired return on sales) | Unit cost = variable + fixed / units. Simple, reduces price competition when everyone does it, feels fair; but ignores demand, perceived value and competition, so rarely optimal. Markups run higher on seasonal, specialty, slow, bulky or inelastic items. |
| **Target-rate-of-return** | Unit cost + (desired return x invested capital) / units | Common in regulated industries. Check with a **break-even** volume = fixed cost / (price - variable cost); test other prices and volumes, since it ignores elasticity and competitors. |
| **Economic-value-to-customer** | Price on the customer's total perceived value: performance, channel deliverables, warranty, support, reputation, trust | Must deliver and communicate the value. Strongest argument: lowest **total cost of ownership**. Itemize the premium (durability + reliability + service + warranty) and show the customer gets more than they pay. Charge for valuable services rather than giving them away. Price in units customers can compare (per mile of tire wear rather than technical specs). Some segments still buy on price alone. |
| **Competitive (going-rate)** | Price off competitors, keep a stable premium or discount | Common in commodities and when costs, demand or competitive response are uncertain. |
| **Auction** | English (ascending, one seller, many buyers); Dutch (descending, or reverse auctions where sellers bid down); sealed-bid (one bid each) | Reverse auctions can feel opportunistic to incumbent suppliers; many bidders raise satisfaction on both sides. |

Worked markup example (from the method above, own numbers): variable cost $40, fixed cost $200,000, expected 10,000 units → unit cost $60; 25% return on sales → price $80. Break-even at $80 = 200,000 / (80 - 40) = 5,000 units.

### 6. Set the final price and price structure

Companies build a **pricing structure** varying by geography, segment, timing, order size, delivery frequency, guarantees and service contracts. After discounts, allowances and promotions, profit per unit varies.

**Price discrimination**: selling at prices that do not reflect proportional cost differences.

- **First degree**: a separate price per customer by intensity of demand.
- **Second degree**: lower prices for larger volumes (though tiered usage plans can charge more for more use).
- **Third degree**, by segment:
  - **Customer segment** (students, seniors).
  - **Product form** (the same water in a bottle vs a spray).
  - **Channel** (restaurant vs vending machine).
  - **Location** (seat position).
  - **Time** (early-bird, weekend, seasonal peaks).
- **Yield pricing**: discounted limited early sales, higher late prices, lowest rates for inventory about to expire.

Conditions for price discrimination to work:

1. The market is segmentable with different demand intensities.
2. Low-price buyers cannot resell to high-price buyers.
3. Competitors cannot undersell in the high-price segment.
4. Segmenting and policing costs do not exceed the extra revenue.
5. It does not breed resentment.

Making variable prices acceptable: bundle uniquely so prices are hard to compare, and frame variation as a **reward** (cheaper for booking early) rather than a penalty. Legal limits: some discrimination within the same trade class is illegal unless justified by cost differences; **predatory pricing** (below cost to eliminate competitors) is unlawful.

## Product-mix pricing

Find the set of prices that maximizes profit on the whole mix:

- **Loss-leader**: cut price on a known item to build traffic; pays only if extra sales cover lost margin. Brand owners dislike it (dilution, complaints from full-price retailers).
- **Optional-feature**: decide what is standard and what is extra (cheap food with profitable drinks, or the reverse).
- **Captive-product**: low price on the base (razor, printer, device), high margin on consumables or required items. Overpriced captives invite counterfeits and substitutes.
- **Two-part**: fixed fee plus usage fee; keep the fixed fee low enough to induce purchase and earn on usage.
- **By-product**: price by-products on their value; the income lets you cut the main price if needed, and by-products can grow into a business.
- **Bundling**:
  - **Pure bundling**: only as a bundle; tied sales.
  - **Mixed bundling**: items alone or bundled, with the bundle cheaper; savings must be enough to tempt people who did not plan to buy everything.
  - **Unbundling or rebundling** for buyers who want less: if removing a service saves you $100 and you cut the price $80, both sides win.

## Initiating and responding to price changes

### Price cuts

- Reasons: excess capacity that other levers cannot fill; a drive to dominate through lower costs.
- Risks:
  - Trains customers to demand concessions and salespeople to give them.
  - Signals low quality.
  - Buys share, not loyalty.
  - Triggers price wars.
  - Lower-cost rivals can match and outlast you.
- Customers may read a cut as: a new model coming, a faulty product, financial trouble, further cuts coming, reduced quality. Monitor these attributions.

### Price increases

- Small increases have big profit leverage: at a 3% margin, a 1% price rise with unchanged volume lifts profit by a third.
- Reasons: cost inflation (sometimes anticipatory pricing ahead of further inflation or controls); demand exceeding supply (raise price, ration, or both).
- Avoid looking like a gouger. The more similar your products, the more price differences look unfair; differentiate and explain differences.
- Techniques:
  - Give advance notice so customers can buy ahead or shop around.
  - Explain sharp rises in understandable terms.
  - Make low-visibility moves first: remove discounts, raise minimum orders, curtail low-margin products.
  - Use escalator clauses tied to recognized indexes in long contracts.

### Responding to competitors' price changes

Competitors react most when there are few firms, the product is homogeneous and buyers are informed. Anticipate the reaction: either assume a standard response, or analyze each competitor's situation (finances, sales, loyalty, objectives). Share-focused competitors match; profit-focused ones may respond with advertising or quality.

When a competitor cuts, ask:

1. Why: to steal share, use excess capacity, follow costs, lead an industry-wide change?
2. Temporary or permanent?
3. What happens to our share and profit if we do nothing; will others respond?
4. What are the likely responses to each possible reaction?

Options for a leader attacked by low-cost rivals or store brands:

- Further differentiate.
- Launch a low-cost venture.
- Reinvent as a low-cost player.

Choose based on your ability to raise demand or cut costs. Where prices move fast, prepare contingency responses in advance.

## Managing incentives (sales promotion)

Incentives are mostly short-term tools to stimulate quicker or greater purchase by consumers or the trade.

### Effects and dangers

- Strong short-term response, little lasting gain; price promotions rarely build permanent category volume.
- **Stockpiling**: purchase acceleration and extra quantities, then a post-promotion dip.
- Constant deals devalue the brand and teach buyers to wait; categories that are always on sale destroy themselves. Long-running financing and rebate programs are hard to withdraw.
- Salespeople discount to close; word spreads that list price is 'soft'.
- Supplying a deep-discount store brand to fill capacity can erode your own brand.
- **Consumer-franchise-building** tools carry a selling message (frequency rewards, coupons that explain features, product-related premiums). **Non-brand-building** tools: price-off packs, unrelated premiums, contests and sweepstakes, refunds, trade allowances.
- Monitor the share of customers getting discounts, the average discount and salespeople's reliance on it. Run a **net price analysis**: list $3,000 minus average discount $300, promotional spend $450, co-op advertising $150 = real price $2,100.

### Major decisions

1. **Objectives**, derived from marketing objectives.
   - Consumer: more frequent or larger purchases by users, trial by nonusers, switching from competitors. Ideally short-run sales plus long-run equity.
   - Retailer: carry the brand, carry more units, feature and display it and cut its price, push it through store staff.
2. **Size and approach**:
   - Incentive size (meaningful to the target).
   - Conditions for participation (everyone or select groups).
   - Duration.
   - Distribution vehicle (pack, store, mail, online, ads).
   - Timing.
   - Budget = (administrative cost + incentive cost including redemption) x expected units, allowing for partial redemption.
3. **Push vs pull**:
   - **Push** (sales force and trade money induce intermediaries to sell): fits low brand loyalty, in-store choice, impulse items, well-understood benefits.
   - **Pull** (advertising and consumer promotion create demand that pulls product through): fits high loyalty and involvement, visible brand differences, choice made before the store.
   - The best marketers combine both: push works better with pull demand behind it.
4. **Implement, control and evaluate** results against objectives, including post-promotion dips and long-run brand effects.

### Consumer incentives

| Tool | Description |
|---|---|
| Price reductions | Temporary discounts, framed as money off or percentage off |
| Coupons | Certificates for a stated saving; print, in-pack, email, digital (digital coupons are cheaper, updatable and redeem more) |
| Cash refunds (rebates) | Price reduction after purchase on proof of purchase; clear inventory without cutting list price |
| Price packs | Reduced-price pack (two for one) or banded pack (two related products) |
| Premiums | Free or low-cost merchandise with purchase, in or on pack or by mail |
| Frequency programs | Rewards tied to frequency and intensity of purchase |
| Prizes | Contests (judged entries), sweepstakes (draws), games (something with every purchase) |
| Tie-in promotions | Two or more brands combine coupons, refunds or contests |
| Seasonal discounts | Lower prices off-season |
| Financing | Favorable terms or longer payment periods |

### Trade incentives

- **Allowances**: payments for point-of-sale promotion, extra inventory or added services.
- **Free goods**: free merchandise for buying a quantity or featuring an item.
- **Price-off**: a straight discount per case for a period.
- **Payment discount**: for prompt payment ('2/10, net 30': 2% off if paid in 10 days, due in 30).

Big retailers demand more trade money; sales and brand managers fight over trade vs consumer spending. Insist on proof of performance before paying allowances. Watch **forward buying** (stocking up during the deal) and **diverting** (shipping deal-region stock to non-deal regions); limit discounted quantities or deliver partial orders.

### Sales force incentives

Contests, prizes, trips and points to push new products, prospecting and off-season sales; trade shows for B2B launches (highest cost per contact of all communication options).

## Ethics of pricing

Kotler's insight box on prescription drugs shows how pricing essential products purely for maximum profit (huge price hikes on old, sole-source medicines) harms social welfare, causes public outcry and regulation, and calls for transparency. Apply the lesson generally:

- Do not exploit urgent need, captive customers or lack of alternatives with gouging, extreme surge pricing or hidden fees.
- Make price structures transparent; show the total price, not just a small unit price.
- Use only genuine reference prices, real deadlines and real stock limits.
- Treat price changes customers will see as unfair (identical products, different prices with no explanation) as a brand risk, not just a revenue opportunity.

## Worked example: pricing a B2B SaaS add-on

A scheduling SaaS adds an 'AI rostering' add-on.

1. **Objective**: skimming among large hospitals first (high willingness to pay, competitors 12+ months behind, premium signal), then broaden.
2. **Demand**: interviews show buyers frame the cost against overtime spend; the expense is small relative to payroll and partly budgeted by operations (low elasticity).
3. **Costs**: compute per-seat inference cost as variable; engineering as fixed; break-even at 140 customers at $6 per seat per month.
4. **Competitors**: one rival bundles a weaker feature free; value the difference (overtime saved, compliance) and price above it.
5. **Method**: economic value to customer: show '$6 per seat; typical customer saves $40 per seat in overtime' with the calculation open for audit.
6. **Structure**: mixed bundling (add-on or within the Enterprise tier), annual prepay discount framed as a reward, nonprofit pricing tier.
7. **Promotion**: a 60-day pilot for existing customers (trial objective, brand-building because it demonstrates the feature) instead of a blanket discount.

## Workflow

1. Confirm positioning and target; write down the pricing objective explicitly.
2. Map the reference prices customers will use and the quality signals price sends in this category.
3. Estimate demand and elasticity (past data, tests, interviews); note what makes elasticity low or high here.
4. Build the cost picture: fixed, variable, break-even, experience effects, cost to serve by channel.
5. Value competitor differences and decide where to sit relative to them.
6. Choose a method (usually value-based, sanity-checked against cost and competition) and compute candidate prices.
7. Build the structure: tiers, segment, channel, time and volume variations, bundles and options; check the five conditions for price discrimination and legal limits.
8. For a change, run the price change decision sheet; for a competitor attack, answer the four response questions and pick differentiate, low-cost venture or reinvent.
9. For promotions, set objectives, size, conditions, duration, vehicle, timing and budget; choose brand-building tools; balance push and pull; plan proof of performance for trade money.
10. Run a net price analysis periodically and track discount reliance; flag any tactic that could be perceived as unfair or deceptive.

## Red flags

- Price set as cost plus the 'usual' margin, with no view of customer value.
- No stated pricing objective; launch price chosen by gut.
- A premium positioning sold with 9-ending prices and permanent sale banners.
- Fabricated 'was' prices, fake countdown timers or fake low-stock warnings.
- Monthly price shown without the annual total, or fees revealed only at checkout.
- Services given away free that customers value and would pay for.
- Repeated discounts to hit quarterly targets; salespeople discounting by default; list price nobody pays.
- A price cut to win share with no estimate of competitor reaction or cost staying power.
- A price increase announced without notice, explanation or low-visibility steps first.
- Surge or dynamic pricing at moments of genuine need with no cap or explanation.
- Trade allowances paid without proof of performance; unchecked forward buying and diverting.
- Promotions measured only by the spike, ignoring the post-promotion dip and brand effects.
