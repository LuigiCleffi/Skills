# Crafting a Customer Value Proposition and Positioning

No offering wins if it looks like every other offering. Define the full value proposition (all the functional, psychological and monetary benefits and costs you promise versus the customer's alternatives), then distill the positioning: the frame of reference you compete in, the points of parity you only need to be good enough on, and the few points of difference that are desirable, deliverable and differentiating. Capture it in a positioning statement backed by reasons to believe, and test it: if a competitor's name could be swapped in and it still worked, it is not a positioning.

## The value proposition

### Three kinds of value

- **Functional value**: benefits and costs tied to performance: reliability, durability, compatibility, ease of use, customization, form, style, packaging. Dominant for utilitarian offerings (office and industrial equipment).
- **Psychological value**: emotional and self-expressive benefits and costs (the joy and status of a high-performance car). Dominant in luxury and fashion.
- **Monetary value**: price, fees, discounts, rebates, costs of use and disposal, and monetary benefits (cash back, bonuses, rewards, low-interest financing). Dominant for undifferentiated commodities.

### Customer value

- Customers are **value maximizers** within limits of search cost, knowledge, mobility and income. They pick the offer they believe gives the most value; whether it lives up to expectations drives satisfaction and repurchase.
- **Customer value** = (total customer benefit minus total customer cost) for your offering, compared with the same for the perceived alternatives.
  - **Total customer benefit**: the functional, psychological and monetary benefits expected from product, service and image.
  - **Total customer cost**: the functional, psychological and monetary costs of evaluating, obtaining, using and disposing.
- Raise value by increasing benefits or cutting costs on any of the three dimensions.
- The **value proposition** is the whole cluster of benefits promised, broader than the core positioning: a car brand positioned on safety also promises performance, design and environmental care. It is a promise about the experience and relationship, kept only if the value delivery system works. Different segments need different value propositions.

### Customer value analysis

1. Identify the attributes and benefits customers value (define broadly, all inputs to the decision).
2. Have customers rate their importance; if ratings diverge widely, cluster them into segments.
3. Rate your performance and competitors' on each key attribute. If you lead on all important ones, charge more or hold price to gain share.
4. Repeat periodically as economy, technology and features change.

A seller at a disadvantage can raise total benefit (strengthen product, services, brand image) or lower total cost (price, cost of ownership and maintenance, simpler ordering and delivery, absorbing risk with warranties).

## Positioning

**Positioning** is designing the offering and image to occupy a distinctive place in the minds of the target market. Unlike the value proposition, it zeroes in on the key benefits that give the reason to choose.

- It clarifies the brand's essence, the goals it helps customers reach, and how it does so uniquely. Everyone in the organization should know it and use it to make decisions.
- Combine rational and emotional components (head and heart); build emotional appeal on a performance advantage (a scar-treatment product found buyers also sought self-esteem and added that to its medical message).
- **Brand substitution test**: replace your brand with a competitor's in an ad, post, sponsorship or launch. If it would work just as well, the positioning is not distinctive or the execution is not tied to it.
- Keep **one foot in the present and one in the future**: aspirational enough to leave room to grow, realistic enough to be attainable.
- Positioning requires two decisions: (1) the **frame of reference** (target market and relevant competition), and (2) the optimal **points of parity and points of difference** within it.

## Choosing a frame of reference

Customers judge value against a reference point; the same offering looks attractive next to an inferior option and unattractive next to a superior one. Design the frame deliberately.

- The frame follows from the target: choosing a segment defines who you compete with and which brands that segment already considers.
- Start with **category membership**: the products that act as close substitutes.
- Look beyond obvious rivals: growth into new markets may need a broader or aspirational frame, and emerging competitors or technologies may hurt more than current ones. Categories fragment into subcategories where the pioneer may be less relevant.
- Choose frames that evoke **advantageous comparisons**: a roadside-assistance club framing itself as a fourth emergency service; a card game reframed as a mind sport alongside chess; military recruiting reframed from duty to leadership training to compete with employers.
- Stable markets may have one to three key competitors; dynamic categories may need several frames.

## Points of difference (PODs)

Attributes or benefits that consumers **strongly associate** with the brand, **evaluate positively**, and believe they **could not find to the same extent** in a competitor. They can rest on any attribute or benefit (most stylish, longest lasting, best advice), including **brand authenticity**: being faithful to the brand's essence and reason for being. Strong brands often have several PODs.

A candidate POD must pass three tests:

| Criterion | Test | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Desirable** to consumers | Personally relevant, with a compelling **reason to believe** | Substantiate: an ingredient, a heritage, a patented or branded component, a measurable spec |
| **Deliverable** by the company | Resources and commitment to create and maintain it feasibly and profitably | Does it need real product change or only a perception shift (easier)? Best PODs are preemptive, defensible and hard to attack; performance-based positions are easier to sustain than fashion-based ones |
| **Differentiating** from competitors | Seen as distinctive and superior to relevant rivals | Check against every brand in the frame |

## Points of parity (POPs)

Associations not unique to the brand, shared with others. Three kinds:

- **Category POPs**: what customers see as essential to be a legitimate, credible member of the category. Necessary but not sufficient; the entry fee to play. They change with technology, law and trends.
- **Correlational POPs**: potential negatives that arise from your positives, because customers see some benefits as inversely related (cheap therefore not high quality). Research the trade-offs buyers make.
- **Competitive POPs**: associations that neutralize competitors' PODs. Role-play each competitor's positioning to infer their intended PODs, which tell you your required POPs.

**Zone of tolerance**: parity does not mean equal; enough customers must believe the brand is good enough on that dimension, so they decide on other factors. A lighter product need only taste close enough to the full version. Often the key to winning is achieving parity rather than more difference: two card networks spent years neutralizing each other's advantages (one adding prestige tiers, the other widening acceptance). If you break even where you are weak and win where you are strong, the position can be unbeatable.

## Aligning frame, POPs and PODs

- A brand may face **several frames** (a coffee chain competing with quick-service restaurants, at-home coffee and local cafes, with different POPs and PODs against each). Options:
  1. Develop the best positioning per frame, then find one combined positioning robust enough for all.
  2. If competition is too diverse, prioritize and pick the most important frame.
  3. With many categories, position at the category level or against one exemplar per category.
- Never try to be all things to all people; that yields **lowest-common-denominator** positioning.
- **Straddle positioning**: one set of PODs and POPs spans two frames, with the POD in one frame serving as the POP in the other (healthy and tasty sandwiches; luxury plus performance cars that created an umbrella category). It widens coverage but risks being legitimate in neither category if the claims are not credible.
- Usually two or three PODs and POPs define the battlefield; develop those carefully.
- **80-20 rule**: the positioning should fit about 80 percent of the brand's products; review the rest for proper branding rather than diluting the positioning to cover 100 percent.
- **Perceptual (positioning) maps**: plot brands on the dimensions customers use and overlay preferences to find holes that signal unmet needs.

## Sustainable competitive advantage

- **Competitive advantage**: the ability to satisfy a need better than alternatives, giving customers a reason to choose; a firm's ability to perform in ways competitors cannot or will not match.
- Start from the belief that any offering can be differentiated; avoid the commodity trap.
- Few advantages are inherently sustainable; most get copied. Seek **leverageable** advantages that spring to new ones, and keep inventing new PODs.
- Sources: performance benefits (fashionable watches, cheap reliable insurance); repositioning when the environment changes (a soft drink repositioned as all-natural with few ingredients); images that meet social and psychological needs; physical spaces; channels designed to make buying easier and more rewarding (premium pet food sold through vets and breeders).

### Three core strategies

| Strategy | What it means | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Differentiate on an existing attribute** | Be better on something customers already value: shave quality, low price, service, driving experience, safety, luxury | Hard as category performance converges and products look alike |
| **Introduce a new attribute** | Add something rivals lack: a give-one program, all-natural formula, subscription delivery, cashless payment, machine learning; or turn a neglected attribute (packaging, design) into a POD | Valued new attributes get copied fast; keep creating new ones |
| **Build a strong brand** | Meaning that influences perceived quality, reliability and durability and adds value beyond the product; vital in commodity categories (a premium vodka where few can taste a difference) | Requires sustained investment |

A strong brand also drives behavior through **top-of-mind awareness**: the first brand recalled becomes the reference point and **default option** against which others are judged, and buyers pick the default unless given a strong reason not to.

## Communicating the positioning

### The positioning statement

Kotler's format: **For [target customers], [brand] offers [the key benefit / value proposition].** Examples in the chapter take the form "for busy professionals, a fast, convenient way to rent the right car at the airport".

- Lead with **benefits**, which customers care about; use **attributes** as **reasons to believe** or proof points (a quarter moisturizing cream supporting softer skin; highly trained crews supporting superior service). Attributes can change; benefits endure.
- Share the statement internally so it guides everyone's words and actions.

Extended template the agent can produce:

```markdown
## Positioning statement
For <target segment and need>,
<brand> is the <frame of reference / category>
that <key point(s) of difference as customer benefits>,
because <reasons to believe: attributes, proof points>.
Unlike <primary competitive alternative>, we <how we compare>.

## Points of parity (good enough on)
- Category POPs: ...
- Correlational POPs: ...
- Competitive POPs: ...

## Points of difference (test each)
| POD | Desirable? (reason to believe) | Deliverable? (resources, product vs perception) | Differentiating? (vs each rival) |
|---|---|---|---|

## Full value proposition
Functional / psychological / monetary benefits and costs vs alternatives.

## Brand substitution test
Would this statement, ad or sponsorship still work with <competitor> in it? If yes, sharpen.
```

### Communicating category membership

Usually state membership before difference: people need to know what it is before judging whether it is better. New products build awareness first, image second. Three ways:

- **Announce category benefits** (durability for tools, efficacy for antacids, taste for a dessert mix, backed by ingredients or user imagery).
- **Compare to exemplars**: associate with well-known members of the category (an unknown designer pictured alongside recognized names).
- **Use the product descriptor** after the brand name (a crossover vehicle called a "sports wagon" to signal a distinct position).

Also reinforce membership when buyers know you are in the category but doubt you are a valid member. Deliberately borrowing a category you do not belong to can dramatize a POD (a frozen pizza compared with delivery; a pay channel saying it is not ordinary TV), provided buyers know your real category.

### Communicating conflicting benefits

Many POPs and PODs are negatively correlated: low price versus high quality, powerful versus safe, taste versus low calories, strong versus refined, nutritious versus tasty, ubiquitous versus exclusive, efficacious versus mild, varied versus simple. Heritage can mean expertise and authenticity or old-fashioned. Customers want both. Options:

- Best: make the product genuinely perform on both (a fabric both breathable and waterproof; technology without the hassle).
- Run separate campaigns, each devoted to one benefit.
- Link the brand to a person, place or thing with the right equity to establish the weaker benefit (credible endorsers who would not drink a beer that tasted bad).
- Convince customers that the relationship, seen differently, is actually positive.

### Positioning as storytelling

- Narrative branding draws on deep metaphors tied to memories and associations. Five elements: the brand story in words and metaphors; the consumer journey and touch points; the visual language; the experiential, sensory expression; the role the brand plays in people's lives.
- Four parts of a brand story: **setting** (time, place, context), **cast** (the brand as a character, its role, relationships, history or creation myth), **narrative arc** (actions, experiences, defining events, the moment of epiphany), **language** (authentic voice, metaphors, symbols, themes, leitmotifs).
- **Primal branding** treats a brand as a belief system with seven assets: creation story, creed, icon, rituals, sacred words, a way of dealing with nonbelievers, and a leader.

## Positioning a start-up (or small brand)

- Find a compelling product or service **performance advantage** that is demonstrable and meaningful.
- Build **one or two strong brands on one or two key associations**, reinforced consistently over time.
- Encourage **trial** any way possible (sampling, demos, free tiers).
- Use a cohesive **digital strategy** to look bigger and better than your size.
- Create **buzz and a loyal brand community**; recruit passionate power users as ambassadors.
- Use a well-integrated set of **brand elements** (name, logo, packaging) that are memorable and meaningful; packaging can do the work of advertising.
- Leverage **secondary associations** (people, places, partners, endorsers, certifications) to signal quality and credibility cheaply.

Kotler shows a challenger mobile carrier that repositioned against the incumbents by removing the industry's biggest frustrations while matching network quality, illustrating parity where you are weak plus difference where customers hurt most.

## Worked example: positioning an expense-management app for mid-size companies

- **Target**: finance managers at companies with 50 to 500 employees who reconcile receipts by hand each month-end.
- **Frame of reference**: options considered (spreadsheets plus email receipts, the expense module of their accounting suite, and enterprise expense platforms). Chosen frame: "expense management software for mid-size finance teams", benchmarked mainly against the accounting-suite module (the status quo most prospects use).
- **Category POPs**: receipt capture, approvals, accounting integration, mobile app, audit trail.
- **Competitive POPs**: enterprise-grade security and SSO (neutralizing the enterprise platforms' security POD); price within 10 percent of the accounting-suite add-on (neutralizing its cost POD).
- **Correlational POP**: being simple can imply "not powerful enough", so show policy rules and multi-entity support as proof.
- **PODs tested**:
  - "Month-end close in one day instead of five" (desirable: the finance manager's main pain; deliverable: auto-matching card transactions to receipts already works at 95 percent accuracy; differentiating: suite modules require manual matching). Keep.
  - "Beautiful design" (desirable to employees, not to the buyer; easily matched). Drop as a POD; keep as a supporting attribute.
- **Positioning statement**: For finance managers at growing mid-size companies, [App] is the expense management software that cuts month-end close from days to one day, because card transactions are matched to receipts automatically and exceptions are flagged by policy. Unlike accounting-suite expense modules, nobody has to chase receipts or match lines by hand.
- **Reasons to believe**: 95 percent auto-match rate, customer case with measured close time, SOC 2 report, native integrations.
- **Substitution test**: swapping in the accounting suite's name breaks the claim, so the positioning is distinctive.
- **Story**: setting (the last three days of every month), cast (the finance manager as hero, the app as a tireless assistant), arc (from chasing receipts at night to closing by lunch), language (calm, precise, "close by lunch").

## Workflow

1. Confirm the target segment and the need (from the segmentation skill).
2. Run a customer value analysis: attributes and benefits, importance weights, your and competitors' performance; quantify functional, psychological and monetary value versus alternatives.
3. Choose the frame of reference: list the alternatives the target actually considers, pick the frame that creates the most advantageous comparison, and note any secondary frames.
4. List category, correlational and competitive POPs; set the "good enough" bar for each.
5. Brainstorm candidate PODs and filter with desirable, deliverable, differentiating; keep two or three with reasons to believe.
6. If facing several frames, decide between a combined positioning, a prioritized frame, or a straddle; check credibility.
7. Pick the advantage strategy (existing attribute, new attribute, strong brand) and plan how to keep renewing it.
8. Write the positioning statement and full value proposition with the template; plan how to signal category membership and how to resolve conflicting benefits.
9. Run the brand substitution test on the statement and key executions; check it fits about 80 percent of the product line and balances present and aspiration.
10. Turn it into a brand story and brief for communications; for start-ups, apply the small-brand guidelines.

## Red flags

- A positioning that would work equally well for a competitor (fails the substitution test).
- Points of difference no customer asked for, or claims with no reason to believe.
- Claiming difference on a dimension where the brand is not even at parity, or ignoring a competitor's POD that the brand must neutralize.
- A long list of "differentiators" instead of two or three.
- Lowest-common-denominator positioning that tries to appeal to every segment and frame.
- A straddle claim that is not credible in either category.
- Positioning based only on today's product with no room to grow, or so aspirational that it cannot be delivered.
- Announcing superiority before customers understand what category the product belongs to.
- Features listed as the message rather than benefits supported by features.
- Differentiating on a new attribute with no plan for when rivals copy it.
- Fabricated or unverifiable claims ("best", "number one", "clinically proven") or comparisons with competitors that are not valid; positioning must be truthful and substantiated.
