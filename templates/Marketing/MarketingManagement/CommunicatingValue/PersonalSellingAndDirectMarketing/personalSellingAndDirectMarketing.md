# Personal Selling and Direct Marketing

Personal selling is direct interaction with one or more prospective buyers to present, answer questions and win orders; it is the most effective tool in the late stages of buying, when preference, conviction and action are at stake. Direct marketing reaches and serves customers through consumer-direct channels without intermediaries. Run selling as a disciplined process from qualified lead to after-sale service, design and manage the sales force deliberately (objectives, strategy, structure, size, pay, recruiting, training, motivation, evaluation), and use direct channels for targeted, testable, measurable offers that respect customers' privacy and consent.

## Personal selling

Three qualities:

- **Customized**: the message can be designed for any individual.
- **Relationship oriented**: from matter-of-fact selling to deep personal relationships.
- **Response oriented**: the buyer is given choices and encouraged to respond directly.

Formats: field and retail selling; one-to-many party plans (a host invites friends, the seller demonstrates and takes orders); **multilevel (network) marketing**, where independent distributors earn on their own sales and on the sales of people they recruit. The legal line: a legitimate multilevel business earns from sales to real end customers, not mainly from recruiting; firms that limit how many downline levels earn commission keep the focus on customer sales. Do not market a 'business opportunity' whose income depends on recruiting, and do not overstate earnings.

Training turns passive order-takers into active order-getters.

## The selling process

Six steps:

1. Prospecting and qualifying.
2. Pre-approach.
3. Presentation and demonstration.
4. Persuasion.
5. Closing.
6. Servicing.

### 1. Prospecting and qualifying

- Companies increasingly generate and qualify leads centrally so reps spend expensive time selling.
- **BANT**: does the prospect have the **Budget**, the **Authority** to buy, a compelling **Need**, and a **Timeline** that fits what you can deliver?
- Go beyond BANT with data and AI signals (hiring patterns, job postings, social posts by customers and employees) to rank prospects worth a call.
- Balance quantity and quality: too many leads swamp reps and good ones slip through; too few or poor ones demoralize them.
- Know that buyers qualify you too: professional buyers visit suppliers, meet staff and build shortlists, and push suppliers to adapt proposals to make the cut.

### 2. Pre-approach

- Learn about the company (needs, who is in the buying decision) and the buyers (personal traits, buying styles).
- Understand the purchasing process: 'who, when, where, how and why'. Purchasing may be centralized and strategic (favoring large suppliers that meet all needs) or decentralized for small items.
- Set call objectives: qualify, gather information, or make an immediate sale.
- Choose the contact approach (visit, call, email, letter): access to time-starved, well-informed buyers is harder than ever.
- Plan an overall strategy for the account.

### 3. Presentation and demonstration: FABV

| Element | Meaning | B2B SaaS example |
|---|---|---|
| **Features** | Physical or technical characteristics | Automatic invoice matching with OCR |
| **Advantages** | Why the features give the customer an edge | No manual keying; matches in seconds |
| **Benefits** | Economic, technical, service and social payback | Finance team closes the month three days sooner; fewer late-payment penalties |
| **Value** | Worth, often in money | Saves about $80,000 a year in staff time and penalties for a 50-person finance team |

Reps tend to over-talk features (product orientation) and under-sell benefits and value (customer orientation), especially in competitive markets or for customized and premium-priced offerings. Every pitch must be relevant, engaging and compelling; a competitor is always waiting.

### 4. Persuasion: objections and negotiation

Selling is interactive; buyers question and object.

- **Psychological resistance**:
  - Resistance to interference.
  - Preference for established suppliers or brands.
  - Apathy; reluctance to give something up.
  - Unpleasant associations with the rep.
  - Predetermined ideas; dislike of making decisions; anxiety about money.
- **Logical resistance**: price, delivery schedule, product or company characteristics.

Techniques:

- Keep a positive approach.
- Ask the buyer to clarify the objection.
- Ask questions that lead buyers to answer their own objection.
- Deny an objection that is factually invalid, with evidence.
- Turn it into a reason for buying.

Negotiated issues besides price: completion time, quality, volume, safety, and responsibility for financing, risk, promotion and title. Reps often give discounts too easily. Train them to **sell the price, not sell through price**: give them customer history and value-adding options instead of price cuts (Kotler cites a firm whose revenue grew while profit stayed flat until it retrained reps this way).

### 5. Closing

- Watch for closing signals: physical actions, statements or comments, questions.
- Closing methods:
  - Ask for the order.
  - Recapitulate points of agreement.
  - Offer to help write up the order.
  - Ask whether the buyer wants A or B.
  - Get minor choices settled (color, size).
  - Point out what the buyer loses by not ordering now.
- Inducements: extra service, extra quantity, a token gift.
- If stuck, you may be talking to the wrong person; reach someone with authority, or reinforce how the offering relieves the client's financial or other pressures.
- Close in a way that builds the long-term relationship. Use 'what you lose by waiting' only when it is true (a real price change or capacity limit), never as manufactured pressure.

### 6. Servicing

- Immediately confirm delivery time, terms and other details that matter to the customer.
- Schedule a follow-up after delivery for installation, instruction and service.
- Detect problems early, suggest solutions, reassure, and reaffirm the buyer's choice.
- Plan how to maintain and grow the account; service beyond the sale builds trust.

## Managing the sale as a relationship

Many customers want long-term suppliers that deliver coordinated products and services to many locations, solve problems fast anywhere, and work with their teams to improve products and processes.

- Contact key customers at times other than when an order is due, with useful business suggestions.
- Monitor accounts, know their problems, adapt to their situations.
- **SPIN questioning**:
  - **Situation**: facts about the buyer's current state.
  - **Problem**: difficulties and dissatisfactions.
  - **Implication**: consequences of the problems.
  - **Need-payoff**: the value of solving them, in the buyer's own words.

Discovery-call template:

| Stage | Example questions (rewrite for the product) |
|---|---|
| Situation | How do you handle X today? Who is involved? What tools do you use? |
| Problem | Where does that process break down? What frustrates your team most? |
| Implication | What does that cost in time, money, risk or customers? Who else feels it? |
| Need-payoff | If that were solved, what would it be worth? What would it let you do? |
| BANT check | Is there budget for this? Who signs off? What is driving the timeline? |

## Designing the sales force

Selling is often the biggest promotional spend and an expensive asset; design five things: **objectives, strategy, structure, size, compensation**.

### Objectives and tasks

Reps today diagnose problems and propose solutions that improve customer profitability; the best bring fresh insights and surface needs customers had not articulated. Tasks:

- **Information gathering**: research and intelligence.
- **Targeting**: allocating time between prospects and customers.
- **Communicating**: information about products and services.
- **Selling**: approaching, presenting, answering, handling objections, closing.
- **Servicing**: consulting, technical help, financing, expediting.
- **Allocating**: deciding who gets scarce product in shortages.

Marketing and sales often blame each other (not enough leads vs not converting them). Improve collaboration: joint meetings, rotations between the functions, sometimes one leader for both.

### Strategy: direct vs contractual sales force

- **Direct (company) sales force**: employees working only for you. **Inside** salespeople sell from the office; **field** salespeople travel.
- **Contractual sales force**: manufacturers' reps, agents and brokers paid commission.
- Choosing:
  1. Estimate sales each option would generate. Company reps focus on your products, are better trained and customers like dealing direct; agents may have more people, contacts, independence and market knowledge.
  2. Estimate the cost of selling each volume level. Agents are cheaper at first, but commission costs rise faster.
  3. Find the break-even sales level: below it, agents are cheaper; above it, a company force wins. This is why small firms and low-volume territories use agents.
- Agents pose control problems: they favor customers who buy the most overall, not necessarily your products, and may not master technical details or promotional materials.

### Structure

- **Territorial**: one product line, one industry, customers in many places.
- **Product, service or customer-need** structures: many products, diverse needs.
- Complex combinations of four types:
  - **Strategic market** force for major accounts.
  - **Geographic** force for territories.
  - **Distributor** force calling on and coaching distributors.
  - **Inside** force selling and taking orders online and by phone.
- **Leveraged sales force**: reps sell complex, customized products to large accounts and are rewarded for key-account growth; inside sales and online ordering handle low-end selling; lead generation, proposals, fulfillment and post-sale support go to others.
- Reps act as **account managers** orchestrating contact between buying and selling organizations, supported by top management, technical people, customer-service reps and office staff (analysts, expediters, assistants).

### Size: the workload approach

1. Group customers into size classes by annual sales volume.
2. Set desirable call frequencies per class.
3. Multiply accounts in each class by call frequency to get the total annual workload in calls.
4. Determine how many calls an average rep can make per year.
5. Divide total calls by calls per rep to get the number of reps.

Example: 400 'A' accounts x 24 calls + 1,500 'B' accounts x 6 calls = 18,600 calls; at 800 calls per rep per year, about 23 reps.

### Compensation

- Reps want regular income, extra reward for above-average performance, and fair pay for experience and longevity. Management wants control, economy and simplicity. These conflict.
- Four components:
  - **Fixed** (salary): stability.
  - **Variable** (commissions, quotas, bonus, profit sharing): stimulates effort.
  - **Expense allowances**: travel and entertainment.
  - **Benefits**: security and satisfaction.
- **Fixed pay fits** jobs with a high ratio of non-selling to selling duties, and technically complex, team-based selling. **Variable pay fits** cyclical sales or those driven by individual initiative.

| Plan | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| Straight salary | Secure income; reps do non-selling tasks; less incentive to overstock customers; simple; low turnover | Weaker push for volume |
| Straight commission | Attracts high performers; motivation; less supervision; selling cost tied to sales | Favors the sale over the relationship |
| Combination | Benefits of both, limits on each's downside | More complex |

- Trend: tie the variable part to gross profitability, customer satisfaction and retention rather than volume, and partly to team or company performance.

## Managing the sales force

Five activities: **recruiting and selecting, training, supervising, motivating, evaluating**.

### Recruiting and selecting

- Bad hires are costly; average sales turnover is high, and turnover means lost sales, replacement and training costs, and strain on remaining reps.
- Background, personality and lifestyle variables predict performance weakly. Better predictors: **composite tests and assessment centers** that simulate the real job. Combine tests with references, history and interviews.
- Look for analytical marketing skills too (analyzing sales data, measuring potential, gathering intelligence, planning); reps who understand marketing perform better long term.

### Training and supervising

- Customers expect deep product knowledge, ideas to improve their operations, efficiency and reliability.
- Training length varies with task complexity and recruit type (longest for industrial products, shortest for consumer products); methods include programmed and distance learning, video, role-play and empathy training.
- Commission-paid reps get less supervision; salaried reps covering defined accounts get more. In multilevel selling, independent distributors manage their own recruits.

### Productivity

- Reps drift toward small, familiar accounts and current customers. Set call norms by account value and specify **prospecting time** (for example a fixed share of time on new accounts and a stop rule after a number of unsuccessful calls); some firms use a **missionary sales force** to open new accounts.
- Use time-and-duty analysis of the day (planning, travel, waiting, selling, admin) to free selling time.
- **Inside selling** costs a fraction of a field visit per contact; virtual meetings and social platforms enable selling with few face-to-face meetings, often from home. Inside reps handle inventory checks, order follow-up and small accounts, freeing field reps for major accounts and big contracts.
- Digital tools: client backgrounds, prewritten letters, order transmission, on-the-spot service resolution, digital samples and brochures. The company website introduces self-identified prospects and can take first orders.
- **Social selling**: monitor social media for front-end prospecting and lead qualification and for back-end relationship management (a rep who spotted a public complaint about a competitor's product closed a deal within hours).

### Motivating

- Higher motivation → more effort → better performance → rewards → satisfaction → more motivation.
- Most valued rewards: pay, then promotion, personal growth and sense of accomplishment; least valued: liking and respect, security, recognition.
- Tailor by rep type:
  - **Stars**: no commission caps, overachievement commissions, contests with many winners.
  - **Core performers**: multitier targets as stepping stones; varied contest prizes.
  - **Laggards**: consistent quarterly bonuses and social pressure.
- **Quotas** (on dollar sales, units, margin, activity or product type), set from the sales forecast and usually summing above it.
  - Too low overpays reps; too high frustrates them and drives them out.
  - Quotas can push volume at the cost of service and long-term satisfaction, and new-product launches may need more reps because established-product quotas suffer. Some firms drop quotas.

### Evaluating

- Good feed-forward needs good feedback. Sources:
  - Sales reports.
  - Personal observation.
  - Self-reports.
  - Customer letters, complaints and surveys.
  - Other reps.
- **Activity plans**: weekly or monthly work plans ('plan your work and work your plan'); an annual **territory-marketing plan** for new and existing accounts, used to set quotas.
- **Activity results**: call reports, expense reports, new-business and lost-business reports, local conditions reports.
- Key indicators:
  - Calls per rep per day; average call time.
  - Revenue per call; cost per call; entertainment cost per call.
  - Orders per hundred calls.
  - New customers and lost customers per period.
  - Sales force cost as a % of sales.
- High sales do not mean customers rate the rep well (weak competitors, a better product, or constant replacement of lost customers can mask it); survey customers. Analyze call outcomes by internal factors (effort, ability, strategy) and external ones (task, luck).

## Major account management

- Major (key, national, global, house) accounts: important customers with many divisions and locations that use uniform pricing and coordinated service. A major account manager typically handles a handful of accounts and supervises field reps calling on the customer's sites; large accounts get cross-functional strategic account teams (product development, technical support, supply chain, marketing, communication), sometimes stationed at the customer.
- Growing because buyers consolidate through mergers, centralize purchasing and involve more people in complex purchases; a lone rep lacks the skill, authority or coverage.
- Select accounts that:
  - Buy high volume, especially of profitable products.
  - Buy centrally.
  - Need high service across locations.
  - May be price sensitive.
  - Want a long-term partnership.
- The major account manager:
  - Is the single point of contact.
  - Grows the business and understands decision processes.
  - Finds added-value opportunities and provides competitive intelligence.
  - Negotiates and orchestrates service.
- Major accounts often value added value over price: single contact, single billing, special warranties, electronic data links, priority shipping, early information, customization, efficient maintenance and upgrades, and goodwill from people invested in their success.

## Direct marketing

Direct marketing uses consumer-direct channels to reach and deliver to customers without intermediaries, usually seeking a measurable response (an order) through **direct-order marketing**.

Advantages:

- Often more cost-effective than intermediated marketing.
- Rich information on needs, usage and what customers value or want improved.
- A better service experience and a stronger brand.

Channels: direct mail, catalogs, telemarketing, kiosks, websites, mobile.

### Direct mail and email

- Offers, announcements, reminders: 'salespeople with wings'. Selective, personal, flexible, testable; higher cost per thousand than mass media but much better prospects. Clutter is its weakness.
- Uses: leads, relationships, education, reminders, reinforcing recent purchases.
- **RFM scoring**: points for **recency** (time since last purchase), **frequency** (number of purchases) and **monetary** amount; the more points, the more attractive the customer for an offer.
- **Lists**: best prospects are past buyers. Bought lists suffer duplication, incomplete data and obsolete addresses; better lists add demographic and psychographic overlays. Test a sample before buying more; build your own lists by collecting responses to offers.
- **Testing**: test product, features, copy platform, mailer type, envelope, price and list under real conditions; one image change can move sales substantially.
- Response rates understate impact: measure effects on awareness, purchase intention and word of mouth too.
- **Email**: a fraction of the cost of mail; converts better than social ads; must be timely, targeted and relevant (many personalized variants by click, browsing and purchase history).
- **Privacy**: many consumers refuse to share data even for better offers. Ask whether and when they want messages (opt-in reminder preferences), honor unsubscribes, and follow consent and anti-spam law.

### Catalogs

- Full-line, specialty consumer and business catalogs, print and online; combining print with a searchable online version works well.
- Success factors:
  - Careful list management (no duplicates, no bad debts).
  - Inventory control.
  - Quality merchandise for low returns.
  - A distinctive image.
- Extras: editorial features, swatches, hotlines, gifts for best customers, cause donations; online catalogs reach global buyers and cut print and mail costs.

### Telemarketing

- Phone and call centers to attract prospects, sell to customers and serve them: **inbound** (customers call) and **outbound** (company calls).
- Consumer telemarketing has lost effectiveness; B2B telemarketing and video calls are growing as substitutes for expensive field visits.
- **Robo-calls** (autodialed prerecorded messages): in the US legal for relevant information (appointment reminders, flight changes, fraud alerts) and political campaigns, but not for selling products and services. Check do-not-call and consent rules in each market.

### Infomercials and home shopping

30- and 60-minute programs combining commercial selling power with information and entertainment, for complex products that need explanation; shopping channels sell for fast delivery.

### Why direct marketing keeps growing

- Customers: convenience (always-open sites, toll-free numbers, next-day delivery, service commitment) and access to specialty items stores dropped.
- Sellers:
  - Lists for almost any niche; personalization and continuous relationships timed to life stages.
  - Reach prospects when they want a solicitation.
  - Test media and messages for cost-effectiveness.
  - Keep offers less visible to competitors.
  - Measure which campaigns are most profitable.
- Integrate direct marketing with other communications and channels (cross-promote stores, catalogs and sites). Treat each interaction as a chance to up-sell, cross-sell or deepen the relationship; know each valuable customer well enough to personalize and plan **lifetime marketing** around life events and transitions.
- Kotler's examples of direct sellers facing regulators over switching customers onto worse plans and misrepresenting savings show the risk: make offers clear, honor what was promised, and make it easy to reach the company and change plans.

## Worked example: B2B SaaS sales design

A workflow-automation SaaS selling to mid-market firms (500 accounts) and enterprises (60 accounts).

- **Structure**: leveraged. Inside reps handle mid-market via video; field account executives own enterprise; one strategic account team for the 8 largest customers; marketing runs lead generation and qualification.
- **Qualification**: BANT plus signals (hiring operations analysts, posting about manual processes).
- **Discovery**: SPIN script; presentation in FABV order with a value calculator.
- **Objections**: price → sell the price with a payback model and phased rollout, not a discount; 'we already use spreadsheets' (preference for established ways) → pilot on one process.
- **Size**: enterprise 60 x 20 interactions + mid-market 500 x 6 = 4,200 interactions; field AE capacity 400/year for enterprise (3 AEs), inside rep capacity 1,200/year for mid-market (3 reps).
- **Pay**: 60/40 salary/variable for AEs, variable tied to gross margin and 12-month retention, not bookings alone; team bonus for net revenue retention.
- **Evaluation**: win rate per 100 qualified opportunities, cost per sale, new and lost customers, customer survey of rep helpfulness.
- **Direct marketing**: RFM-style scoring on product usage (recency of login, frequency, seats) to target expansion emails; consented nurture sequences; A/B test subject lines and offers on samples before full sends.

## Workflow

1. Decide the role of personal selling vs direct and digital channels by stage of the buying process and account value.
2. Define the lead process with marketing: who generates, how leads are qualified (BANT plus signals), service levels for follow-up, and a quantity-quality balance.
3. Write the sales playbook: pre-approach research checklist, SPIN discovery guide, FABV presentation with a value case, objection-handling guide for psychological and logical resistance, honest closing options, post-sale service plan.
4. Choose direct vs contractual coverage per territory using a break-even comparison; plan controls for agents.
5. Pick the structure (territory, product, market, major accounts, inside, leveraged) and support roles.
6. Size the force with the workload method; revisit when launching new products.
7. Design compensation: fixed vs variable by task type, variable tied to profit, satisfaction and retention, adjusted for stars, core and laggards; set quotas from the forecast with care.
8. Recruit with job simulations; train on product, process and customer empathy; set prospecting time and productivity routines; equip with digital and social selling tools.
9. Evaluate with work plans, territory plans, call and result reports, the key indicators and customer feedback.
10. For direct marketing, build and clean consented lists, score with RFM, test offers on samples, personalize and time messages, measure response plus awareness, intention and word of mouth, and integrate with stores, sites and sales.

## Red flags

- Reps spend their time qualifying cold leads instead of selling; or marketing hands over hundreds of unqualified leads.
- Presentations are feature tours with no stated benefit or value.
- Discounting is the default answer to every objection; revenue grows while margin falls.
- Closing tactics based on fake deadlines, invented scarcity or pressure on minor choices to trap the buyer.
- No after-sale follow-up; account growth is left to chance.
- Commission tied only to bookings, with churn, returns or customer complaints rising.
- Quotas set so high that reps quit, or so low that they coast.
- Agents covering territories where volume is now well above break-even for a direct force.
- Sales force size set by budget or habit rather than workload.
- Hiring on personality impressions alone; no job simulation.
- Sales and marketing blaming each other with no shared lead definition.
- Multilevel compensation driven by recruiting rather than end-customer sales; income claims that are atypical.
- Bought lists mailed without testing; no RFM or segmentation; one message to everyone.
- Emails or calls to people who never opted in; prerecorded sales calls; hard-to-find unsubscribe or cancellation.
- Direct offers whose savings or terms are misrepresented, or that silently move customers to worse plans.
